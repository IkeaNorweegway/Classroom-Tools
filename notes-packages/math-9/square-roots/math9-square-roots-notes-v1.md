**The principal square root is the *positive* root.**

Both 5 and −5, when squared, equal 25. But √25 = ___ only, because the radical symbol √ means the _______________ root.

---

> **MISCONCEPTION: √9 = ±3**
>
> Wrong idea: "The square root of 9 is plus or minus 3, because both 3² and (−3)² equal 9."
>
> Why it fails: While it is true that both 3 and −3 are square roots of 9, the principal square root — written √9 — is defined as the non-negative root. The symbol √ gives exactly one answer. If a problem intends both roots, it writes ±√9.
>
> Correct: √9 = 3 (positive only). If asked for all square roots of 9: ±3.
>
> Which expression gives −3? Write it: ___

### Guided Notes

**Most numbers are NOT perfect squares.**

√3, √7, √10 are not integers. They are not fractions either. They are _______________ numbers — they cannot be written as a/b where a and b are integers.

**What does irrational mean?**
- The decimal expansion never terminates and never repeats
- √2 = 1.41421356... (goes on forever with no repeating block)
- It exists on the number line — it has a location — but cannot be expressed as a ratio

---

> **MISCONCEPTION: A non-perfect square's root is "just a messy fraction"**
>
> Wrong idea: "√2 must equal some fraction like 139/98 or something — we just haven't found it yet."
>
> Why it fails: It has been proven (since ancient Greece) that √2 cannot equal any fraction a/b. If you assume it does and simplify, you reach a contradiction. This is not a matter of precision — it is literally impossible to write √2 as a fraction, no matter how many decimal places you use.
>
> Correct: √2 is irrational. 1.41421... is just the decimal approximation, not the exact value. The only exact form is √2.

> **MISCONCEPTION: The Pythagorean theorem works for all triangles**
>
> Wrong idea: "I can use a² + b² = c² on any triangle."
>
> Why it fails: The theorem only holds for right triangles — those with exactly one 90° angle. For other triangles, a² + b² ≠ c² in general. If you are not told a triangle has a right angle, you cannot use this theorem.
>
> Test it: Does a triangle with sides 3, 4, 6 follow the theorem? Check: 3² + 4² = ___ and 6² = ___. Does it work? ___ Is this a right triangle? ___

### Guided Notes

**Square roots of fractions:**

√(a/b) = √a / √b

This works because: (√a/√b)² = (√a)²/(√b)² = a/b ✓

> √(9/16) = √9/√16 = ___/___

**Square roots of decimals — two approaches:**

**Method 1:** Convert to fraction, then take root.
√0.49 = √(49/100) = √49/√100 = ___/___  = ___

**Method 2:** Think of a decimal that squares to 0.49.
___ × ___ = 0.49, so √0.49 = ___

---

**Connecting square roots to exponents:**

Pattern from the power laws:

x^(1/2) × x^(1/2) = x^(1/2 + 1/2) = x^1 = x

But also: √x × √x = x

So x^(1/2) = ___ (the square root of x)

This means: **√x = x^(1/2)**

Verify: 9^(1/2) = ___ and √9 = ___. They match ✓

---

> **MISCONCEPTION: √(a + b) = √a + √b**
>
> Wrong idea: √(9 + 16) = √9 + √16 = 3 + 4 = 7
>
> Why it fails: Evaluate the left side: √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5. That is not 7. The radical sign applies to the entire sum *inside*, not to each term separately. Square roots do not distribute over addition.
>
> Verify: √(a + b) = √a + √b?
> Let a = 16, b = 9: Left side = ___ Right side = ___. Equal? ___
>
> What DOES work: √(a × b) = √a × √b (roots distribute over multiplication, not addition).

---

**Table — what distributes under a radical?**

| Expression | Distributes? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| √(a × b) | Yes | √(4 × 9) = √4 × √9 = ___ |
| √(a ÷ b) | Yes | √(36/4) = √36/√4 = ___ |
| √(a + b) | No | √(9 + 16) ≠ 3 + 4 |
| √(a − b) | No | √(25 − 9) ≠ 5 − 3 |
