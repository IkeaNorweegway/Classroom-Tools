## Pre-Knowledge Check

1. What do you think a **government** does?
2. Who do you think makes the laws in Canada?
3. Name one thing you think the Alberta government is in charge of, and one thing Canada's government in Ottawa is in charge of.
4. What questions do you have about how Canada's government works?

## Section 1: Designing a Country

Learning target: I can put the steps to Confederation in order and explain how each one helped create Canada's system of government.

### Vocabulary
- Deadlock
- Conference
- Resolution
- British North America Act

::: misconception
WRONG: "Confederation was decided at one big meeting in 1867."
WHY: Leaders met three times over three years: Charlottetown (September 1864), Quebec (October 1864) and London (1866–67). They argued about almost everything, and some colonies left the talks. Then Britain's Parliament had to pass the plan as a law.
RIGHT: Confederation was a **[[process]]** of several meetings and agreements, finished by the BNA Act on July 1, 1867.
:::

#### A government that could not decide

In the 1860s the Province of Canada had a problem. Canada West and Canada East had the **same number of seats** in their assembly, but they disagreed on many things, such as language and religion. The government kept falling apart. When a government cannot make decisions, it is called a **[[deadlock]]**.

In 1864 three rival leaders, John A. Macdonald, George-Étienne Cartier and George Brown, agreed to work together in a "Great Coalition." Their goal was a new, bigger system of government that could break the deadlock.

#### Three conferences

At **Charlottetown** (September 1864), the leaders agreed to talk about a larger union of the colonies.

At **Quebec** (October 1864), they wrote the plan: the **72 Resolutions**. The resolutions answered the hard questions. How would laws be made? How would each region be **represented**? Which powers would belong to the new national government, and which to the [[provinces]]?

At **London** (1866–67), leaders from the Province of Canada, Nova Scotia and New Brunswick met with the British government to finish the plan. Britain's Parliament then passed it as the **British North America Act**. It came into force on **July 1, 1867**.

::: connection
Think of a group project where people disagreed. What did your group do to reach a decision? How is that like the conferences?
:::

::: retrieval
1. What is a deadlock?
   => When a government cannot make decisions (the Province of Canada in the 1860s).
2. Put in order: Quebec Conference, BNA Act, Charlottetown Conference, London Conference.
   => Charlottetown (Sept. 1864) → Quebec (Oct. 1864) → London (1866–67) → BNA Act (July 1, 1867).
3. What was written at the Quebec Conference?
   => The 72 Resolutions, the plan for the new government.
4. Application: Why did the plan need to go to London?
   => The colonies belonged to Britain, so Britain's Parliament had to pass the plan as a law (the BNA Act).
5. ★ Stretch: Explain how each conference contributed to Canada's system of government.
   (lines 3)
   => Charlottetown opened the talks; Quebec wrote the plan (how laws are made, representation, division of powers); London finished it with Britain, which passed the BNA Act.
:::

Learning target: I can explain representative government, responsible government and representation by population.

### Vocabulary
- Representative government
- Responsible government
- Representation by population
- Riding

#### Three big ideas

The Fathers of Confederation built Canada's government on three democratic ideas. Each one answers a question about power.

**Representative government** means citizens elect people to make laws for them. Canada is divided into areas called **ridings**, and each riding elects one Member of Parliament (MP).

**Responsible government** means the government must keep the support of the elected House of Commons. If most MPs vote against it on an important bill, the government must [[resign]] or call an election. The colonies had won responsible government in the late 1840s, and the BNA Act kept it.

**Representation by population** (rep by pop) means the more people a region has, the more seats it gets in the House of Commons. George Brown of Canada West had pushed for it for years, because Canada West had more people than Canada East but the same number of seats.

::: note
**Quick test:** Ontario has many more people than Prince Edward Island. Under rep by pop, which one gets more MPs? (Ontario.)
:::

Your class is electing a student council. Which of the three ideas would you use, and how?
:::

::: retrieval
1. What does representative government mean?
   => Citizens elect people (MPs) to make laws on their behalf.
2. Under responsible government, what must happen if the government loses the support of the House of Commons?
   => It must resign or call an election.
3. What is a riding?
   => An area that elects one Member of Parliament.
4. Application: In 1864 Canada West had more people than Canada East but the same number of seats. Which idea would fix this, and how?
   => Representation by population: Canada West would get more seats because it had more people.
5. ★ Stretch: Which of the three ideas protects citizens most from a government that stops listening? Explain.
   (lines 2)

## Section 3: The Three Branches

Learning target: I can describe the executive, legislative and judicial branches and the job of each.

### Vocabulary
- Executive branch
- Legislative branch
- Judicial branch
- The Crown

#### Splitting up the jobs

The BNA Act divided the work of government into three **branches**. Each branch has a different job, so no single part holds all the power.

The **executive branch** carries out laws and runs the government day to day. Its power formally belongs to **the Crown**, the monarch, represented in Canada by the **Governor General**. In practice the Governor General acts on the advice of the Prime Minister and Cabinet (officially, the Privy Council).

The **legislative branch** is **Parliament**. It debates and passes laws. Parliament has two "houses": the House of Commons and the Senate.

The **judicial branch** is the **courts**. Judges decide what laws mean and whether they have been broken.

::: connection

:::

::: retrieval
1. Name the three branches of government.
   => Executive, legislative, judicial.
2. Which branch makes laws?
   => The legislative branch (Parliament).
3. Which branch interprets laws?
   => The judicial branch (the courts).
4. Application: A judge decides whether a new law was broken. Which branch is this?
   => Judicial.
5. ★ Stretch: Why might it be dangerous for one person to make the laws, carry them out and judge them?
   (lines 2)
   => They could make unfair laws, enforce them however they liked, and no one could stop them. Splitting the jobs means each branch can check the others.
:::

## Section 4: Inside Parliament

Learning target: I can describe the House of Commons, the Senate, the Prime Minister and Cabinet, and the Governor General.

### Vocabulary
- House of Commons
- Senate
- Prime Minister
- Cabinet
- Governor General

::: misconception
WRONG: "Canadians vote directly for the Prime Minister."
WHY: Look at a federal ballot: it lists only the candidates in **your riding**. The party leader's name appears only on the ballot in the leader's own riding.
RIGHT: Canadians elect a local **MP**. The leader of the party with the most seats in the House of Commons usually becomes the **[[Prime Minister]]**.
:::

#### The House of Commons

The **House of Commons** is the lower house and the main law-making body. Its members, MPs, are elected by the citizens of their ridings. In 1867 there were 181 MPs. Today there are 343.

#### The Senate

::: misconception
WRONG: "Senators are elected, just like MPs."
WHY: No Canadian has ever voted in a national election for a senator. The BNA Act set up the Senate as an appointed body.
RIGHT: Senators are **appointed** by the Governor General on the advice of the Prime Minister. They serve until age 75.
:::

The **Senate** is the upper house. It was designed to give laws a "sober second thought": senators read every bill the House of Commons passes and can suggest changes. In 1867 the Senate had 72 seats, 24 each for Ontario, Quebec and the Maritimes, so that smaller regions had a stronger voice than rep by pop alone would give them.

#### The Prime Minister, Cabinet and Governor General

::: misconception
WRONG: "The Governor General runs the country."
WHY: The Governor General signs every law (royal assent) and opens Parliament, but by long tradition acts on the advice of the Prime Minister and Cabinet.
RIGHT: The Governor General represents the monarch. Real day-to-day power belongs to the **Prime Minister and Cabinet**.
:::

The **Prime Minister** is the head of government and the leader of the party with the most seats. The PM chooses **Cabinet ministers**, usually from among the MPs, and the Governor General appoints them. Each minister is in charge of one department, such as defence or finance.

## Section 5: Federalism: Sharing Power

Learning target: I can explain how the BNA Act divided powers between the federal and provincial governments, and give one benefit and one challenge of dividing power.

### Vocabulary
- Federalism
- Federal government
- Provincial government
- Division of powers

::: misconception
WRONG: "The federal government is the boss of the provinces in everything."
WHY: The BNA Act gave some powers only to the provinces. Ottawa cannot run Alberta's schools, and Alberta cannot print money.
RIGHT: Under **federalism**, each level has its **own** powers. The BNA Act listed federal powers in section 91 and provincial powers in section 92.
:::

#### Who does what?

The Fathers of Confederation wanted a strong central government, but French-speaking Canada East and the Maritime colonies wanted to keep control of things that mattered to their own communities. The answer was **federalism**: power divided between a **federal** government in Ottawa and **provincial** governments.

| Federal (Ottawa) | Provincial (e.g. Alberta) |
|---|---|
| Defence (army, navy) | Education |
| Money and banking | Hospitals and health |
| Trade and commerce | Property and civil rights |
| Criminal law | Cities and towns (municipalities) |
| Citizenship | Natural resources |

## Section 6: The Courts

Learning target: I can explain why a country needs a national court system and a top court of appeal.

### Vocabulary
- Court of appeal
- Supreme Court of Canada
- Judicial Committee of the Privy Council

#### One law, one top court

The BNA Act set up the **judicial branch** and allowed Parliament to create a "general court of appeal for Canada." In 1875 Parliament created the **Supreme Court of Canada**.

Why does that matter? Imagine judges in Alberta and judges in Nova Scotia reading the same federal law differently. A person could be guilty in one province and innocent in the other. A top court of appeal hears cases from every province and gives the **final** answer, so the law means the same thing everywhere.

For many years, Canada's top court still did not have the very last word. Until 1949, some cases could be appealed to the **Judicial Committee of the Privy Council**, a court in London, England. Moving that last step to Ottawa is one example of how Canada gained control of its own affairs gradually.

::: connection
Two referees at two different games call the same play two different ways. How is a top court like having one head referee for the whole league?
:::

::: retrieval
1. When was the Supreme Court of Canada created?
   => 1875.
2. What does a court of appeal do?
   => It checks whether the law was applied correctly in a lower court's decision.
3. Until 1949, where could some Canadian cases still be appealed?
   => The Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London.
4. Application: Explain how one top court keeps the law the same across the country.
   (lines 2)
   => It hears cases from every province and its decision applies everywhere, so different courts can't keep reading the same law differently.
5. ★ Stretch: How does the story of the Supreme Court show that "Canadian sovereignty was developed gradually"?
   (lines 3)
   => The BNA Act only allowed a top court; it was created in 1875; but final appeals still went to London until 1949. Control came in steps, not all at once in 1867.
:::
