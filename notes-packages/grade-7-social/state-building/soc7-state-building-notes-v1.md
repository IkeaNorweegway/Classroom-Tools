## Pre-Knowledge Check

1. What do you think the word **"Confederation"** means?
2. In what year do you think Alberta became a province? Why do you think so?
3. Who do you think lived on the Prairies before European settlers arrived?
4. What questions do you have about how Canada became a country?

## Section 1: British North America Before 1867

Learning target: I can describe the colonies and territories that made up British North America before Confederation, and explain how Britain came to control them.

### Vocabulary
- Colony
- British North America
- New France
- Acadia
- Rupert's Land

#### Two empires, one continent

For about 150 years, **Britain** and **France** competed for land and trade in North America. France built a colony called **New France** along the St. Lawrence River, and another called **Acadia** on the Atlantic coast. Britain had colonies to the south and the north.

The two empires fought many wars. Each war moved the **boundaries**, the lines that show who controls an area. In 1713 Britain took over most of Acadia and renamed it [[Nova Scotia]]. In 1759 British soldiers won the Battle of the Plains of Abraham at Quebec, and in 1763 France gave New France to [[Britain]].

By the 1860s, all of these lands were part of **British North America**: a group of separate colonies and territories, each ruled from Britain. They did not form one country.

The Province of Canada had two parts: **Canada West** (mostly English-speaking, today's Ontario) and **Canada East** (mostly French-speaking, today's [[Quebec]]). The Atlantic colonies were New Brunswick, Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island and Newfoundland. **British Columbia** was on the Pacific coast.

In between lay **Rupert's Land**, a huge area that drains into Hudson Bay. King Charles II had given it to the [[Hudson's Bay Company]] in 1670 so the company could trade furs. Farther north was the **North-Western Territory**.

## Section 2: Why the Colonies Joined Together

Learning target: I can explain why leaders wanted Confederation and how the Fathers of Confederation created the Dominion of Canada in 1867.

### Vocabulary
- Confederation
- Fathers of Confederation
- Expansion
- Dominion
- British North America Act

::: misconception
WRONG: "In 1867 Canada became an independent country, separate from Britain."
WHY: After 1867 Britain still made Canada's decisions about wars and treaties with other countries. The British monarch was still Canada's head of state, and the British Parliament still had to pass any big change to Canada's laws. In 1914, when Britain went to war, Canada was at war too, without a vote of its own.
RIGHT: In 1867 Canada became a self-governing **[[Dominion]]**: it ran its own affairs at home, but it was still part of the British Empire. Full control of its own foreign policy came in 1931.
:::

#### A big neighbour

In the 1860s many people in British North America were worried about the **United States**. From 1861 to 1865 the U.S. fought the **American Civil War**, and at the end it had one of the largest armies in the world. Some Americans believed their country should spread across the whole continent. In 1866 armed Irish-American groups called the Fenians raided across the border.

Leaders in the colonies feared the U.S. might take over British North America one piece at a time. Separate colonies were [[weak]] on their own. Joined together, they could defend themselves, build a railway and trade more easily. This plan to unite the colonies was called **Confederation**.

#### The Fathers of Confederation

The leaders who planned Confederation are called the **Fathers of Confederation**. They came mainly from the Province of Canada, New Brunswick and Nova Scotia. Prince Edward Island and Newfoundland sent leaders to the early meetings but decided to wait. Their goal was one [[united]] country with more control over its land, its government and its defence. Two of the best-known were John A. Macdonald from Canada West and George-Étienne Cartier from Canada East.

On **July 1, 1867**, a British law called the **British North America Act** (the BNA Act) created the **Dominion of Canada**. It had four provinces: Ontario, Quebec, Nova Scotia and [[New Brunswick]].

::: note
**Remember the order:** worry about the U.S. → three conferences → the BNA Act → the Dominion of Canada, with four provinces.
:::

::: connection
Have you ever joined a team or group because it was stronger than being on your own? How is that like the colonies joining in 1867? How is it different?
:::

::: retrieval
1. Name one event that made leaders worry about the United States.
   => The American Civil War (1861–65) and its huge army, U.S. talk of spreading across the continent, or the Fenian raids (1866).
2. Put these in order: London Conference, BNA Act, Charlottetown Conference, Quebec Conference.
   => Charlottetown (1864) → Quebec (1864) → London (1866–67) → BNA Act (1867).
3. Which four provinces made up Canada on July 1, 1867?
   => Ontario, Quebec, Nova Scotia, New Brunswick.
4. Application: Was Canada fully independent in 1867? Explain.
   => No. It was a self-governing Dominion inside the British Empire; Britain still controlled its foreign affairs.
5. ★ Stretch: Justify this claim with evidence: "Fear of the United States was one of the strongest reasons for Confederation."
   (lines 3)
   => Strong answers link the Civil War army, expansion talk and the Fenian raids to the colonies' weakness alone and the plan to unite for defence.
:::

## Section 3: Expanding West

Learning target: I can explain how and why Canada took over Rupert's Land and the North-Western Territory, and what it gained.

### Vocabulary
- Expansion
- Territory
- Sovereignty
- Hudson's Bay Company

::: misconception
WRONG: "The West was empty land just waiting for settlers."
WHY: First Nations such as the Cree, Blackfoot (Siksikaitsitapi), Dene, Saulteaux and Nakota had lived, hunted, traded and governed across the West for thousands of years. Métis communities had grown up along the rivers, especially at Red River. Calling the land "empty" erases all of them.
RIGHT: The West was already **home to many peoples**. When Canada took over the region, it took control of land where people already lived, which is why expansion led to conflict.
:::

#### A deal with the Hudson's Bay Company

Right after Confederation, Canada's leaders wanted to grow the country west to the Pacific. In 1869 Canada agreed to take over **Rupert's Land** and the **North-Western Territory** from the Hudson's Bay Company. Britain helped arrange the deal. The company was paid [[£300,000]] (about $1.5 million) and kept land around its trading posts.

::: misconception
WRONG: "Canada bought Rupert's Land from the people who lived there."
WHY: The deal was between Canada, Britain and the Hudson's Bay Company. No First Nations or Métis leaders were part of it, and no one asked the people who lived there.
RIGHT: Rupert's Land was **transferred from the Hudson's Bay Company**. The people living on the land were not consulted.
:::

#### Why did Canada want the West?

Canada's government saw the West as the key to a strong country:

- **Land** for farms and new settlers
- **Resources** such as furs, timber and good soil
- **Protection** against the United States taking the region first
- A path to **British Columbia**, which joined Canada in 1871 when Canada promised a railway to the Pacific

Taking over the West made Canada one of the [[largest]] countries in the world. It also meant the government now claimed authority over thousands of First Nations and Métis people who had never agreed to it.

::: connection
Think of a place you care about, like your home or neighbourhood. How would you feel if two groups made a deal about it without asking you?
:::

3. Who was not consulted in the deal?

## Section 4: The Red River Conflict and the Northwest Resistance

Learning target: I can describe the Red River Conflict and the Northwest Resistance from more than one point of view, and explain how the government responded.

### Vocabulary
- Métis
- Provisional government
- Resistance
- Scrip

#### Red River, 1869

The **Métis** are a distinct people with First Nations and European ancestry, their own language (Michif), culture and communities. By 1869 about 12,000 people lived at **Red River** (near today's Winnipeg), and most of them were Métis. They farmed long, narrow river lots and hunted buffalo.

In 1869, before the land transfer was even finished, Canada sent surveyors to Red River. The surveyors started marking out square lots that cut across the Métis river lots. No one had asked the people of Red River about joining Canada.

A young Métis leader named **Louis Riel** led the response. The Métis stopped the surveyors and took over Upper Fort Garry. In December 1869 they formed a [[provisional government]], a temporary government to run Red River and bargain with Canada.

The provisional government sent a list of rights to Ottawa: land rights, French and English languages, and Catholic and Protestant schools. In March 1870 it executed Thomas Scott, a settler from Ontario who had fought against it. The execution made many people in Ontario furious at Riel.

#### The government's response

In 1870 Parliament passed the **Manitoba Act**. Manitoba became Canada's fifth province, with French and English rights and 1.4 million acres promised to Métis children. Canada also sent soldiers to Red River, and Riel fled to the United States.

The promised land was handed out through **scrip**: a certificate that could be traded for land or money. The process was slow and confusing, and many Métis sold their scrip cheaply to land buyers or never received land. Many Métis families moved farther west, to the South Saskatchewan River.

#### The Northwest Resistance, 1885

By the 1880s the same problems had followed the Métis west. The buffalo were nearly gone, surveys again ignored their river lots, and their petitions to Ottawa got no answer. First Nations on the Prairies were facing hunger on their reserves. The Métis asked Riel to come back and lead them.

In 1885 fighting broke out at Duck Lake and Batoche. The government used the new railway to move about 5,000 soldiers west in about a week. In 1870 the same trip had taken soldiers three months. The Métis were defeated at Batoche. Riel surrendered, was tried for **treason** in Regina, was convicted and was executed in November 1885. Cree leaders Poundmaker and Big Bear were also jailed.

::: teacher
label: Perspective note
Students will meet strong views of Riel. Some people see him as a traitor who led armed rebellions; many Métis and French Canadians see him as a defender of his people's rights, and Manitoba now calls him a founder of the province. Teach both, ask for evidence, and avoid settling the question for students. "Resistance" is the current curriculum term; older sources say "Rebellion", which is worth discussing as a word choice that shows a perspective.
:::

::: connection
Older books call 1885 the "North-West Rebellion." The curriculum calls it the "Northwest Resistance." What does each word suggest about who was in the right?
:::

## Section 5: Treaties and the Indian Act

Learning target: I can explain what the numbered treaties and the Indian Act were, and how they changed control over land for First Nations.

### Vocabulary
- Treaty
- The Crown
- Reserve
- Indian Act

::: misconception
WRONG: "The treaties were simple land sales. Both sides understood them the same way."
WHY: The written treaties, in English, said First Nations would "cede, release, surrender" their land. But treaties were negotiated through interpreters, and many First Nations leaders understood them as sacred agreements to **share** the land in peace, while keeping their ways of life. Elders' accounts of what was promised often differ from the written text.
RIGHT: Treaties were **agreements between First Nations and the Crown**, and each side often understood them differently. Those differences are still debated in courts today.
:::

#### The numbered treaties

To settle the West, the government wanted the land opened for farms and the railway. Between 1871 and 1921, Canada and First Nations signed **[[11]] numbered treaties**. Most of Alberta is covered by Treaty 6 (1876), Treaty 7 (1877) and Treaty 8 (1899).

The written treaties included **reserves** (land set aside for a First Nation), yearly payments (usually $5 per person), farm tools, schools, and rights to hunt and fish. Treaty 6 also included a "medicine chest", which many First Nations understand as a promise of health care. First Nations agreed to treaties for many reasons: the buffalo were disappearing, diseases were spreading, and leaders wanted to protect their people's future.

#### The Indian Act, 1876

At Confederation, the BNA Act gave the federal government control over "Indians, and lands reserved for the Indians." In 1876 Parliament passed the **Indian Act**, a federal law that controlled many parts of First Nations life: who counted as "Indian" under the law, how reserves were run, and how bands chose leaders. Later changes banned ceremonies such as the Potlatch and the Sun Dance, and the Act was used to run residential schools.

The Indian Act still exists today, with many changes. Its **legacy** is still debated in courts and in Parliament.

## Section 6: Filling the West and New Provinces

Learning target: I can explain how the National Policy, the railway and immigration built the West, and use a timeline to show how Canada's boundaries changed.

### Vocabulary
- National Policy
- Canadian Pacific Railway
- Settlement
- Province
- Territory

::: misconception
WRONG: "All of Canada's provinces joined in 1867."
WHY: Only four did. The rest joined one at a time over more than 80 years, and the map of the North changed again as recently as 1999.
RIGHT: Canada grew **step by step**: Manitoba (1870), British Columbia (1871), Prince Edward Island (1873), Yukon (1898), Alberta and Saskatchewan (1905), Newfoundland (1949) and Nunavut (1999).
:::

#### The National Policy and the railway

Prime Minister **John A. Macdonald** had a plan called the **National Policy** (1879). It had three parts: tariffs to protect Canadian factories, a railway across the country, and settlers to farm the West. Settlers would grow wheat to sell, and buy goods made in eastern factories.

The **Canadian Pacific Railway** (CPR) was finished in 1885. It kept the promise to British Columbia and carried settlers west and wheat east. Workers from China did much of the most dangerous building in British Columbia, and hundreds died.

The government advertised free land across Europe and the United States. Newcomers arrived from many places: settlers from **Britain** and **Ukraine**, farmers from the **United States**, including Black settlers from Oklahoma, and **Francophone** families from Quebec. Each group experienced settlement differently. You will study immigration in depth in another unit.

#### Alberta joins Canada

By 1905 so many settlers had arrived on the Prairies that they needed their own governments. On September 1, 1905, **Alberta** and **Saskatchewan** became provinces, carved out of the Northwest Territories. Edmonton became Alberta's capital.
