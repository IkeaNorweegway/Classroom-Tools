## Section 1: Expansion and Regional Economies

Learning target: I can explain how expanding west helped Canada's economy grow, and why different regions built different economies.

### Vocabulary
- Economy
- Natural resource
- Region
- Agriculture
- Industry

#### Each region, its own economy

An **economy** is the way a place makes, buys and sells goods and services. After 1867 Canada's economy changed fast. The country expanded west, built new **industries** (businesses that make things), and traded more with other countries.

Different parts of Canada built different economies, because each **region** had different **natural resources**: useful things found in nature, like soil, trees, fish and minerals.

## Section 2: The National Policy

Learning target: I can explain the National Policy and weigh its short-term and long-term effects.

### Vocabulary
- Tariff
- Import
- Protect
- National Policy

#### Macdonald's plan

In 1879 Prime Minister **Sir John A. Macdonald** brought in the **National Policy**. It had three parts that worked together:

1. **Tariffs**: taxes on [[imported]] goods, so Canadian-made goods would be cheaper by comparison and people would buy Canadian.
2. **A railway** across the country to connect the regions.
3. **Settlement** of the West, so farmers would grow wheat and buy goods from eastern factories.

::: misconception
WRONG: "The National Policy helped every region of Canada equally."
WHY: Tariffs made imported goods more expensive. Prairie farmers had to buy machinery, tools and clothes at higher prices, mostly from protected factories in Ontario and Quebec, while selling their wheat at world prices they could not control. Many farmers felt the plan was built for the East.
RIGHT: The National Policy **helped some regions more than others**. Factories in Central Canada gained most; many Prairie farmers and Maritimers paid [[higher]] prices.
:::

#### Short term and long term

| | Short term (1880s–1900s) | Long term (1900s and after) |
|---|---|---|
| Gains | New factory jobs in Ontario and Quebec; the CPR finished in 1885 | A country linked east to west; a Prairie wheat economy; Canadian industries that lasted |
| Costs | Higher prices for farmers and families | Western anger at policies "made in Ottawa for the East," which shaped Prairie politics for a century |

## Section 3: The Railway That Tied Canada Together

Learning target: I can explain how the Canadian Pacific Railway supported economic growth.

### Vocabulary
- Canadian Pacific Railway
- Transportation
- Market
- Trade

#### Connecting the regions

The **Canadian Pacific Railway** (CPR) was finished on November 7, 1885, when the "last spike" was driven at Craigellachie, British Columbia. The government helped pay for it with money and about 25 million acres of land.

The railway changed the economy in three ways:

- **Moving goods.** Wheat could travel from the Prairies to ports, and then to buyers around the world. Factory goods could travel west.
- **Moving people.** Settlers could reach the Prairies in days instead of months.
- **Connecting markets.** A **market** is a place where people buy and sell. The CPR turned the whole country into one connected market.

Towns grew wherever the railway stopped. Calgary, for example, grew quickly after the CPR arrived in 1883.

## Section 4: Tariffs or Free Trade?

Learning target: I can compare the opportunities and challenges of tariffs and free trade.

### Vocabulary
- Free trade
- Protectionism
- Consumer
- Producer

::: misconception
WRONG: "A tariff is a fee the other country pays."
WHY: A tariff is collected when goods **enter** Canada. The importer pays it, and the cost is usually passed on to Canadian buyers as a higher price.
RIGHT: Tariffs are usually paid, in the end, by **[[consumers]]** in the country that charges them, through higher prices.
:::

#### Worked example: how a tariff changes a choice

A plough made in the United States costs **$100**. A plough made in Ontario costs **$120**. Canada adds a **30% tariff** on imported ploughs.

1. Find the tariff: 30% of $100 = **$30**.
2. Add it on: $100 + $30 = **$130** for the American plough.
3. Compare: the Ontario plough ($120) is now cheaper, so more farmers buy Canadian.

**Who wins?** The Ontario factory and its workers. **Who pays?** The farmer, who pays $120 instead of $100.

#### Free trade

**Free trade** means trading with no tariffs. **Consumers** (buyers) get more choice and lower prices, and **producers** can sell to bigger markets. But some local businesses may close if they can't compete with cheaper imports.

Canadians argued about this for a long time. In the 1911 election, Prime Minister Wilfrid Laurier proposed freer trade with the United States, called reciprocity. He [[lost]] the election, partly because many voters feared it would hurt Canadian factories and tie Canada too closely to the U.S. Canada and the U.S. finally signed a free trade agreement in 1988.

| | Tariffs | Free trade |
|---|---|---|
| Opportunities | Protect new local industries and jobs; raise money for the government | Lower prices and more choice; bigger markets for producers |
| Challenges | Higher prices; other countries may charge tariffs back | Some local businesses can't compete; more dependence on other countries |

::: connection
Tariffs are back in the news today. Find one recent example. Who is charging the tariff, and who do you think pays?
:::

::: retrieval
1. What is free trade?
   => Trading between countries with no tariffs.
2. Who usually ends up paying for a tariff?
   => Consumers in the country that charges it, through higher prices.
3. Application: A $50 American jacket has a 20% tariff. What does it cost in Canada? Would a $55 Canadian jacket now sell better?
   => $50 + $10 = $60. Yes, the $55 Canadian jacket is now cheaper.
4. Give one opportunity and one challenge of free trade.
   => Opportunity: lower prices, more choice, bigger markets. Challenge: local businesses may close; more dependence on another country.
5. ★ Stretch: Was Macdonald right to choose tariffs in 1879? Use one opportunity and one challenge in your answer.
   (lines 3)
   => Accept either judgement with both sides: tariffs helped young Canadian factories grow and created jobs, but made goods more expensive, especially for Prairie farmers.
:::

## Section 5: Measuring and Sharing the Economy

Learning target: I can explain GDP and why governments give extra support to some regions.

### Vocabulary
- Gross domestic product (GDP)
- Equalization payments
- Inequality

::: misconception
WRONG: "GDP is how much money a country has saved."
WHY: GDP counts what a country **produces** in one year, not what it has in the bank. A country can have a big GDP and still owe a lot of money.
RIGHT: **Gross domestic product** is the total value of all the goods and services produced in a country in one [[year]].
:::

#### Worked example: a tiny GDP

A small town in one year grows $5,000 of wheat, builds $3,000 of furniture and gives $2,000 of haircuts.

GDP = $5,000 + $3,000 + $2,000 = **$10,000**.

If the town grows more wheat next year, its GDP goes **up**. Canada's real GDP is about $3 trillion a year.

#### Sharing between regions

Some provinces have more valuable resources or bigger industries, so they collect more tax money. That creates **inequality** between regions.

::: misconception
WRONG: "Equalization means Ottawa gives every province the same amount of money."
WHY: Provinces that can raise plenty of money on their own get nothing. Only provinces below the national average get payments, and the amounts differ.
RIGHT: **Equalization payments** come from federal taxes paid by Canadians everywhere and go to provinces that can raise **less** money on their own, so every province can offer similar services, like schools and hospitals.
:::

## Section 6: Banks and the Bank of Canada

Learning target: I can explain how banks support the economy, how GDP and banking affect each other, and what the Bank of Canada does.

### Vocabulary
- Bank
- Loan
- Invest
- Bank of Canada

#### What banks do

**Banks** help people and businesses manage money. They keep savings safe, make **loans**, and help people **invest**: put money into something that can grow. A farmer might borrow to buy land; a factory might borrow to build a new building.

Some of Canada's banks are older than Canada itself. The Bank of Montreal opened in 1817 and the Bank of Nova Scotia in 1832. The Royal Bank of Canada, the Canadian Imperial Bank of Commerce, the National Bank of Canada and the Laurentian Bank also grew from banks started in the 1800s.

When the economy produces more, people and businesses need more banking: more loans, savings and payments. And when banks lend money, businesses can produce more, so GDP grows. That's why we say GDP and banking have a **reciprocal** (two-way) relationship.

#### The Bank of Canada

::: misconception
WRONG: "The Bank of Canada is a bank where people open accounts."
WHY: You can't open a chequing account at the Bank of Canada. It is the government's central bank.
RIGHT: The **Bank of Canada**, created in the 1930s, looks after the whole money system: it issues Canada's banknotes and works to keep the banking system and prices stable.
:::

The Bank of Canada was created in 1934, during the Great Depression, when many people lost trust in money and banks. Look at any Canadian banknote: it says "Bank of Canada."

::: connection
Look at a banknote. What does it tell you about who controls Canada's money?
:::

::: retrieval
1. Name two things banks do.
   => Keep savings safe, make loans, help people invest, handle payments.
2. When was the Bank of Canada created?
   => 1934 (in the 1930s).
3. Does the Bank of Canada hold ordinary people's accounts?
   => No. It is the central bank: it issues banknotes and keeps the money system stable.
4. Application: A Prairie town's farms have a great harvest. How might this affect the local bank?
   => More money to deposit and more businesses wanting loans or payments, so the bank does more business.
