## Pre-Knowledge Check

1. Why do you think people move to a new country?
2. What does it mean to treat someone **fairly**?
3. Do you know when or why your family, or a family you know, came to Canada?
4. What questions do you have about who came to Canada in the past, and how they were treated?

## Section 1: Wanted: Settlers

Learning target: I can explain why the Canadian government wanted immigrants in the West, and describe push and pull factors.

### Vocabulary
- Immigrant
- Push factor
- Pull factor
- Discrimination

#### Filling the West

After Canada took over the West and built the railway, the government needed people to live there. Settlers would farm the land, buy goods from eastern factories and strengthen Canada's claim to the territory so the United States could not take it.

From 1896 to 1905, **Clifford Sifton** was the minister in charge of immigration. His department advertised across Britain, Europe and the United States with posters and pamphlets about "the last, best West." Between 1896 and 1914, more than [[two]] million immigrants came to Canada.

#### Push and pull

People move for reasons that **push** them away from home and reasons that **pull** them toward a new place.

## Section 2: Free Land: The Dominion Lands Act

Learning target: I can describe how the offer of land influenced immigrants' decisions to settle on the Prairies.

### Vocabulary
- Dominion Lands Act
- Homestead
- Quarter section

#### The homestead deal

In 1872 Parliament passed the **Dominion Lands Act**. It offered a **homestead**: a quarter section of land, **160 acres** (about 65 hectares), to any settler who would farm it.

To keep the land, a settler had to:

1. Pay a $[[10]] registration fee.
2. Live on the land for at least three years.
3. Build a house and break (plough) part of the land for crops.

After three years, the land belonged to the settler. For many families from crowded parts of Europe, owning 160 acres was something they could never have at home.

## Section 3: Building the Prairies

Learning target: I can describe how immigrants built farms, towns and Canada's economy.

### Vocabulary
- Settlement
- Bloc settlement
- Agriculture

#### Who came?

Settlers came from many places, and many groups settled near each other in **bloc settlements**, so they could share their language, church and customs.

- **Ukrainians** began arriving in 1891. Most settled east of Edmonton, around places like Vegreville and Mundare. They brought knowledge of farming that helped turn prairie land into productive farmland, and they cleared and farmed land many others had passed over.
- **Francophone** families from Quebec and from France and Belgium settled in communities such as Saint-Paul, Bonnyville and Legal, Alberta.
- **Americans**, many of them experienced farmers, came north once good land in the U.S. was taken. Among them were **Black families from Oklahoma** who founded Amber Valley, north of Edmonton, around 1910.
- **British** settlers came in large numbers, along with Germans, Scandinavians, Icelanders, Mennonites, Jewish families and many others.

#### What they built

Immigrants built **towns** along the railway, churches, schools and stores. Above all they grew **wheat**. By the 1920s Canada was one of the world's biggest wheat exporters, and the Prairie wheat economy was one of the main reasons Canada's economy [[grew]].

## Section 4: Keeping Some Groups Out

Learning target: I can explain why the government made laws that restricted immigration from certain groups.

### Vocabulary
- Head tax
- Exclusion
- Continuous Journey Regulation
- Order-in-Council

::: misconception
WRONG: "Canada welcomed all immigrants equally."
WHY: The same government that recruited settlers from Britain, the U.S. and Europe passed laws and rules to keep out people from China, India and other places, and tried to stop Black Americans from coming.
RIGHT: Canada **chose** which immigrants it wanted, and some choices were based on race and where people came from.
:::

::: misconception
WRONG: "Discrimination was just a few rude people."
WHY: Some discrimination was written into **government laws and rules**, applied to whole groups.
RIGHT: Discrimination in this period came from individuals **and** from [[laws]] passed by the government.
:::

#### Laws and rules that restricted immigration

**The Chinese Head Tax.** About 15,000 workers from China helped build the CPR. Once the railway was finished in 1885, the government charged every Chinese immigrant a **head tax** of $50. It was raised to $100 in 1900 and to **$500** in 1903, about two years' pay for a worker. No other group paid it. In 1923 the **Chinese Exclusion Act** stopped almost all Chinese immigration until 1947.

**The Continuous Journey Regulation (1908).** This rule said immigrants had to arrive by a non-stop trip from their home country. There were no non-stop ships from India to Canada, so the rule kept out most immigrants from [[India]], even though they were British subjects too.

**Order-in-Council P.C. 1911-1324.** When Black farmers from Oklahoma began arriving on the Prairies, many white settlers and newspapers protested. In 1911 the government approved an order proposing a **one-year ban on Black immigrants**. It was cancelled before it took effect, but officials kept discouraging Black settlers in other ways, such as strict medical checks.

#### Why did the government make these laws?

Governments at the time gave reasons such as protecting jobs and wages for white workers. Many politicians and voters also held **racist** beliefs: that some groups could never "fit in" or belonged in Canada less than others. The laws reflected what many voters wanted, which is why examining them means looking at both the government and the society that supported it.

::: teacher
label: Sensitive content
Name the harm plainly and keep the focus on the policies and the people affected, not on repeating slurs or racist cartoons in detail. If you use a primary source with racist language, frame it first ("this shows what some people believed then") and give students a way to respond. Canada apologized for the head tax in 2006 and for the Komagata Maru in 2016; both are worth mentioning as evidence that views changed.
:::

::: connection
The head tax charged one group only. Why does it matter that it was a law, not just one person's opinion?
:::

## Section 5: Conflict on the West Coast

Learning target: I can describe the causes and consequences of the Anti-Asian Riots and the Komagata Maru incident.

### Vocabulary
- Riot
- Cause
- Consequence
- Komagata Maru

#### The Anti-Asian Riots, 1907

In 1907 more immigrants were arriving from Asia, and many workers on the Pacific coast, in Canada and the United States, feared losing jobs. A group called the Asiatic Exclusion League organized a march in Vancouver in September 1907. It turned into a **riot**: a crowd attacked Chinatown and the Japanese neighbourhood, smashing windows and destroying property. The crowd demanded a complete stop to Asian immigration. Japanese residents fought back to defend their homes and shops.

**Consequences:** the government paid for some of the damage, but it also made new agreements and rules to limit Asian immigration, including the Continuous Journey Regulation in 1908.

#### The Komagata Maru, 1914

In May 1914 a ship called the **Komagata Maru** arrived in Vancouver with **376 passengers** from India, most of them Sikh. They had challenged the Continuous Journey Regulation on purpose: as British subjects, they argued, they had a right to enter.

The government would not let most passengers off the ship. For two months they waited in the harbour, short of food and water. Only about two dozen people, mostly men who already lived in Canada, were allowed to stay. In July the navy ship HMCS Rainbow [[escorted]] the Komagata Maru out of Canadian waters. When the ship reached India, British police fired on passengers, and at least 19 were killed.

## Section 6: Rights, Fairness and Evidence

Learning target: I can explain the Manitoba Schools Question and Regulation 17, tell facts from opinions in historical sources, and judge whether policies were fair.

### Vocabulary
- Minority rights
- Fact
- Opinion
- Bias

#### Language and religion in schools

Not all conflict was about who could enter Canada. Some was about the rights of **minorities** already here.

**The Manitoba Schools Question (1890).** When Manitoba joined Canada in 1870, Catholic and Protestant schools both got public money, and French was used alongside English. As English-speaking Protestant settlers arrived, the population changed. In 1890 Manitoba stopped funding Catholic schools and dropped French as an official language. Francophone Catholics said their rights, promised in 1870, had been taken away. The fight became a national crisis.

**Regulation 17 (1912).** Ontario passed a rule limiting French in schools to the first two grades. Franco-Ontarians protested for years, and the rule was not dropped until 1927.

Both disputes asked the same question: when a minority's rights are promised, can a majority take them away?

::: misconception
WRONG: "If a source is old, it must be true."
WHY: Newspapers, posters and speeches from 1907 or 1914 were written by people with their own views. A headline calling newcomers an "invasion" tells you what the writer believed, not what happened.
RIGHT: Every source needs to be checked: **facts** can be confirmed with other evidence; **opinions** show a point of view, or **bias**.
:::

#### Was it fair?

Historians judge the past with **evidence**, not just feelings. To judge a policy, ask: Who did it affect? Did it treat groups differently because of who they were? What was the result?

::: connection
Choose one policy from this unit. Using the three questions above, was it fair? Write your judgement and one piece of evidence.
:::

::: retrieval
1. What did Manitoba change about schools in 1890?
   => It stopped funding Catholic schools and dropped French as an official language.
2. What did Regulation 17 do?
   => Limited French in Ontario schools to the first two grades (1912; dropped 1927).
3. What is the difference between a fact and an opinion?
   => A fact can be checked with evidence; an opinion is what someone believes.
4. Application: A 1907 newspaper says, "Our city is being overrun." Fact or opinion? How would you check what really happened?
   (lines 2)
   => Opinion. Check population records, government reports, and other accounts, including those of Asian residents.
5. ★ Stretch: Assess whether the government's immigration policies from 1872 to 1914 were fair to different groups. Use at least two examples.
   (lines 4)
   => Strong answers contrast the homestead offer to European and American settlers with the head tax, Continuous Journey Regulation or P.C. 1911-1324, and conclude with evidence (most will judge them unfair to Asian and Black immigrants).
:::
