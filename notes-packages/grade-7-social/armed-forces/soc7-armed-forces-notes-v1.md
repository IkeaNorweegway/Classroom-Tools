## Section 1: Policing the West

Learning target: I can explain why the North-West Mounted Police was created and how it changed into today's RCMP.

### Vocabulary
- Authority
- North-West Mounted Police (NWMP)
- RCMP
- Sovereignty

#### Why a police force?

After Canada took over the West in 1870, it claimed the land but had almost no way to keep order there. American whisky traders crossed the border and sold alcohol in the West, which caused great harm in First Nations communities. In 1873 American wolf hunters and whisky traders killed more than 20 Nakoda people in the Cypress Hills Massacre.

To show that Canada's laws applied in the West, and to protect its claim from the United States, Parliament created the **North-West Mounted Police** (NWMP) in [[1873]]. In 1874 about 275 officers made the long "March West." They built **Fort Macleod** in what is now southern Alberta, and soon Fort Calgary.

The NWMP enforced Canadian law, stopped the whisky trade, were present at treaty signings and helped bring settlers. That made Canada's **authority** real: not just a claim on a map, but laws people actually followed. A country that can enforce its own laws across its territory has more **sovereignty**: the power to run its own affairs.

::: misconception
WRONG: "The RCMP has always existed, just like it is today."
WHY: The force changed its name and job several times. It began as the NWMP, a police force for the western territories only.
RIGHT: The NWMP (1873) became the Royal North-West Mounted Police (1904), then the **Royal Canadian Mounted Police** in [[1920]], a national force that today oversees national security across Canada.
:::

## Section 2: Building a Military

Learning target: I can describe the steps Canada took to develop its own military forces after Confederation.

### Vocabulary
- Militia
- Militia Act
- Royal Canadian Navy
- Independence

::: misconception
WRONG: "Canada got its own military, and its independence, all at once."
WHY: For decades after 1867, British soldiers and ships defended Canada. Canada built its own forces one step at a time over more than 50 years.
RIGHT: Canada's military, like its sovereignty, grew **[[step by step]]**.
:::

#### Step by step

**1855: the Militia Act.** The Province of Canada created a paid volunteer **militia**, part-time citizen soldiers who trained to defend their home.

**1871: the British Army leaves.** Britain withdrew most of its soldiers from Canada (it kept naval bases at Halifax and Esquimalt until 1906). Canada now had to take more responsibility for its own [[defence]].

**1904: a new Militia Act.** The commander of the militia no longer had to be a British officer. It was another step toward Canadian control.

**1910: the Royal Canadian Navy.** Until then, Britain's Royal Navy protected Canada's coasts. The Naval Service Act created Canada's own navy.

## Section 3: The Boer War

Learning target: I can discuss the significance of Canada's role in the Boer War.

### Vocabulary
- Boer War
- Volunteer
- Paardeberg

#### Canada's first war overseas

In 1899 Britain went to war in South Africa against the **Boers**, settlers of Dutch background who ran two independent republics. Britain asked Canada to help. Canadians were divided:

- Many **English Canadians** wanted to support Britain and the Empire.
- Many **French Canadians**, led by Henri Bourassa, said Canada should not fight in Britain's wars far away.

Prime Minister Wilfrid Laurier chose a compromise: Canada would send and pay for [[volunteers]] only. About 7,000 Canadians, including 12 nurses, served. About 270 died.

#### Paardeberg, 1900

In February 1900 Canadian soldiers helped win the **Battle of Paardeberg**, part of the first major British victory of the war. It was the first time Canadian troops had won fame in a battle overseas, and newspapers in Canada celebrated.

#### Why it mattered

The Boer War showed that Canada could raise and send its own soldiers overseas, and that Canadians took pride in their troops. It also showed a deep divide between English and French Canada about Canada's role in the Empire.

## Section 4: The First World War

Learning target: I can examine Canada's participation in the First World War and explain why key battles mattered to Canada.

### Vocabulary
- Canadian Expeditionary Force (CEF)
- Canadian Corps
- Vimy Ridge
- Casualty

::: misconception
WRONG: "Canada decided for itself to go to war in 1914."
WHY: Britain still controlled Canada's foreign affairs. When Britain declared war on August 4, 1914, Canada was automatically at war. Canada's Parliament could only decide **how much** to contribute.
RIGHT: In 1914 Canada was at war **because Britain was**, but Canada chose to send a large force of its own.
:::

#### A Canadian army

Canada raised the **Canadian Expeditionary Force** (CEF). Over the war, about 620,000 Canadians served, out of a population of about 8 million. About 61,000 were killed.

For the first time, Canadian soldiers fought together as **distinct Canadian units**, not mixed into British ones. By 1916 the four Canadian divisions formed the **Canadian Corps**. In 1917 a Canadian, General Arthur Currie, became its commander.

::: misconception
WRONG: "Canadians only fought at Vimy Ridge."
WHY: Vimy is the best-known battle, but Canadians fought in many battles across the whole war, from 1915 to 1918.
RIGHT: Vimy Ridge was one of **many** important battles, including Second Ypres, the Somme and Passchendaele.
:::

#### Key battles

- **Second Battle of Ypres** (Belgium, April 1915): the Germans used poison gas for the first time on the Western Front. Canadian soldiers held the line, at the cost of about 6,000 casualties.
- **Vimy Ridge** (France, April 9–12, 1917): for the first time all **[[four]]** Canadian divisions attacked together. They captured a ridge that British and French armies had failed to take. About 3,600 Canadians were killed. Many people see Vimy as a moment when Canada acted as a nation.
- **Passchendaele** (Belgium, October–November 1917): Canadians captured the village in deep mud, with about 16,000 casualties.

::: misconception
WRONG: "Beaumont-Hamel and Gallipoli were Canadian battles."
WHY: In 1915–16 Newfoundland was a separate Dominion, not part of Canada. Its own force, the Newfoundland Regiment, fought at **Gallipoli** (Turkey, 1915) and **Beaumont-Hamel** (France, July 1, 1916). At Beaumont-Hamel about 800 men went into battle; the next morning only 68 answered roll call.
RIGHT: These were battles of the **[[Newfoundland]] Regiment**. Since Newfoundland joined Canada in 1949, they are remembered as part of Canada's history, and July 1 is Memorial Day in Newfoundland and Labrador.
:::

## Section 5: Who Served, and How We Remember

Learning target: I can describe how Canada's diverse communities contributed to the war and discuss ways of honouring those who served.

### Vocabulary
- No. 2 Construction Battalion
- Royal 22e Régiment
- Remembrance Day
- Memorial

#### Many communities served

People from across Canada served, even when they were not treated equally at home.

- **Black Canadians** were often turned away from regular units. Many served in the **No. 2 Construction Battalion** (1916), about 600 men, who built roads, railways and defences in France. In 2022 Canada formally apologized for how they were treated.
- **Indigenous soldiers**: more than 4,000 First Nations men enlisted, and many Métis too, though they were not required to serve. Francis Pegahmagabow, an Anishinaabe soldier from Ontario, was one of the most decorated snipers of the war.
- **Sikh Canadian soldiers**, among them Private Buckam Singh, enlisted even though Sikhs in Canada faced the restrictions you studied in the Immigration unit.
- **Francophone soldiers** served in the **22nd Battalion**, a French-speaking unit that became the **Royal 22e Régiment**, the "Van Doos."

#### Heroic and gallant acts

The **Victoria Cross** is the highest award for bravery. Canadians earned about 70 in the First World War. Researching the story behind one medal is a way to understand what the war asked of people.

#### How we remember

- **Remembrance Day**, November 11, the day the fighting ended in 1918.
- **Indigenous Veterans Day**, November [[8]], honours First Nations, Inuit and Métis veterans.
- **War memorials**, such as the **Canadian National War Memorial** in Ottawa and the **Newfoundland National War Memorial** in St. John's, and the cenotaphs in towns across Alberta.

## Section 6: From Colony to Sovereign Nation

Learning target: I can explain how the armed forces and the Statute of Westminster helped Canada gain sovereignty.

### Vocabulary
- Sovereignty
- Foreign affairs
- Statute of Westminster

#### Sacrifice earns a voice

Canada's huge contribution to the First World War changed how Canada saw itself and how other countries saw Canada. Because of it, Canadian leaders demanded a voice:

- At the end of the war Canada **signed the peace treaty**, the Treaty of Versailles (1919), separately from Britain.
- Canada got its own seat in the new **League of Nations**.

#### The Statute of Westminster, 1931

In 1931 the British Parliament passed the **Statute of Westminster**. It said Britain's Parliament would no longer make laws for Canada unless Canada asked. Canada gained control of its own [[foreign affairs]]: the freedom to make its own decisions about alliances with other countries and whether to fight in wars.

The difference showed in 1939. When Britain declared war on Germany, Canada's own Parliament debated and declared war a week later, by its own decision. (You'll study this in Grade 9.)
