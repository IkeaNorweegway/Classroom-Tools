### Notes

Migration is rarely just one decision — it is usually the result of two forces working at once. A **push factor** is a condition in a person's home country that makes them want to _______________ — war, persecution, poverty, or the destruction of their community. A **pull factor** is a condition in the destination country that makes it _______________ — safety, jobs, land, or family already living there.

After the Second World War (1945–51), one of the most significant groups to arrive in Canada were **displaced persons** — refugees, including _______________, who had lost their homes, families, and countries during the war and could not safely return. Canada's immigration policy gradually opened to admit them, though not without resistance and restriction at the time.

The result was a Canada with changing and increasingly _______________ demographics. International migration after the war meant that Canada's _______________ reflected a more diverse, _______________ population than before — a shift that would only accelerate in later decades as immigration expanded further beyond Europe.

> **Think about it:** A "push" and a "pull" often happen for the same person at the same time. For a Holocaust survivor arriving in Canada in 1948, what might have been the push? What might have been the pull? Write one sentence for each.

## Section 3: Environmental Consequences of Urbanization

**Learning target:** I can explain the social, economic, and environmental consequences and challenges of increased urbanization.

### Notes

Growth is never free. As Canadian cities and industries expanded after the war, they created lasting environmental impacts, including:

- **Pollution** — air and water contamination from factories, vehicles, and energy production
- **Land consumption** — farmland and wilderness converted into _______________ and industrial sites
- **Land division** — large natural areas broken into smaller parcels for development
- **Wildlife migration changes** — roads, fences, and buildings disrupting the routes animals had used for generations

> **Common misconception:** "Urbanization only has economic benefits — the environmental costs came later, with modern industry."
>
> In fact, the environmental consequences of pollution, land division, and habitat disruption were already visible during the first postwar urban boom of the 1950s and 60s — they were simply less discussed and less regulated at the time than they are today.
