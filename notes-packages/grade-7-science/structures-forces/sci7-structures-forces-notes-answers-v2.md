## Section 1: Types of Structures

### Vocabulary

| Term | Model definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Solid structure | A structure made of solid material all the way through; its mass carries the load | Dam, brick, tree trunk |
| Frame structure | A structure made of connected parts (a skeleton) with open spaces between them | Bicycle, building frame, spider web |
| Shell structure | A thin, curved outer surface that carries the load through its shape | Egg, igloo, helmet |
| Combination structure | A structure that uses two or three of the types together | House, car |

### Retrieval Check — Section 1

1. A frame is a rigid skeleton of connected members with open spaces; the members carry the load. A shell is a thin curved surface; the load spreads along the surface.
2. (a) egg — shell (b) bicycle — frame (c) concrete dam — solid
3. Example: a house — wood frame (frame) + siding or brick exterior (shell) + concrete foundation (solid). Accept any combination structure with its types correctly identified.
4. Shape determines strength. The curved wall of the tube spreads the load evenly around its whole surface, and the tube resists bending; a flat sheet has almost no resistance to bending.
5. **Stretch:** A frame or shell made from shaped paper will beat flat sheets: roll the paper into tubes, or fold it into triangular beams or accordion pleats, then tape them into a span. Reason: the same material carries far more load when its shape resists bending. Accept any prediction with a shape-based reason.

## Section 2: Loads

### Vocabulary

| Term | Model definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Dead load | The permanent weight of the structure itself | The steel and concrete of a bridge |
| Live load | The changing weight of the things the structure supports | People, furniture, vehicles, snow on a roof |
| Dynamic load | A force that changes suddenly or keeps changing in size or direction | Wind gusts, earthquakes, moving traffic |

### Guided Notes

| Blank / Prompt | Expected Answer | Annotation |
|---|---|---|
| Dead load: the ___ weight of the structure itself | permanent (constant) | |

| Live load: the weight of ___ | the people and objects (contents) | |
| Acts ___ (gravity) | downward | |
| Dynamic load: ___ | changing (moving / sudden) | |
| and often ___ in direction | unpredictable (variable) | |
| Wind — direction | sideways (horizontal) | |
| Earthquake — direction | back and forth and up and down (many directions) | |
| Moving traffic — direction (2 blanks) | downward (vertical); along the direction of travel (horizontal) | |
| Dynamic loads are ___ in direction | unpredictable | |
| and ___ in magnitude | variable (changing) | |
| They can also cause ___ | resonance | |
| wind matched the bridge's ___ frequency | natural | |

**Note on snow:** the notes list "snow on a roof" as a live-load example, then under "Alberta loads to consider" call snow a "dead load that accumulates." Live load is the standard classification (it comes and goes). Mark "live load" as correct and flag the wording to students.

### Retrieval Check — Section 2

1. Dead load is the permanent weight of the structure itself (e.g., a bridge's steel). Live load is the changing weight of what the structure supports (e.g., cars, people, snow).
2. Dynamic loads change suddenly, can come from any direction, and vary in size, so they are hard to design for. They can also cause resonance, where vibrations build until the structure fails.
3. Snow load — a live load that built up on the roof beyond what it was designed to carry.
4. (a) dead (b) dynamic (accept live) (c) dynamic (d) live
5. **Stretch:** Wind and chinook gusts — sideways, pushing on the walls and causing sway. Earth tremors — ground shaking back and forth and up and down. Moving people, elevators, and nearby traffic or trains — vertical and horizontal vibration. Accept temperature swings (expansion and contraction) and impact. Each load needs a direction.

## Section 3: Internal Forces

### Vocabulary

| Term | Model definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Compression | A force that squeezes or pushes material together | A column holding up a roof |
| Tension | A force that stretches or pulls material apart | A cable on a suspension bridge |
| Shear | Forces pushing in opposite directions that make parts of a material slide past each other | Scissors cutting paper; a bolt between two plates |
| Torsion | A twisting force | Wringing out a towel |

| Rope/cable — strong in | tension | |

### Retrieval Check — Section 3

1. Compression pushes or squeezes material together; tension pulls or stretches it apart. They act in opposite directions.
2. Concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension — it cracks when stretched. Steel rebar carries the tension, so reinforced concrete handles both.
3. Cable holding up a drawbridge: tension. Column supporting a roof: compression. Bolt connecting two plates: shear.
4. Beam on two supports with the weight in the middle; C marked along the top surface at mid-span; T marked along the bottom surface at mid-span.
5. **Stretch:** Steel I-beam under torsion: steel is ductile, so it twists and deforms visibly before failing, usually at joints or welds. Concrete column under tension: concrete is brittle and weak in tension, so it cracks and breaks suddenly with little warning.

## Section 4: Stability

### Vocabulary

| Term | Model definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Centre of gravity | The single point where all of an object's weight seems to act | The middle of a ruler balanced on a finger |
| Base of support | The area on the ground enclosed by the points where a structure touches it | The area between the four legs of a chair |
| Stability | The ability of a structure to stay upright and not tip over | A wide, low sports car |
| Triangulation | Using triangles in a structure to make it rigid | Trusses in a bridge or roof |

### Guided Notes

| Blank / Prompt | Expected Answer | Annotation |
|---|---|---|
| For a uniform object, the CoG is at the ___ | centre (geometric centre) | |
| stable when its ___ of gravity | centre | |
| is ___ its base of support | above (over / within) | |
| the structure ___ | topples (tips over) | |
| Lower CoG → ___ stable | more | |
| Wider base → ___ stable | more | |
| Widen the ___ | base | |
| Lower the centre of ___ | gravity | |
| Use ___ (rigid triangular units) | triangulation (triangles) | |
| This is why ___ is the fundamental structural element | the triangle (triangulation) | |
| Buckling — ___ in a slender column | compression | |
| Toppling — CoG moves ___ base | outside the | |
| Arches redirect ___ down and outward | the load (compression forces) | |

### Retrieval Check — Section 4

1. Whether its centre of gravity stays above its base of support. If the centre of gravity moves outside the base, the structure topples.
2. A triangle cannot change shape unless the length of one of its sides changes. A square can be pushed into a rhombus without any side changing length, so it deforms easily.
3. The low body gives a lower centre of gravity. With the same wheelbase, the car has to tilt much farther before its centre of gravity moves outside the base, so it is harder to tip.
4. Any two: widen the base; lower the centre of gravity (heavy parts at the bottom); use triangulation or bracing.
5. **Stretch:** The crane risks toppling when the combined centre of gravity (crane plus load) moves past the edge of its base of support. Diagram: base of support marked; with a small load the CoG sits above the base; with an excessive load the CoG has shifted out toward the load, beyond the base edge. Counterweights and outriggers shift the CoG back and widen the base.

## Section 5: Simple Machines and Mechanical Advantage

### Vocabulary

| Term | Model definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Simple machine | A basic device that changes the size or direction of a force | Lever, pulley, ramp |
| Mechanical advantage (MA) | How many times a machine multiplies your force; load force ÷ effort force | An MA of 4 lifts 200 N with 50 N of effort |
| Effort force | The force you apply to the machine | Pushing down on a crowbar |
| Load force | The force the machine applies to the object (the weight being moved) | The weight of the rock being lifted |

### Retrieval Check — Section 5

1. Mechanical advantage is load force ÷ effort force — how many times the machine multiplies your force. An MA of 3 means the output force is three times the effort, and you must move the effort three times as far.
2. (a) inclined plane (b) lever (3rd class) (c) pulley (single fixed) (d) screw
3. MA = 200 ÷ 50 = 4
4. No. You apply one-quarter of the force, but over four times the distance. Work = force × distance, so the total work is the same (slightly more in practice because of friction). Machines do not create energy.
5. **Stretch:** MA needed = 600 ÷ 150 = 4, so four supporting rope segments. Drawing: a block and tackle with two pulleys on top and two on the bottom, four rope segments holding up the lower block.

## Section 6: Materials and Structural Design

### Vocabulary

| Term | Model definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Strength | How much force a material can take before it breaks | Steel beam |
| Hardness | How well a material resists scratching and denting | Diamond, hardened steel |
| Flexibility / ductility | The ability to bend or stretch without breaking | Copper wire, steel |
| Elasticity | The ability to return to its original shape after being bent or stretched | Rubber band, spring |
| Brittleness | Breaking suddenly with little or no bending first | Glass, concrete |

### Guided Notes

| Blank / Prompt | Expected Answer | Annotation |
|---|---|---|

| Strength — good example | steel | |
| Hardness — resists ___ or scratching | denting | |
| Flexibility — can ___ without breaking | bend | |
| Elasticity — returns to ___ shape | its original | |
| Brittleness — ___ with little deformation | breaks (shatters / fractures) | |
| Tower column under compression — required property | compressive strength | |

| Earthquake-resistant building frame — best material | steel | |
| Cutting edge of a tool — required property | hardness | |
| A triangulated ___ frame made of cardboard | truss | |

### Retrieval Check — Section 6

1. Strength is how much force a material withstands before it breaks. Hardness is how well its surface resists scratching and denting. A material can be hard but still break easily (glass).
2. Steel is flexible: it bends and absorbs the shaking without breaking. Concrete is brittle and cracks suddenly under the same sideways forces.
3. (a) tensile strength (b) compressive strength (c) flexibility / elasticity (absorbs impact)
4. **Stretch:** For: wood is locally available in Alberta, renewable, light for its strength, and flexible enough to handle wind and movement. Limits: it burns, rots if wet, and is weaker than steel or concrete for very tall buildings, so it needs fire protection, moisture protection, and engineered products (laminated timber). A reasoned judgement either way is acceptable.

## Unit Synthesis

1. **Table from memory:**

| Concept | Model explanation |
|---|---|
| Three types of structures | Solid (solid all through — dam); frame (skeleton of connected members — bicycle); shell (thin curved surface — egg) |
| Three types of loads | Dead (the structure's own weight); live (what it supports — people, snow); dynamic (changing or sudden forces — wind, earthquake) |
| Four internal forces | Compression (squeezing); tension (stretching); shear (sliding in opposite directions); torsion (twisting) |
| What determines stability | Where the centre of gravity is compared to the base of support — a low centre of gravity and wide base are most stable |
| Why triangles are strong | A triangle cannot change shape without changing the length of a side |
| Mechanical advantage | Load force ÷ effort force — how many times a machine multiplies your force |

2. **Suspension bridge:**

| Component | Structural type | Main internal force |
|---|---|---|
| Concrete deck | solid | compression (accept "compression on top, tension underneath" — it bends) |
| Vertical steel cables | frame | tension |
| Main cables | frame | tension |
| Concrete anchor blocks | solid | compression (their mass holds against the pull of the cables; accept tension where the cable attaches, with reasoning) |

3. **The ramp claim.** Partly right. A longer ramp does give more mechanical advantage, so less force is needed. But the student does not do less work: the smaller force is applied over a longer distance, and work = force × distance stays the same (a little more with friction). Energy is conserved.
4. **Return to the Pre-Knowledge Check.** Open reflection. Look for: Q1 — strength comes from shape and structure type as well as material; Q4 — a ramp reduces the force needed, not the work.
