## Section 1: Cell Theory and Plant Cells

### Vocabulary

| Term | Model definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Cell theory | The idea that all living things are made of cells, cells are the basic unit of life, and all cells come from other cells | An onion and a person are both made of cells |
| Cell wall | The rigid outer layer of a plant cell that gives support and shape | The stiff outline seen in onion skin cells |
| Cell membrane | The thin layer just inside the cell wall that controls what enters and leaves the cell | Lets water in, keeps the cell contents in |
| Vacuole | A large storage space in a plant cell that holds water and keeps the cell firm | A wilted plant has vacuoles low on water |
| Chloroplast | The green structure where photosynthesis happens | Green ovals in a leaf cell |
| Nucleus | The control centre of the cell; holds the DNA | Dark round body in an onion cell |

### Guided Notes

| Blank / Prompt | Expected Answer | Annotation |
|---|---|---|
| All living things are made of ___ | cells | |
| Cells are the basic ___ of life | unit | "unit of structure and function" is the full phrase |
| All cells come from ___ cells | pre-existing (other living) | |
| Cell wall — ___ border; rigid | thick outer (outermost) | |
| Cell wall — provides ___ and shape | support (strength, protection) | |
| Cell membrane — controls what ___ in and out | moves (passes) | |
| Central vacuole — stores ___ | water (cell sap) | |
| Chloroplasts — site of ___ | photosynthesis | |
| Nucleus — contains ___ | DNA (genetic information) | |

### Retrieval Check — Section 1

1. (1) All living things are made of cells. (2) Cells are the basic unit of life. (3) All cells come from pre-existing cells.
2. (a) cell membrane (b) nucleus (c) chloroplast
3. Any two: stores water; keeps the cell firm (turgor pressure); stores waste.
4. Plant cell — the rigid outer wall is a cell wall, the large central space is the central vacuole, and the green oval bodies are chloroplasts. Animal cells have none of these.
5. **Stretch:** Animal cells need to move, change shape, and squeeze past each other (muscle cells contract, blood cells travel); a rigid wall would prevent this. Animals get support from skeletons instead. Plants stay in one place and use cell walls for support.

## Section 2: Plant Systems and Vascular Tissue

### Vocabulary

| Term | Model definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Root system | The underground part of the plant that absorbs water and minerals, anchors the plant, and stores food | Carrot taproot; grass fibrous roots |
| Shoot system | The above-ground part of the plant: stem, leaves, flowers, fruit | The stalk, leaves, and head of a wheat plant |
| Vascular tissue | The tubes that carry water and food through a plant | Veins in a leaf; strings in celery |
| Xylem | Tubes that carry water and minerals up from the roots | Dyed water rising in a celery stalk |
| Phloem | Tubes that carry sugars from the leaves to the rest of the plant | Maple sap carrying sugar |

| Phloem — from ___ to ___ | leaves; the rest of the plant (roots, fruit, growing tips) | Phloem moves sugar to wherever it is needed; "down" is a simplification |

| Diagram labels | flower, leaves, stem, roots labelled; xylem arrow (↑) from roots up the stem to the leaves; phloem arrow (↓) from leaves down the stem to the roots | |

### Retrieval Check — Section 2

1. Root (any two): absorbs water and minerals, anchors the plant, stores food. Shoot (any two): support, photosynthesis, reproduction.
2. Xylem carries water (and minerals) from roots to leaves. Phloem carries food (sugars) from leaves to the rest of the plant.
3. Xylem — it carries water upward from the roots, and the dye is dissolved in the water.
4. Vascular plants have xylem to carry water up to any height. Mosses have no transport tubes, so every part must stay close to the water source.
5. **Stretch:** (a) The leaves keep making sugar at first but cannot send it away. (b) The roots receive no sugar; they have no chloroplasts and cannot make their own, so they starve and die. Once the roots fail, they stop supplying water, and the leaves then wilt and die too.

## Section 3: Photosynthesis

### Vocabulary

| Term | Model definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Photosynthesis | The process plants use to make food (glucose) from carbon dioxide and water using light energy | A leaf making sugar in sunlight |
| Chlorophyll | The green pigment in chloroplasts that captures light energy | Why leaves are green |
| Glucose | The sugar plants make as food; their energy source and building material | Stored as starch in a potato |
| Carbon dioxide (CO₂) | A gas in the air that plants take in through their leaves | The gas we breathe out |

### Retrieval Check — Section 3

1. Photosynthesis uses carbon dioxide, water, and light energy to produce glucose and oxygen.
2. In the chloroplasts.
3. Chlorophyll absorbs most colours of light but reflects green, so leaves look green.
4. Photosynthesis is not breathing. It is how a plant makes its food: it uses light energy to turn carbon dioxide and water into glucose, releasing oxygen as a by-product.
5. **Stretch:** In darkness photosynthesis stops, so the plant no longer removes CO₂ or releases O₂. The plant still uses its stored food for energy, so CO₂ rises and O₂ falls. After two weeks the plant is weakened and may die as its stored glucose runs out.

## Section 4: Plant Reproduction

### Vocabulary

| Term | Model definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Sexual reproduction | Reproduction that combines sex cells from two parents; offspring are genetically different | A canola seed formed after pollination |
| Asexual reproduction | Reproduction from one parent with no sex cells; offspring are identical to the parent | A strawberry runner |
| Pollination | The transfer of pollen from the anther to the stigma | A bee carrying pollen between flowers |
| Fertilization | The joining of the pollen's sex cell with the egg to form a seed | A fertilized ovule becoming a pea |
| Germination | When a seed begins to grow into a new plant | A bean sprouting |
| Vegetative reproduction | Asexual reproduction from a plant part such as a stem, root, or leaf | Potato tubers, cuttings |

### Retrieval Check — Section 4

1. Stamen: produces pollen. Pistil: receives pollen and contains the ovules, which become seeds after fertilization.
2. Any two with examples: runners (strawberry); bulbs (tulip, garlic); tubers (potato); cuttings (willow, geranium).
3. Animal dispersal by hitchhiking (hooks or burrs). The seeds are carried far from the parent plant, which reduces competition and spreads the species to new areas.
4. Variation means individuals have different traits. When conditions change (disease, drought, climate), some individuals are likely to survive and reproduce, so the species continues.
5. **Stretch:** Biologically it is one organism — one root system and one set of genes — even though it looks like many trees. Advantages: spreads quickly, shares water and nutrients, regrows after fire from the roots. Risk: no genetic variation, so one disease or pest could kill the entire grove.

### Vocabulary

| Term | Model definition | Example |

### Retrieval Check — Section 5

1. Food: any two of wheat, canola, barley, peas, lentils. Fibre: flax and hemp.
2. A food crop is grown to be eaten (or pressed for edible oil). A fibre crop is grown for its fibres to make materials such as fabric, rope, or paper.
3. Advantage: efficient large-scale planting and harvesting. Disadvantage: a single pest or disease can wipe out the whole crop (also lower biodiversity, soil depletion).
4. **Stretch:** Many Alberta crops, especially canola, depend on or benefit from insect pollination to set seed. Fewer bees means less pollination, lower seed yields, and less food and oil produced. Alberta is also a major honey producer.

## Section 6: Plant Biotechnology

### Vocabulary

| Term | Model definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Selective breeding | Choosing parent plants with wanted traits and breeding them over many generations | Wild mustard bred into broccoli and cabbage |
| Genetically modified organism (GMO) | An organism whose genes have been directly changed by scientists | Herbicide-tolerant canola |
| Biotechnology | Using living things or their parts to make products or solve problems | Breeding drought-tolerant wheat |

### Guided Notes

| Blank / Prompt | Expected Answer | Annotation |
|---|---|---|
| Humans choose plants with ___ traits | desirable (wanted) | |
| Scientists directly ___ specific genes | change (modify / alter) | |
| Perspectives — Agronomist | Support: higher yields, less pesticide and tillage, crops that tolerate drought or pests | Open response — accept any reasoned view that fits the role |
| Perspectives — Ecologist | Concern: less genetic diversity, genes spreading to wild relatives, herbicide-resistant weeds, effects on other insects | |
| Perspectives — Consumer | Mixed: wants proof of safety and clear labelling; may welcome lower prices or better nutrition | |
| Perspectives — Indigenous farmer | Concern: who owns the seeds; being able to save and share traditional seed varieties; responsibility to the land. Some may also see benefits | Avoid a single "correct" stance — the point is that values differ |

### Retrieval Check — Section 6

1. Choosing plants with desired traits and breeding only those, repeated over many generations. It has been used for thousands of years (about 10,000).
2. Any two: herbicide tolerance, pest resistance, drought tolerance.
3. Selective breeding: breeds whole plants, slow (many generations), less precise — many genes change at once. Genetic modification: changes specific genes directly in a lab, much faster, very precise.
4. **Stretch:** Both are blanket statements about a technique, but safety depends on the specific change. Questions to ask: What gene was changed and what does it do? Has the crop been tested for effects on health? Can it spread to wild plants or affect other species? Who benefits and who carries the risk? Each GMO should be judged on its own evidence.

## Unit Synthesis

1. **Plant cell drawing.** At least five labelled structures with functions: cell wall (support and shape), cell membrane (controls what enters and leaves), central vacuole (stores water, keeps the cell firm), chloroplasts (photosynthesis), nucleus (controls the cell, holds DNA).
2. **Photosynthesis in words.** Carbon dioxide + water, using light energy, → glucose + oxygen. Carbon dioxide comes from the air through pores in the leaf; water comes from the soil through the roots and xylem; light comes from the sun and is captured by chlorophyll; glucose is used for energy and growth or stored as starch (moved by phloem); oxygen is released into the air.

3. **Table from memory:**

| Concept | Model explanation |
|---|---|
| Three tenets of cell theory | All living things are made of cells; cells are the basic unit of life; all cells come from pre-existing cells |
| Xylem vs. phloem | Xylem carries water and minerals up from the roots; phloem carries sugars from the leaves to the rest of the plant |
| Why plants aren't getting food from soil | Plants make their own food by photosynthesis from carbon dioxide and water; soil supplies only water and minerals (van Helmont's willow gained 74 kg while the soil lost 57 g) |
| Sexual vs. asexual reproduction | Sexual: two parents, sex cells combine, offspring vary. Asexual: one parent, identical offspring, fast |
| Selective breeding vs. GMOs | Selective breeding chooses parents over many generations (slow, less precise); GMOs change specific genes directly (fast, precise) |

4. **Recommendation.** Either approach is acceptable if explained. Selective breeding: breed the plants that survive dry years, repeat over many generations — slow, and narrows genetic variety. GMO: insert or change a gene for drought tolerance — fast and precise; risks include gene spread to other plants, seed cost and ownership, and consumer acceptance.
5. **Return to the Pre-Knowledge Check.** Open reflection. Look for: Q1 — plants make food by photosynthesis, not from soil; Q2 — the leaf captures light with chlorophyll and makes glucose and oxygen from carbon dioxide and water.
