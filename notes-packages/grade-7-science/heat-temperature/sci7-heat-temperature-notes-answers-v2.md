## Section 1: Temperature and Measurement

### Vocabulary

| Term | Model definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | A measure of the average kinetic energy (speed of motion) of the particles in a substance | A room at 21 °C |
| Kelvin scale | A temperature scale that starts at absolute zero; used in science | Water freezes at 273 K |
| Celsius scale | A temperature scale based on water freezing at 0 and boiling at 100 | Body temperature is 37 °C |
| Absolute zero | The lowest possible temperature, where particles have the least possible motion | 0 K = −273 °C |
| Thermometer | A tool that measures temperature | A liquid thermometer on a classroom wall |

### Retrieval Check — Section 1

1. The average kinetic energy (speed of motion) of the particles in the substance.
2. 25 °C = 298 K; −10 °C = 263 K
3. Absolute zero (0 K, −273 °C) is the temperature at which particles have the least possible motion. You cannot go lower because there is no more motion energy to remove.
4. Liquids expand when heated because their particles move faster and spread farther apart. In a narrow tube, a small expansion shows as a clear rise in the liquid level, which can be read against a scale.
5. **Stretch:** 0 °C is just the freezing point of water — particles still have plenty of energy. 0 K means no particle motion, so Kelvin numbers are in proportion to particle energy. A negative Celsius value in an equation would suggest "negative energy" or give a meaningless answer (e.g., doubling −10 °C). Kelvin has no negatives, so the maths works.

## Section 2: Heat vs. Temperature

### Vocabulary

| Term | Model definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Heat | Energy that is transferred from a warmer object to a cooler one | Heat moving from soup into a spoon |
| Thermal energy | The total kinetic energy of all the particles in a substance | A bathtub of warm water has more than a cup at the same temperature |
| Heat transfer | The movement of thermal energy from one place or object to another | A hot stove warming a pan |
| Thermal equilibrium | When two objects in contact reach the same temperature and heat stops flowing | A drink left out until it reaches room temperature |

### Guided Notes

| Blank / Prompt | Expected Answer | Annotation |
|---|---|---|
| Misconception box — cup and bathtub at 90 °C have the same ___ | temperature | |
| The bathtub has far more ___ | thermal energy (heat energy) | More particles at the same average speed |
| Heat is energy ___ from a hotter object to a cooler one | transferred (moving) | |
| it is what we call the ___ of energy | transfer (flow / movement) | |
| Heat always flows from ___ (higher temperature) | hot (warmer) | |
| to ___ (lower temperature) | cold (cooler) | |
| reach the ___ temperature | same | |
| Table — total heat energy, cup | small (low) | |
| Table — total heat energy, lake | enormous (very high) | |
| Table — which cools your burn faster if spilled? | the cup — it holds so little total energy that it cools almost at once; the same amount of lake water would keep delivering heat | The point: same temperature, very different total energy |
| "Cold travels" box — ___ leaves your warm house | heat (thermal energy) | |
| your body ___ heat to the moving cold air | losing (transferring) | |
| Self-check question — the soup | No. Heat flowed from the hot soup into the cooler air, bowl, and table until the soup was close to room temperature. Nothing "cold" moved in | |

### Retrieval Check — Section 2

1. Temperature is the average kinetic energy of the particles (how fast they move). Heat is energy transferred between objects at different temperatures; it depends on how much matter is involved.
2. From hotter to cooler. When both reach the same temperature, heat transfer stops — thermal equilibrium.
3. The swimming pool. Its temperature is lower, but it has vastly more particles, so its total thermal energy is far greater.
4. "You're letting the heat out." Heat flows from the warm room to the cold outside; cold is not a substance that moves in.
5. **Stretch:** In the soup: heat flows from the hot soup into the cooler spoon (conduction) until the spoon nears the soup's temperature. On the plate: the spoon is now the warmer object, so heat flows from the spoon into the cooler plate — the spoon cools and the plate warms until they reach the same temperature (thermal equilibrium).

## Section 3: The Particle Model and Heat

### Vocabulary

| Term | Model definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Particle model | The idea that all matter is made of tiny particles that are always moving | Particles in a solid vibrate in place |
| Thermal expansion | Matter taking up more space when heated because its particles move farther apart | A bridge getting longer in summer |
| Thermal contraction | Matter taking up less space when cooled because its particles move closer together | Power lines tightening in winter |
| Change of state | Matter changing between solid, liquid, and gas as heat is absorbed or released | Ice melting |

| Blank / Prompt | Expected Answer | Annotation |

| Freezing — heat ___ | released | |
| Evaporation/boiling — heat ___ | absorbed | |
| Condensation — heat ___ | released | |
| Condensation — particle change | particles slow down and move close enough for attraction to pull them together into a liquid | |
| added heat goes into ___ the attractive forces | breaking (overcoming) | |
| the temperature ___ | stays the same (stays constant) | |

### Retrieval Check — Section 3

1. Heating gives the particles more energy, so they move or vibrate faster and push farther apart. The material takes up more space.
2. Steel rails expand in summer heat. Without gaps they would have nowhere to go and would buckle.
3. The added energy is being used to break the attractive forces holding the ice particles in place (changing state), not to speed the particles up. The temperature stays at 0 °C until all the ice has melted.
4. (a) Cold solid: particles close together in a regular pattern, small vibration marks. (b) Warm solid: same pattern, slightly farther apart, larger vibration marks. Particles are the same size and number in both.
5. **Stretch:** Temperature range = 35 − (−40) = 75 °C. 0.0012% = 0.000012 per degree. Expansion = 2000 m × 0.000012 × 75 = 1.8 m. The bridge is about 1.8 m longer in summer than in winter, which is why it needs expansion joints. *Correction: the v1 key gave 0.18 m (18 cm) by using 0.0000012 — one decimal place too many.*

## Section 4: Modes of Heat Transfer

### Vocabulary

| Term | Model definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Conduction | Heat transfer by direct contact, passed from particle to particle | A spoon warming in hot soup |
| Convection | Heat transfer by the movement of a fluid (liquid or gas) | Warm air rising from a heat register |
| Radiation | Heat transfer by electromagnetic waves; needs no matter | Feeling the Sun or a campfire on your face |
| Insulator | A material that transfers heat slowly | Wood, foam, wool, air |
| Conductor | A material that transfers heat quickly | Copper, aluminum, steel |

| Dark/matte surfaces ___ radiation better | absorb | |

### Retrieval Check — Section 4

1. Campfire from 2 metres away: radiation. Spoon in hot soup: conduction. Hot air rising from a radiator: convection.
2. Radiation travels as electromagnetic waves, which are energy and do not need particles to carry them. They can cross empty space.
3. Both desks are at room temperature. Metal is a good conductor, so it carries heat away from your hand quickly and your hand feels cold. Wood is a poor conductor, so your hand loses heat slowly and the wood feels warm.
4. Convection needs the material itself to flow and circulate. Particles in a solid are held in fixed positions, so they cannot move from place to place.
5. **Stretch:** Vacuum layer: stops conduction and convection — no particles to pass energy along or to circulate. Shiny inner walls: reduce radiation by reflecting it back. Plastic cap: an insulator that slows conduction, and it stops warm air escaping by convection at the top.

## Section 5: Specific Heat Capacity

### Vocabulary

| Term | Model definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Specific heat capacity | The amount of heat energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 gram of a substance by 1 °C | Water needs far more energy than sand to warm up |
| Thermal reservoir | A large body of material that stores a great deal of heat and releases it slowly | The Pacific Ocean; a large lake |

### Guided Notes

| Blank / Prompt | Expected Answer | Annotation |
|---|---|---|
| raise the temperature of 1 gram by ___ | 1 °C | |
| Some materials heat up (and cool down) ___ than others | faster (more quickly) | |
| Water requires about ___ times more energy | 4 | Unit context figure ("about 4× most metals"); for iron or copper the real ratio is closer to 10 |
| Water — very ___ | high | |
| Metal — real-world implication | heats up and cools down quickly (a pan heats fast; a metal slide burns in the sun) | |
| Sand / soil — real-world implication | heats quickly in the sun and cools quickly at night (hot beach sand, cold desert nights) | |
| Air — real-world implication | temperature changes very quickly (big day-to-night swings; chinooks) | |
| Used as a ___ in car radiators | coolant | |
| Oceans act as a ___ | thermal reservoir | |
| Inland Alberta has ___ temperature swings | larger (more extreme) | |
| Why soil freezes faster than rivers in autumn | soil has a much lower specific heat than water, so it has less stored heat to lose before reaching 0 °C; river water holds more heat (and keeps moving) | |

### Retrieval Check — Section 5

1. The amount of heat energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 gram of a substance by 1 °C. Water has the higher specific heat.
2. Water can absorb a large amount of heat with only a small rise in its own temperature, so it carries heat away from the engine efficiently.
3. Sand has a low specific heat, so the same sunlight raises its temperature quickly. Water has a high specific heat, so the same energy raises its temperature only a little.
4. **Stretch:** The Pacific Ocean is a thermal reservoir: it absorbs heat all summer and releases it slowly in winter, and moving air (convection) carries that warmth over Vancouver. Calgary has no large body of water nearby; land and air have low specific heat, so they warm and cool quickly and temperatures swing widely.

## Section 6: Insulation and Applications

### Vocabulary

| Term | Model definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Insulation | Material that slows heat transfer | Fibreglass batts, a down jacket, foam |
| R-value | A number that rates how well insulation resists heat flow; higher is better | Attic insulation rated R-50 |
| Thermal pollution | Harmful warming of a lake or river by waste heat | Warm cooling water released by a power plant |

### Guided Notes

| Blank / Prompt | Expected Answer | Annotation |
|---|---|---|
| Insulation works by slowing ___ | heat transfer | |
| Trapped air — which mode it slows | conduction and convection | |
| Air is a poor ___ | conductor | |
| Fibreglass batts — which mode | conduction (and convection) | |
| Vacuum — which mode | conduction and convection | |
| Shiny surfaces — which mode | radiation | |
| R-value: a measure of insulation ___ | effectiveness (resistance to heat flow) | |
| Biggest sources of heat loss (3 blanks) | windows and doors; attic/roof; walls (also air leaks, basement) | |
| ___ levels in water decrease | dissolved oxygen | |
| slows the ___ of heat | transfer (flow) | |
| Misconception box — slows heat transfer in ___ directions | both | |

### Retrieval Check — Section 6

1. Insulation slows heat transfer in both directions; it does not make heat. A vacuum stops conduction and convection (it does not stop radiation).
2. Alberta winters are much colder, so the temperature difference between inside and outside is larger and heat is lost faster. More insulation is needed to slow it.
3. Thermal pollution is the release of warm water (from industry or power plants) into rivers and lakes. Warm water holds less dissolved oxygen, which harms fish and other aquatic life.
4. **Stretch:** Example design — shiny reflective outer layer (reflects radiation from the sun); thick foam or a vacuum layer (slows conduction and stops convection); tight-fitting insulated lid (stops warm air entering by convection and slows conduction); optional ice packs inside. Each layer must be matched to the mode it blocks, with a reason.

## Unit Synthesis

1. **Table from memory:**

| Concept | Model explanation |
|---|---|
| Difference between heat and temperature | Temperature = average kinetic energy of the particles. Heat = energy transferred from a hotter object to a cooler one |
| Why metal feels colder than wood | Both are at the same temperature; metal conducts heat away from your hand much faster |
| Three modes of heat transfer | Conduction (direct contact, particle to particle); convection (moving fluids); radiation (electromagnetic waves, no matter needed) |
| Why water has a high specific heat | It takes a lot of energy to raise its temperature, so it heats and cools slowly and stores large amounts of heat. (Grade 7 level: students state what this means, not the molecular cause) |
| How insulation works | It slows heat transfer in both directions, mostly by trapping still air; it does not create heat |
| Absolute zero | 0 K = −273 °C; the lowest possible temperature, where particles have the least possible motion |

2. **The mug of cocoa.** Conduction: heat passes from the cocoa into the mug, and from the mug into the table and into your hand. Convection: air warmed above and beside the mug rises and carries heat away; evaporation from the surface also carries energy off. Radiation: the warm mug gives off infrared waves in all directions.
3. **Three recommendations.** Any three with the science, e.g., add attic and wall insulation (trapped air slows conduction and convection; higher R-value); install double- or triple-pane windows (the gas gap is a poor conductor; coatings reflect radiation); seal air leaks around doors and windows (stops warm air escaping by convection); use heavy masonry, tile, or water to store daytime heat (high heat capacity releases heat slowly at night).
4. **Return to the Pre-Knowledge Check.** Open reflection. Look for: Q1 — heat and temperature are different; Q2 — metal and wood are the same temperature, metal just conducts faster; Q4 — "cold" does not come from anywhere; Alberta winters are cold because less energy arrives from the Sun (lower Sun angle, shorter days) and heat is lost from the ground and air.
