### Notes

**What is matter?**

Matter is anything that has _______ and takes up _______. The chair you sit on, the air you breathe, the water you drink — all of these are matter.

**The particle model — a scientific abstraction**

Scientists use a model called the **particle model of matter** to explain how matter behaves. The particle model has four key ideas:

1. All matter is made up of tiny _______ that are too small to see with the naked eye.
2. There are _______ between particles — particles are not packed together with zero space.
3. Particles are always _______. They never stop completely.
4. The _______ of that movement changes with temperature.

---

> *Connection to Computer Science:* The particle model is an **abstraction** — it keeps only what matters (particle spacing and movement speed) and removes everything else (actual molecular structure, chemical bonding, quantum behaviour). In computer science, abstraction means making a simplified version of something complex that is still useful for the job. A map is an abstraction of a city — it removes buildings, textures, and smells, but keeps streets and directions. The particle model does the same thing for matter.

---

> **MISCONCEPTION**
>
> *"A particle is just a tiny piece of the material — like a tiny piece of steel."*
>
> This is one of the most common misunderstandings in this unit. A particle in the particle model is **not** a miniature version of the material. A particle of steel is not a tiny steel shaving. Particles are discrete units with **empty space between them** — that space is real, and it is what allows matter to expand and contract. If particles were stuck together with no space, nothing could ever change size with temperature. The space between particles is as important as the particles themselves.

**The experimental logic of phase changes:**

Scientists who study phase changes control one variable at a time. To test how temperature affects the phase change of water, you would:
- Keep the same _______ of water each time (the amount doesn't change the melting or boiling points)
- Change only the _______ (or the heat source)
- Observe the temperature at which the _______ happens

This is controlled testing — changing one thing and watching for one result.

---

**CRITICAL IDEA — Mass is conserved during a phase change:**

When matter changes state, _______ is conserved. Only the _______ and _______ of the particles change.

100 g of ice → melts → still _______ g of liquid water
100 g of liquid water → evaporates → still _______ g of water vapour (as a gas)

The mass does NOT change. The particles are the same particles — they are just moving differently and arranged differently.

Volume, however, _______ change during a phase change. Water vapour takes up far more space than the same mass of liquid water.

---

> **MISCONCEPTION**
>
> *"When ice melts, some of the mass disappears — that's why there's less water."*
>
> Mass is **never** created or destroyed during a phase change. The ice and the puddle of water weigh exactly the same. What changes is the **arrangement** of particles and the **volume** they take up — not the number of particles or their mass. If water seems to "disappear" when it evaporates, it has not vanished — it has become water vapour in the air, still carrying the same mass. Conservation of mass is one of the most fundamental laws in science.

**The Celsius scale**

Temperature is measured in degrees Celsius (°C) in science and in everyday life in Canada.

The Celsius scale uses two reference points based on water:

| Reference point | Temperature |
|---|---|
| Water _______ (liquid → solid) | 0°C |
| Water _______ (liquid → gas) | 100°C |
| Normal human body temperature | ~37°C |
| Room temperature (comfortable) | ~20–22°C |
| A cold winter day in Alberta | ~−20°C |

These reference points were chosen because they are _______ (they always happen at the same temperature, at standard conditions) and easy to reproduce in any lab.

---

**How a thermometer works**

A traditional liquid thermometer works because of the same particle behaviour you just studied. Here is the process:

1. The thermometer contains a liquid (often coloured alcohol) sealed in a glass tube.
2. When temperature _______, the liquid particles move faster and spread further apart. The liquid _______ and rises up the tube.
3. When temperature _______, the liquid particles slow down and come closer together. The liquid _______ and falls down the tube.
4. A scale on the glass shows what temperature the liquid level matches.

The thermometer works because the _______ of the liquid changes predictably with temperature — more heat = more expansion = higher reading.

### Notes

**The general rule: expansion and contraction**

The particle model predicts a clear pattern for most materials:

- When a material is _______, its particles move faster and spread further apart. The material takes up more space — it _______.
- When a material is _______, its particles slow down and move closer together. The material takes up less space — it _______.

This applies to solids, liquids, and gases — though gases expand and contract far more dramatically than solids or liquids.

---

**Real-world applications of expansion and contraction:**

Engineers must account for expansion and contraction in design. Materials that are fixed rigidly in place and cannot expand will _______, crack, or buckle.

**Expansion joints in bridges and roads:**

Long bridges and highway sections are not one continuous piece of material. Engineers leave small _______ between sections — these are called expansion joints.

[SVG: bridge deck cross-section — three labeled bridge sections (A, B, C) with highlighted expansion joint gaps between them. Arrows point to each gap. Fill-in lines for summer (gap gets ___) and winter (gap gets ___).]

Without expansion joints, the force of expansion would _______ the bridge.

**Gaps in railway tracks:**

Traditional rail tracks also leave small gaps between rail sections for the same reason. Modern continuous welded rail is pre-stressed during installation to account for _______.

**Power lines:**

Electrical power lines hang lower in _______ (summer heat causes expansion and sagging) and are pulled tighter in _______ (winter cold causes contraction). Line engineers calculate this when setting the height of the towers.

**The water anomaly — when the general rule breaks**

For almost every material, the solid is _______ than the liquid. More tightly packed particles = smaller volume for the same mass = higher density.

Water breaks this rule. This is called the **water anomaly**.

When water freezes:
- Most liquids become more _______ when they solidify
- Water becomes _______ dense when it freezes

This means ice takes up _______ space than the same mass of liquid water. Ice is less dense than liquid water. This is why ice _______.

---

**Why water is different — the particle model explanation:**

In liquid water, particles are packed closely and moving freely. When water freezes, the particles slow down and lock into a crystal structure. This crystal structure has a _______ arrangement that actually pushes particles further apart — creating more space, not less.

[SVG: liquid water vs ice particle comparison — LEFT: liquid water particles randomly and closely packed (Density: HIGHER). RIGHT: ice particles in a regular hexagonal offset-grid pattern with more space between them (Density: LOWER, ice floats). Side-by-side boxes with captions.]

---

> **MISCONCEPTION**
>
> *"Ice is denser than liquid water — solids are always denser than liquids."*
>
> For most materials, this is true — but **water is a major exception**. Ice is **less** dense than liquid water. This is why ice floats at the surface rather than sinking to the bottom. The reason is the structure water forms when it freezes: a hexagonal crystal lattice that takes up **more** space than randomly arranged liquid water particles. This is not a small exception — it has enormous consequences for life on Earth.

---

**Why the water anomaly matters — ecological and practical consequences:**

**Aquatic life in winter:**

When a lake cools in winter, the ice forms on the _______ and floats. The liquid water beneath the ice stays at approximately _______ — cold, but not frozen. Fish, plants, and other organisms can survive through winter in the liquid layer _______. 

If ice were denser than liquid water, ice would sink to the bottom. Lakes and rivers could freeze _______ to _______ in winter, and most aquatic life would not survive.

**Infrastructure — ice in rock cracks:**

Water that seeps into rock cracks and freezes expands as it solidifies. This expansion breaks rock apart — a process called freeze-thaw weathering. Engineers building roads in cold climates must account for this in drainage design.

---

**FNMI Knowledge — seasonal ice observation in northern Alberta:**

Cree, Dene, and Métis communities in northern Alberta have observed and documented seasonal freeze-thaw cycles on rivers and lakes for generations. This knowledge includes when ice is thick enough for travel, how ice changes near river currents, and how early melt or late freeze signals broader ecological shifts. This is a form of long-term data collection — systematic observation across seasons and years — that aligns with what scientists now measure using sensors and satellite data. Both are ways of tracking the same real patterns. Scientific observation and Indigenous ecological knowledge, in this case, describe the same anomalous behaviour of water from different frameworks.

---

**The water anomaly as a testable prediction:**

Most people, if asked, would predict: ice sinks. The particle model (incorrectly applied without knowing about water's crystal structure) would also predict: solid is denser, so solid sinks.

The observation — ice floats — **surprises** that prediction. This is exactly how science works: you make a prediction, you test it, and when the result surprises you, you do not discard the observation. You revise your model. The water anomaly was a real scientific surprise. The hexagonal crystal structure explanation came *after* scientists observed that ice floats and had to figure out why.
