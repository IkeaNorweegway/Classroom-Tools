> **MISCONCEPTION**
>
> **"Renewable = clean"**
>
> Renewable means the resource is _______ naturally and will not run out. It does **NOT** mean the resource is environmentally harmless. Large hydroelectric dams flood entire river ecosystems. Wind turbines require land and affect bird migration. Solar panel manufacturing uses toxic materials. **Renewable and clean are separate ideas** — a resource can be one without being the other.

## Section 2: Before and After Processing

**Learning target:** I can explain the difference between using an energy resource directly and using it after processing, and I can identify examples of each — including resources that are used both ways.

---

### Vocabulary

| Term | Definition in your own words | One example |
|---|---|---|
| Before processing | | |
| After processing | | |
| Conversion | | |

---

### Notes

Some energy resources can be used _______ in their natural form — before any processing or conversion happens.

Some energy resources must be _______ into another form first — after processing — before we can use the energy in them.

This does **NOT** map cleanly onto renewable versus non-renewable. A resource can be renewable AND used before processing, or renewable AND used after processing. The two categories are _______.

---

**Wind — the key example:**

Wind is a renewable resource that is used **both** before and after processing:

| | Before processing | After processing |
|---|---|---|
| **Wind** | A traditional windmill uses wind _______ to turn a millstone (grinding grain) or pump water | A modern wind _______ converts wind into electricity — a different form of energy |

The windmill and the wind turbine both use wind, but in very different ways. The turbine adds a conversion step.

---

**Before and after processing — multiple examples:**

| Resource | Before processing | After processing |
|---|---|---|
| Wood | Burning logs directly to heat a room | Converting wood to _______ (charcoal), which burns hotter and cleaner |
| Solar energy | A south-facing window lets sunlight warm a room _______ | A photovoltaic (PV) cell converts sunlight into _______ |
| Crude oil | — (not used directly in natural form) | Refined into _______, diesel, and other fuels |
| Moving water | A water wheel uses moving water _______ to turn a millstone | A _______ dam generates electricity |
| Biomass (grain/sugar) | Eaten directly as food — the body extracts energy | Fermented and distilled into _______ fuel |

---

> **MISCONCEPTION**
>
> **"Before processing = renewable, after processing = non-renewable"**
>
> This is **not true**. The before/after processing categories and the renewable/non-renewable categories are completely independent. Look at the examples:
> - Wind (_______ — renewable) can be used before processing (windmill) or after (turbine)
> - Wood (can be renewable) can be used before processing (burning logs) or after (charcoal)
> - Crude oil (non-renewable) is only used after processing — but that is not because it is non-renewable. It is because oil in its raw form is not useful as a fuel.
>
> **Do not mix these two classification systems. They answer different questions.**

## Section 3: Selecting Energy Resources

**Learning target:** I can name and explain the five factors used to select an energy resource, apply all five to a real Alberta decision, and explain why selecting an energy resource involves trade-offs and evidence.

---

### Vocabulary

| Term | Definition in your own words | One example |
|---|---|---|
| Availability | | |
| Societal impact | | |
| Economic impact | | |
| Environmental impact | | |
| Responsible management | | |

---

### Notes

Choosing which energy resource to use is not simple. Many factors must be considered. Scientists, engineers, governments, and communities all weigh these _______ when making energy decisions.

There are **five selection factors:**

---

**Factor 1: Availability**

Is the resource present in _______ quantity at the location?

- A region with consistent _______ can use wind power effectively.
- A region with little sunlight in winter may not get enough energy from _______ panels year-round.
- A community without a nearby _______ cannot use hydroelectric power.

Availability is about geography and _______ — does the resource exist in usable amounts, where and when it is needed?

---

**Factor 2: Societal Impact**

How does using this resource affect people and communities?

Societal impact includes:
- _______ — does extracting or generating this energy create employment?
- _______ effects — does the resource benefit or harm nearby communities?
- Public _______ — do people support or oppose this energy project in their community?
- _______ — can this energy source deliver power consistently, or does it only work when the sun shines or wind blows?

Indigenous land stewardship principles align closely with this factor. First Nations and Métis communities in Alberta understand resources as being held _______ for future generations — not simply extracted for present-day benefit. Many First Nations and Métis communities have been significantly affected by oil sands development on or near their territories. Today, many of the same communities are leaders in _______ energy development on their lands, bringing both economic benefit and stewardship values to the work.

---

**Factor 3: Economic Impact**

What does it cost?

Economic impact includes the cost to:
- _______ the system (building a wind farm, installing solar panels, drilling for natural gas)
- _______ the system (ongoing upkeep and repairs)
- _______ the system day-to-day (running costs)

A resource that is cheap to extract may be expensive to process. A resource that is expensive to install (e.g., solar) may cost almost nothing to operate once built. Economic decisions look at the _______ cost over the full life of the project.

---

**Factor 4: Environmental Impact**

How does using this resource affect the environment?

Environmental impact includes:
- _______ — releasing greenhouse gases or particulates into the air
- _______ use — how much land is disturbed or taken up?
- _______ disruption — does extraction or infrastructure harm wildlife or ecosystems?
- Water _______ — does the extraction process affect rivers, groundwater, or lakes?

> *Computer Science connection:* Technology is designed to solve problems, but it always has unintended consequences. Smart grids, electric vehicles, and renewable energy infrastructure are **computational artifacts** — they are designed systems, built to reduce emissions and improve energy efficiency. But they have their own unintended consequences: mining rare earth metals for batteries and solar panels disturbs ecosystems; rapid expansion of electric vehicles creates e-waste; connecting more renewable sources to the electrical grid can cause _______ instability when supply and demand are mismatched. Every technological solution creates new trade-offs that must be monitored and managed.

---

**Factor 5: Responsible Management**

How do we use the resource wisely over the long term?

Responsible management includes:
- _______ — using less energy overall, reducing waste
- Long-term _______ — will this resource still be available and affordable in 50 years?
- _______ monitoring — using data to track the impacts of energy use and adjust as needed
- Leaving options open for _______ generations — not locking in decisions that cannot be reversed

This factor connects directly to scientific methods: responsible management requires _______ collection (how much energy is being used?), evidence-based decisions (what do the data show about environmental impact?), and a willingness to _______ decisions when evidence changes.

This factor also aligns with Indigenous land stewardship — the idea that the land, water, and resources we use today are held in trust, not owned. What we take now affects what is available to those who come after us.

---

**Applying all five factors — a real Alberta decision:**

A small Alberta town must choose how to generate electricity. Three options are on the table:

| Factor | Natural gas | Wind | Solar |
|---|---|---|---|
| Availability | Gas pipelines already connect the town | Strong winds in the region year-round | Good summer sun; limited winter daylight |
| Societal impact | Jobs in existing industry; some air quality concerns | New industry jobs; some noise concerns near turbines | Minimal disruption; no noise |
| Economic impact | Low install cost; rising long-term fuel costs | High install cost; very low operating cost | High install cost; very low operating cost |
| Environmental impact | CO₂ and methane emissions | Land use; bird and bat migration | Panel manufacturing uses toxic materials |
| Responsible management | Fossil fuel — non-renewable; supply will eventually decline | Renewable indefinitely if maintained | Renewable indefinitely if maintained |

*No single option is perfect. The town must weigh all five factors and make a decision with evidence — knowing there are trade-offs.*
