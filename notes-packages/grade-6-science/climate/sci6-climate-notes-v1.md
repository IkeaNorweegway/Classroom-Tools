### Notes

**Weather** is the state of the atmosphere at a _______ place and _______ time — hour to hour, or day to day.

**Climate** is the _______ pattern of weather in a region, measured over _______ — usually at least 30 years.

Weather can change from one day to the next. Climate describes what is _______ over many decades.

---

**What weather tells us — and what it doesn't:**

A single cold week in January does not tell us anything about climate. A single hot summer does not confirm climate change. These are _______ data points.

Climate claims require _______ data: measurements collected consistently over decades, across many locations.

[SVG: timescale comparison — weather bar shows day-by-day scale; climate bar shows three 10-year spans; arrows indicate what each timescale is useful for]

---

**Alberta examples — making the distinction:**

| Observation | What it tells us |
|---|---|
| Edmonton had its coldest recorded February in 2024 | Weather — one cold stretch in one location |
| Average winter temperatures in Alberta have risen 2°C over the past 50 years | Climate trend — long-term, large-scale pattern |
| July 2023 was the hottest month globally since records began | Possible climate signal — needs decades of context to interpret as a trend |

---

**Why this difference matters for making claims:**

When scientists make claims about climate change, they are using _______ data — often from thousands of weather stations, ocean sensors, and satellites, measured over decades.

A claim like "climate change isn't real because it snowed in May" confuses _______ (a short event) with _______ (a long-term pattern). These are different questions requiring different kinds of evidence.

The timescale of your evidence must match the _______ of your claim.

---

> **MISCONCEPTION**
>
> *"Weather and climate are the same thing — one cold year proves climate change is wrong."*
>
> Weather and climate operate on entirely different timescales. A cold week or even a cold year tells you nothing about a 30-year trend, just as one bad hair day tells you nothing about whether your hair is growing longer over the year. Climate scientists look at decades of data, not individual events. A single cold year inside a long-term warming trend is still a warming trend — the trend is defined by the full data set, not the exceptions inside it. Claims about climate require climate-scale evidence: decades of data, not days or months.

Earth's climate has always changed. It has been much warmer and much colder in its past — long before humans existed. Understanding what caused those changes helps us understand what is causing the changes happening now.

**Natural causes of climate change:**

**1. Volcanic eruptions**

**2. Changes in Earth's orbit (Milankovitch cycles)**

**1. Burning fossil fuels**

**4. Industrial pollution**
Factories and industrial processes release a range of greenhouse gases including _______ oxide (N₂O) from fertilisers, and various industrial gases with very high warming potential.

---

> **MISCONCEPTION**
>
> *"Climate has always changed naturally, so current climate change is just natural variation."*
>
> Natural climate changes are real — Earth has had ice ages and warm periods. But the rate and pattern of current change is outside natural variation. Ice core data shows that CO₂ levels today (~420 ppm) are higher than at any point in the past 800,000 years of natural cycles. Natural variations happen over thousands to tens of thousands of years; current warming has happened in roughly 150 years. The pattern — and the speed — match the rise in human fossil fuel use, not any natural cycle scientists can identify.

---

- Orbital cycles: _______ — these operate over tens of thousands of years

**Computer models — climate science as computational thinking:**

Climate scientists build **computer climate models** — programs that simulate how Earth's climate system works. These models are **computational artifacts**: tools created through programming and design to solve a specific problem (understanding and predicting climate).

A climate model is an **abstraction**. It represents the key variables of Earth's climate system:
- _______ levels and other greenhouse gas concentrations
- Ocean _______ and circulation
- Ice and snow cover (albedo)
- Solar _______
- Atmospheric circulation patterns

The model _______ this detail in order to be computationally workable — not every tree, wave, or cloud is simulated. This is the same principle as a force diagram (from the Forces unit): keep the essential relationships, remove the unnecessary detail.

> *Connection to Computer Science:* Climate models are built on the same principle as any simulation program. Scientists write code that represents physical laws, run the model forward in time, and compare the output to observed data. When the model's predictions match real-world measurements, that increases confidence in the model. Scientists then use these models to project what happens under different scenarios — what if emissions stay high? What if they drop sharply by 2030?

---

**Long-term data vs. short-term noise:**

Climate data contains a lot of variation from year to year — this is called **noise**. A single year's data point might be unusually high or low for reasons unrelated to the long-term trend.

The _______ tells us the direction the system is moving over decades, despite the year-to-year variation.

[SVG: signal vs. noise scatter graph — temperature on Y axis, time in decades on X axis; eleven data point dots scattered around an upward-sloping accent trend line; legend distinguishes trend line (climate signal) from scatter dots (individual year noise)]

This is why scientists need _______ of data points before making a confident claim about a climate trend — not to be slow, but to separate signal from noise reliably.

## Greenhouse Effect vs. Ozone Hole — Dedicated Callout

> **MISCONCEPTION**
>
> *"The greenhouse effect and the ozone hole are the same thing — both are caused by pollution and both make the planet hotter."*
>
> These are two completely separate phenomena. They involve different gases, different layers of the atmosphere, and different effects:
>
> | | Greenhouse Effect | Ozone Hole |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Gases involved** | CO₂, methane, water vapour | CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons) |
> | **Layer of atmosphere** | Troposphere (lower atmosphere) | Stratosphere (upper atmosphere, ~15–35 km up) |
> | **What it does** | Traps heat — warms the planet | Thins UV protection — allows more UV radiation to reach Earth's surface |
> | **Main effect** | Global temperature increase | Increased UV exposure (skin cancer, ecosystem damage) |
> | **Main human cause** | Burning fossil fuels, deforestation | Aerosol sprays, old refrigerants (now banned under Montreal Protocol) |
>
> Confusing these two produces wrong conclusions about causes and solutions. Fixing ozone depletion (banning CFCs) does not fix climate change (still need to reduce CO₂). They are separate problems requiring separate responses.
