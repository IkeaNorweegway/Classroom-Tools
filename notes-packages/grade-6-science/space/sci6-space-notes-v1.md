**Pluto — why was it reclassified?**

For most of the 20th century, Pluto was called the 9th planet. In 2006, the International Astronomical Union (IAU) officially reclassified it as a _______.

To count as a full planet, a body must:
1. Orbit the _______
2. Have enough mass to pull itself into a roughly _______ shape
3. Have _______ its orbital neighbourhood of other debris

Pluto meets conditions 1 and 2, but not _______. Astronomers discovered many other similar-sized icy objects in the region beyond Neptune called the **Kuiper Belt**. Pluto is one of many — not a unique planet. Reclassifying it made the definition more _______ and accurate.

**Emit vs. reflect light:**

This is one of the most important ideas in this section.

- _______ means to produce and send out light. The Sun does this — it generates its own energy.
- _______ means to bounce back light that came from somewhere else. Planets, moons, and asteroids do this — they are visible because the Sun's light hits them and bounces toward our eyes.

This is why you can see planets in the night sky even though they are not on fire — they are _______ sunlight, not producing it.

---

**Scale — why diagrams lie**

Here is something important: every diagram of the solar system you have ever seen is _______ to scale.

Textbook diagrams make the planets look large and close together. The real solar system is almost entirely _______ space. The distances are so enormous that a true-scale diagram would not fit on any piece of paper.

Here is a real-scale comparison to help you feel the size:

> If the Sun were a **basketball** (about 24 cm across), the Earth would be a **pinhead** roughly **30 metres away**. Neptune — the outermost planet — would be about **1 kilometre away**.

On that same scale, the planets would each be smaller than a grain of sand.

**Why does this matter?**

Scale models are a tool scientists use to _______ complex systems. But you must always know whether a model is to scale or not — because a model that is not to scale can give you wrong ideas about distances and relative sizes.

> *Scientific Methods — Scale models:* A scale model shows accurate proportions. A diagram that is NOT to scale can still show order and relationships — but it cannot tell you about _______ or _______ size. Always ask: "Is this model to scale? What can I — and can I NOT — learn from it?"

---

> **MISCONCEPTION**
>
> *"The solar system diagram in my textbook shows the planets close together, so the solar system must be fairly compact."*
>
> Textbook diagrams are NOT to scale. They compress enormous distances to fit on a page. In reality, the solar system is almost entirely empty space. If the planets were shown at their true distances from the Sun, most of the diagram would be blank. The order of the planets is correct in these diagrams — the distances are not.

---

> **MISCONCEPTION**
>
> *"Planets are visible at night because they produce their own light, like stars do."*
>
> Planets do NOT produce their own light. They reflect light from the Sun. The difference matters: a star is massive enough for nuclear fusion to occur — the process that generates light and heat. Planets are not. When you see Venus, Mars, or Jupiter in the night sky, you are seeing reflected sunlight bouncing off their surfaces or atmospheres — not light they are generating themselves.

---

**Indigenous sky knowledge:**

Indigenous peoples across what is now Canada have long, rich traditions of sky observation. Cree, Blackfoot, and many other First Nations have names and stories for stars, constellations, and seasonal sky patterns. This knowledge was not casual — it was systematic and practical: used for navigation, tracking seasons, timing planting and harvesting, and understanding the year's cycles. This is a form of long-term scientific observation. It developed over generations of careful watching and record-keeping. Western astronomy and Indigenous sky knowledge are two knowledge systems that have observed the same sky — each producing frameworks that are meaningful, purposeful, and grounded in sustained attention to the world above.

**How do we explore space without going there?**

Space is extremely far away. Most of it is impossible for humans to visit directly. Instead, scientists use a range of _______ — each designed to answer a different kind of question.

**Exploration technology table:**

| Tool | What it does | Example mission or use |
|---|---|---|
| Optical telescope | Collects _______ light; used to observe distant objects from Earth | Backyard telescopes; large observatories |
| Radio telescope | Detects _______ waves from space; can "see" through dust and gas that blocks visible light | Arecibo Observatory; studying pulsars and distant galaxies |
| Space-based telescope | Orbits Earth above the atmosphere; no atmospheric blur; sharper images | Hubble Space Telescope; James Webb Space Telescope |
| Space probe | Robotic spacecraft; no crew; travels _______ to other bodies to collect data and send it back | Voyager 1 (interstellar space); New Horizons (Pluto flyby) |
| Rover | Lands on a surface and _______ across it; takes samples and measurements | Curiosity (Mars); Perseverance (Mars) |
| Crewed spacecraft / station | Carries human astronauts; allows direct human work in space | Apollo missions (Moon); International Space Station |
| Computer modelling | Uses software to _______ conditions we cannot directly observe; tests hypotheses mathematically | Simulating how stars form; modelling planetary atmospheres |

**Why use different tools for different questions?**

Each tool has strengths and limits.

- A telescope shows us _______ — we can observe but not interact.
- A space probe gets _______ — it can take measurements at the source, but cannot move around.
- A rover allows detailed _______ — it can test materials, photograph up close, and drill.
- A crewed mission adds human _______ and flexibility — but is expensive and risky.
- Computer models can go where nothing else can — inside a star, billions of years into the past — but they depend on the _______ we already have. A model is only as good as the assumptions that built it.

Scientists choose the tool that matches the _______. There is no single best tool for all of space science.

---

**Ex-Alta 1 — Alberta's satellite**

Ex-Alta 1 is a small satellite called a **CubeSat**. It was designed and built by students at the University of Alberta. It was launched into orbit in _______.

Ex-Alta 1 orbits _______ and collects data on the **upper atmosphere** — specifically on space weather events that affect Earth's magnetic field.

This matters for two reasons:

1. It shows that satellite technology is not only for large space agencies. Students with the right knowledge and tools can build technology that serves a real scientific purpose.
2. It is an example of a **computational artifact** — a human-built system designed to collect data from an environment we cannot directly access, transmit it back to Earth, and contribute to scientific knowledge.

> *CS Connection — Ex-Alta 1 as a designed system:*
> Ask: **What problem does Ex-Alta 1 solve?** It extends human sensing into the upper atmosphere — a region we cannot send people. **What data does it collect?** Measurements of charged particles and magnetic field changes linked to solar storms. **How does it transmit data back?** By radio signal — data is encoded, sent to a ground station, decoded, and analyzed. This is the same logic behind every remote sensing system: a sensor in a hard-to-reach place, connected to a human-readable output through a data pipeline. The satellite does not think — it is designed to sense, encode, and transmit. The meaning comes from the scientists interpreting the data on the ground.

**Natural satellites**

A natural satellite is any body that _______ another body in space — formed through natural processes, not built by humans.

The most familiar natural satellite is Earth's _______. Other planets have moons too: Mars has 2, Jupiter has 95, Saturn has over 140.

**Artificial satellites**

An artificial satellite is a human-built object that has been placed into _______ around Earth or another body.

Examples of artificial satellites and what they do:

| Satellite type | What it does | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| GPS satellites | Determine precise location by timing signals from multiple satellites | Navigation, emergency response, surveying |
| Weather satellites | Image Earth's atmosphere; track storms and cloud patterns | Weather forecasting; disaster warning |
| Communication satellites | Relay phone, internet, and TV signals across long distances | Global communication |
| Scientific satellites | Collect data for research (like Ex-Alta 1) | Atmospheric science, Earth observation |

Natural and artificial satellites are different in origin but similar in _______ — both stay in orbit because of the same physics.

> *CS Connection — satellites as designed systems vs. natural systems:*
> GPS and weather satellites are human-designed systems with a specific purpose. Earth's Moon is a natural system — it formed from a collision between early Earth and another body billions of years ago. Both orbit Earth. The key difference is _______ — natural satellites arise from physical processes; artificial satellites are engineered to solve a problem. Recognizing the difference between a designed system and a natural system is a core idea in science and in computer science.

---

**How does a satellite stay in orbit?**

Satellites do not need engines running constantly to stay in orbit. Here is the key idea:

A satellite moves _______ so fast that as it falls toward Earth, Earth's surface _______ away beneath it at the same rate. The result is that it keeps falling in a _______.

Think of it this way: if you threw a ball perfectly horizontally, it would curve down and hit the ground. Throw it faster — it travels farther before hitting. Throw it fast enough, and the curve of Earth falls away at the same rate the ball drops. The ball never hits the ground. That is an orbit.

Satellites do not _______ in place above Earth. They are always falling — they just keep _______ the curve of Earth.

**Science is self-correcting — the case of Pluto**

Pluto was called a planet from its discovery in 1930 until 2006. When it was reclassified, some people felt that scientists had made a mistake or were being unfair to Pluto.

This misunderstands how science works.

Scientists reclassified Pluto because they had _______ data. When powerful telescopes revealed many similar-sized icy bodies in the Kuiper Belt, it became clear that Pluto was not a unique planet — it was one of a large group of similar objects. The old definition of "planet" did not _______ this new evidence. So scientists updated the definition.

Changing a classification when evidence demands it is not failure. It is exactly what science is _______ to do.

> *Scientific Methods — self-correction:* Science makes progress by being willing to update. A scientist who refuses to change their view when new evidence arrives is not doing science well. A scientist who updates their understanding when evidence demands it is. The reclassification of Pluto is a model of science working correctly: new data → better classification system → more accurate understanding of the solar system.

---

> **MISCONCEPTION**
>
> *"Pluto is still a planet — scientists just changed the rules to be unfair."*
>
> The reclassification of Pluto was not arbitrary. By the early 2000s, astronomers had discovered dozens of similarly sized icy objects beyond Neptune. If Pluto was a planet, so were all of them — which would have meant adding many new planets to the list. Instead, the International Astronomical Union created a clear three-condition definition and applied it consistently. Pluto does not meet condition 3 (clearing its orbital neighbourhood). Updating a classification when new evidence changes the picture is a sign of healthy science — not unfairness to a rock.
