## Unit 1: Matter
**Guiding question:** How do particles change when matter is heated or cooled?

### Big Ideas
- All matter is made of tiny particles. Particles always move.
- Heating makes particles move faster and spread apart.
- Matter can change state. The particles stay the same — only their arrangement changes.
- Water is a special case. Ice is less dense than liquid water.

### Key Vocabulary
| Word | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Matter** | Anything with mass and volume | Steel railway tracks near Lethbridge |
| **Particle** | Tiny unit that makes up matter | Too small to see — millions in one rail |
| **Temperature** | Measure of how fast particles move | −30°C in a Lethbridge winter |
| **Phase change** | Matter moves from one state to another | Ice melting on the tracks in spring |
| **Melting** | Solid becomes liquid (heating) | Ice on the rail joint melts in April |
| **Evaporation** | Liquid becomes gas (heating) | Puddles on the track dry up |
| **Expansion** | Material grows as particles spread | Rail gets longer in summer heat |
| **Contraction** | Material shrinks as particles pull together | Rail shortens in winter cold |
| **Density** | Mass packed into a given volume | Ice is less dense than liquid water |
| **Expansion joint** | Gap left so material can expand safely | Gaps between rail sections |
| **Elastic limit** | Max force before shape change is permanent | Rail steel bends if force is too great |

---

### The Particle Model
**Matter** is anything with mass and volume.
> 📌 **matter** — has mass, takes up space

The **particle model** says four things. All matter is made of tiny **particles**. There is space between particles. Particles always move. Temperature changes how fast they move.
> 📌 **particle model** — explains how matter behaves

When matter heats up, particles move faster. They push apart. The material takes up more space. When matter cools, particles slow down. They pull closer. The material shrinks.

[SVG: cold particles packed close in a 3×3 grid on the left, hot particles spread apart with motion lines on the right]

The steel railway tracks near Lethbridge get longer in summer heat. They get shorter in January cold. Engineers leave gaps between rail sections — called **expansion joints** — so the steel can expand without buckling.
> 📌 **expansion joint** — gap that lets material expand

> ⚠️ **Watch out:** "A particle is just a tiny piece of steel."
> A particle is not a mini version of the material. Particles have empty space between them. That space is what allows matter to expand and contract.

---

### States of Matter
Matter has three states: solid, liquid, and gas.

| State | Particle spacing | Particle movement | Shape | Volume |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Solid** | Tightly packed | Vibrate in place | Fixed | Fixed |
| **Liquid** | Close, not fixed | Slide past each other | Takes container shape | Fixed |
| **Gas** | Far apart | Fast and random | Fills container | Changes |

[SVG: three-panel diagram showing solid particles in a rigid grid, liquid particles loosely grouped, gas particles scattered far apart]

The six **phase changes** are:

[SVG: phase change arrows between SOLID, LIQUID, and GAS — melting, freezing, evaporation, condensation, sublimation, deposition]

When matter changes state, **mass is conserved**. The number of particles does not change. Only their arrangement changes.

100 g of ice melts → still 100 g of liquid water.

---

### Expansion, Contraction, and the Water Anomaly
The general rule: heating causes **expansion**. Cooling causes **contraction**.
> 📌 **expansion** — material takes up more space
> 📌 **contraction** — material takes up less space

This is why Lethbridge rail sections have small gaps. In summer, steel expands and the gaps close. In winter, steel contracts and the gaps open. Without gaps, expanding steel would buckle or crack the track.

Water breaks the general rule. This is the **water anomaly**.
> 📌 **water anomaly** — ice is less dense than liquid water

Most solids are denser than their liquid form. Ice is less dense than liquid water. This is why ice floats. When water freezes, particles lock into a hexagonal crystal structure. That structure pushes particles slightly further apart — so ice takes up more space, not less.

[SVG: liquid water particles densely packed on the left versus ice particles in a spread hexagonal arrangement on the right, with density labels]

Because ice floats, it forms a layer on top of lakes in winter. Liquid water stays below. Fish survive in the liquid layer underneath.

> ⚠️ **Watch out:** "Solids are always denser than liquids."
> For most materials this is true. Water is the major exception. Ice is less dense than liquid water. Ice floats. This matters for every lake and river in Alberta every winter.

### Key Ideas — Matter
- All matter is made of particles. Particles always move. Heat makes them move faster.
- Solids have fixed shape. Liquids flow. Gases fill their container.
- Phase changes are changes in particle arrangement. Mass is always conserved.
- Most materials expand when heated and contract when cooled.
- Water is an exception. Ice is less dense than liquid water, so ice floats.

## Unit 2: Living Systems
**Guiding question:** How do living and non-living parts of an ecosystem depend on each other?

### Big Ideas
- Every ecosystem has biotic (living) and abiotic (non-living) parts. Both matter.
- Plants make their own food through photosynthesis. They do not get food from soil.
- Energy flows from producers to consumers through food webs.
- Every organism in an ecosystem depends on others. Remove one part and the whole system shifts.

### Key Vocabulary
| Word | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Ecosystem** | All living and non-living things in an area, interacting | Boreal forest near Fort McMurray |
| **Biotic** | Living part of an ecosystem | Black spruce, moose, fungi |
| **Abiotic** | Non-living part of an ecosystem | Sunlight, temperature, water, soil |
| **Photosynthesis** | Plants make food using light, water, and CO₂ | Spruce trees in the boreal forest |
| **Chlorophyll** | Green substance that captures light | What makes spruce needles green |
| **Glucose** | Sugar made by photosynthesis — the plant's food | Stored in roots and seeds |
| **Producer** | Makes its own food | Black spruce tree |
| **Consumer** | Eats producers or other consumers | Moose, wolf, hawk |
| **Decomposer** | Breaks down dead matter | Fungi on a fallen spruce log |
| **Food web** | Network of feeding relationships | Spruce → beetle → woodpecker → hawk |
| **Interdependence** | Each organism depends on others | Spruce needs insects to pollinate; insects need spruce for shelter |

---

### Biotic and Abiotic — What Makes Up an Ecosystem?
An **ecosystem** includes all the living things and all the non-living things in an area — and all the ways they interact.
> 📌 **ecosystem** — living + non-living things, interacting

**Biotic** means living.
> 📌 **biotic** — anything alive or recently alive

**Abiotic** means non-living.
> 📌 **abiotic** — non-living physical conditions

| Biotic (living) | Abiotic (non-living) |
|---|---|
| Black spruce trees | Sunlight |
| Moose, wolves, beavers | Temperature |
| Insects, birds | Water and precipitation |
| Fungi, bacteria | Soil type and nutrients |

In the boreal forest near Fort McMurray, sunlight drives photosynthesis in the spruce trees. Cold winters limit which animals can survive year-round. The soil holds nutrients returned by decomposers. Every abiotic factor shapes which organisms can live there.

**Decomposers** break down dead matter. They return nutrients to the soil.
> 📌 **decomposer** — breaks dead matter into nutrients

Without decomposers, dead organisms would pile up. Nutrients would stay locked in dead tissue. Producers would have nothing to grow from.

---

### Photosynthesis — Where Does a Plant's Food Come From?
Plants do not get their food from soil. Soil provides minerals — not food. Plants **make** their own food. This process is called **photosynthesis**.
> 📌 **photosynthesis** — plants make food using light

**The photosynthesis equation:**

[SVG: photosynthesis equation — CO2 plus H2O with a light energy arrow produces glucose plus oxygen, with source labels]

**Chlorophyll** in the leaves captures light.
> 📌 **chlorophyll** — green pigment that captures light

**Glucose** is the food the plant makes.
> 📌 **glucose** — sugar made by photosynthesis

Glucose provides energy for growth. Extra glucose is stored as starch in roots, seeds, and fruit. The black spruce trees in the boreal forest near Fort McMurray make their food from CO₂ in the air — not from the soil beneath them. Their roots absorb water and minerals, not food.

> ⚠️ **Watch out:** "Plants get their food from soil through their roots."
> Soil provides minerals. Minerals are not food. Food is an energy-containing molecule the plant makes itself. Plants make glucose using light, water, and carbon dioxide. Almost all the mass of a tree comes from CO₂ in the air — not from the ground.

---

### Food Webs and Interdependence
A **food chain** shows one path of energy flow. A **food web** shows the full network.
> 📌 **food web** — full network of who eats whom

Each arrow means "is eaten by" — energy flows in the direction of the arrow.

[SVG: boreal forest food web from Sun through Black Spruce to Moose, Wolf, Beetles, Woodpecker, Hawk, and Decomposers]

Ecosystems are systems. Every part connects to every other part. In the boreal forest, if moose populations dropped sharply, wolf populations would follow. Vegetation would change without moose grazing. Insects, birds, and soil organisms would all shift in response. This chain of changes through a food web is called a **trophic cascade**.

Animals depend on plants for food, oxygen, and shelter. Plants depend on animals for pollination, seed dispersal, CO₂, and decomposition. **Interdependence** means neither can survive without the other.
> 📌 **interdependence** — organisms need each other to survive

### Key Ideas — Living Systems
- Ecosystems have biotic (living) and abiotic (non-living) parts. Both are essential.
- Plants make food through photosynthesis. CO₂ + water + light → glucose + oxygen.
- Food webs show how energy flows from producers to consumers to decomposers.
- Removing one part of an ecosystem causes changes throughout the whole system.
- Decomposers are essential — they return nutrients to the soil for producers to use.

## Unit 3: Forces
**Guiding question:** How do forces act on structures, and what happens to materials when forces are applied?

### Big Ideas
- A force is a push or a pull. It has size (magnitude) and direction.
- Forces can be external (from outside) or internal (built up inside the material).
- Materials respond to forces with elastic deformation or plastic deformation.
- Newton's Third Law: every action force has an equal and opposite reaction force on a different object.

### Key Vocabulary
| Word | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Force** | Push or pull on an object | Load on the Riverside bridge deck |
| **Newton (N)** | Unit of force | A textbook weighs about 10 N |
| **Applied force** | Force applied directly by a person or object | Students walking across the bridge |
| **Friction** | Force opposing motion between surfaces | Boots gripping bridge planks |
| **Tension** | Internal stretching force | Bridge cables being pulled apart |
| **Compression** | Internal squeezing force | Bridge posts being pushed together |
| **Shear** | Internal sliding force | Two layers sliding past each other |
| **Torsion** | Internal twisting force | A handrail being twisted |
| **Elastic deformation** | Shape returns after force removed | Bridge cables flex under load |
| **Plastic deformation** | Shape stays changed after force removed | A bent railing stays bent |
| **Elastic limit** | Max force before deformation becomes permanent | Steel cable limit before permanent stretch |

---

### What Is a Force?
A **force** is a push or a pull. Every force has two properties:
> 📌 **force** — push or pull on an object

1. **Magnitude** — how big the force is, measured in **Newtons (N)**
2. **Direction** — which way the force acts

We draw forces as arrows. Longer arrows mean larger forces.

[SVG: force arrows on a bridge deck — 20 N pointing right on the left, 8 N pointing left on the right, net force 12 N to the right]

The timber pedestrian bridge at Riverside School carries many forces at once. Students walking across it apply a downward **applied force**. Friction between boots and planks acts horizontally. The structure must handle all of these forces at the same time.

---

### External and Internal Forces
**External forces** act on an object from outside.
> 📌 **external force** — comes from outside the object

| External force | Direction | Bridge example |
|---|---|---|
| Applied force | Direction you push or pull | Students walking on the deck |
| Friction | Opposite to motion | Boots grip the planks — good here |
| Elastic (spring) force | Returns to original shape | Flex in bridge cables under load |

[SVG: free body diagram of bridge deck with four force arrows — normal force up, weight down, friction left, applied force right]

**Internal forces** build up inside the material when external forces act on it.
> 📌 **internal force** — force acting inside the material

| Internal force | What it does | Bridge example |
|---|---|---|
| **Tension** | Pulls material apart | Suspension cables on the bridge |
| **Compression** | Squeezes material together | Vertical support posts |
| **Shear** | Slides layers past each other | Bolts connecting planks |
| **Torsion** | Twists material | A handrail twisted by a student |

> ⚠️ **Watch out:** "Friction is not a real force — it just slows things down."
> Friction is a real force. It has direction (opposite to motion) and size (measured in Newtons). On the Riverside bridge, friction keeps boots from sliding on wet planks. It is as real as any push or pull.

---

### Elasticity, Plasticity, and Newton's Third Law
When a force acts on a material, the material changes shape — this is **deformation**.
> 📌 **deformation** — change in shape due to force

**Elastic deformation:** the material returns to its original shape when the force is removed. This is a temporary change. The bridge cables at Riverside flex when students walk across — but return to their original shape when the students leave.

**Plastic deformation:** the material keeps its new shape. This is a permanent change. If a rail on the bridge deck bends sharply beyond its **elastic limit**, it stays bent.
> 📌 **elastic limit** — force above which deformation is permanent

[SVG: two-row flow diagram contrasting elastic deformation returning to original shape versus plastic deformation staying changed]

**Newton's Third Law:** When one object pushes on another, the second object pushes back with an equal force in the opposite direction.

> For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction — on a different object.

| Situation | Action force | Reaction force |
|---|---|---|
| Student steps on bridge deck | Student pushes DOWN on deck | Deck pushes UP on student |
| Student pushes on bridge railing | Student pushes railing OUTWARD | Railing pushes student INWARD |
| Boots push backward on planks | Boots push planks backward | Planks push boots forward |

Action and reaction forces do not cancel. They act on **different objects**. Each object's movement is controlled by forces acting on that object only.

> ⚠️ **Watch out:** "Action and reaction forces cancel out, so nothing moves."
> They cannot cancel. They act on different objects. The bridge deck pushes up on the student. That force acts on the student only — it is what lifts the student. The student pushing on the bridge acts on the bridge. Two separate objects, two separate effects.

### Key Ideas — Forces
- Every force has magnitude (Newtons) and direction.
- External forces come from outside. Internal forces build up inside the material.
- The four internal forces are tension, compression, shear, and torsion.
- Elastic deformation is temporary. Plastic deformation is permanent.
- Newton's Third Law: action and reaction forces are equal, opposite, and act on different objects.

## Unit 4: Climate
**Guiding question:** What causes climate to change, and how does it affect Alberta?

### Big Ideas
- Weather is short-term. Climate is a long-term pattern measured over decades.
- The greenhouse effect keeps Earth warm. Extra greenhouse gases trap extra heat.
- Climate change is happening now in Alberta — it is not only a future issue.
- Scientists use many tools to measure climate. Traditional ecological knowledge is a valid long-term data source.

### Key Vocabulary
| Word | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Weather** | Atmospheric conditions at one place, one time | Heavy rain in High River, June 2013 |
| **Climate** | Long-term pattern of weather over decades | Bow River region floods more often now |
| **Greenhouse effect** | Atmosphere traps heat near Earth's surface | Natural process that keeps Earth warm |
| **Greenhouse gas** | Gas that slows heat from leaving Earth | CO₂, methane, water vapour |
| **Fossil fuel** | Fuel formed from ancient living things | Coal, oil, natural gas |
| **Albedo** | How much sunlight a surface reflects | Snow reflects 80%; dark water reflects 6% |
| **Deforestation** | Cutting down forests | Releases stored CO₂ into the air |
| **Climate trend** | Direction of change over many decades | Alberta average temperature +1.5°C since 1950 |
| **Proxy data** | Past climate evidence from physical records | Ice cores, tree rings |
| **Traditional ecological knowledge** | Long-term Indigenous observations of ecosystems | Elder records of Bow River ice break-up |

---

### Weather vs. Climate
**Weather** is the state of the atmosphere at one place and one time.
> 📌 **weather** — atmosphere right now, one place

**Climate** is the average weather pattern over at least 30 years.
> 📌 **climate** — average pattern over decades

One cold week in High River tells you about weather. Flood data from the Bow River over 50 years tells you about climate.

[SVG: timescale comparison bar showing weather measured in days versus climate measured in decades, with usage examples]

Climate claims need long-term data. A single cold January in High River does not tell us anything about a 30-year climate trend.

> ⚠️ **Watch out:** "A cold week proves climate change isn't real."
> Weather and climate operate on different timescales. A cold week is one data point. Climate is the average of thousands of data points over decades. One cold week inside a long-term warming trend is still a warming trend.

---

### Earth's Climate Systems
The Sun drives Earth's climate. Sunlight reaches Earth. Some is reflected back to space. Some is absorbed by land, ocean, and atmosphere. Absorbed energy warms the surface. Warmth rises back toward space as heat.

The **greenhouse effect** slows that heat from leaving.
> 📌 **greenhouse effect** — atmosphere slows heat escaping Earth

[SVG: greenhouse effect — sunlight arrows descend through atmosphere layer containing CO2 and greenhouse gases, heat arrows from Earth's surface are trapped and re-emitted back down]

Without the natural greenhouse effect, Earth's average temperature would be about −18°C — far too cold for liquid water and most life.

The problem: human activities add extra greenhouse gases. Extra gases trap extra heat. Average temperatures rise.

**Albedo** is how much sunlight a surface reflects.
> 📌 **albedo** — fraction of sunlight reflected by a surface

Fresh snow reflects about 80% of sunlight. Dark ocean water reflects about 6%. When ice melts, darker surface is exposed. That darker surface absorbs more heat. More heat melts more ice. This self-reinforcing cycle is the **ice-albedo feedback loop**.

---

### Causes and Effects of Climate Change
**Natural causes** of climate change include volcanic eruptions, changes in Earth's orbit, and changes in solar output. These have always existed. They operate over thousands to millions of years.

**Human causes** are now the dominant driver of current climate change. Burning **fossil fuels** releases CO₂ stored underground for millions of years — very quickly.
> 📌 **fossil fuel** — ancient carbon stored underground

**Deforestation** releases stored CO₂ and removes trees that would absorb future CO₂. Industrial agriculture adds methane. CO₂ levels today (~420 ppm) are higher than at any point in the past 800,000 years of natural cycles.

**Effects already measured in Alberta:**

| Change | What we observe |
|---|---|
| Average temperature | Up ~1.5°C since 1950 |
| Spring | Arrives earlier — rivers ice-free sooner |
| Wildfire season | Starts earlier, ends later |
| Mountain glaciers | Shrinking — less summer meltwater for rivers |
| Flooding | High River 2013 — extreme rainfall events increasing |
| FNMI ice roads | Fewer weeks of safe travel each year |

The 2013 High River flood caused over $6 billion in damage. A warmer atmosphere holds more moisture. More moisture means more intense rainfall events when they occur.

Northern Alberta First Nations communities depend on ice roads built on frozen lakes and rivers. As winters warm, ice roads form later and melt earlier. Some no longer form at all. Communities face higher costs and supply challenges.

**Measuring climate:** Scientists use weather stations, satellites, ice cores (trap ancient air bubbles — records going back 800,000 years), tree rings, and ocean buoys.

Traditional ecological knowledge (TEK) from Indigenous Elders often covers longer time periods than instrument records. Elders along the Bow River have documented spring ice break-up timing across generations. That record shows break-up now happens about two weeks earlier than 40 years ago. TEK is systematic long-term observation — it is a valid scientific data source.

### Key Ideas — Climate
- Weather is short-term. Climate is a decades-long average. They need different kinds of evidence.
- The greenhouse effect is natural. Extra greenhouse gases from human activity trap extra heat.
- Human causes (fossil fuels, deforestation) are now the main driver of current climate change.
- Alberta shows measurable effects now: warmer temperatures, longer wildfire seasons, shrinking glaciers, more extreme flooding.
- Scientists and Indigenous Knowledge Keepers both gather long-term climate evidence. Together their data is stronger.

## Unit 5: Energy Resources
**Guiding question:** How do we choose energy resources wisely?

### Big Ideas
- Energy resources are renewable or non-renewable depending on how fast they are replenished.
- Some resources are used directly (before processing). Others must be converted first (after processing).
- Choosing an energy resource involves five factors. No single resource is perfect.
- Renewable does not mean clean. Every resource has trade-offs.

### Key Vocabulary
| Word | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Energy resource** | Anything used to do work or generate power | Wind, natural gas, solar |
| **Renewable** | Replenished by nature faster than we use it | Wind blowing across southern Alberta |
| **Non-renewable** | Cannot be replaced in a human lifetime | Natural gas under Alberta |
| **Before processing** | Resource used directly in natural form | Windmill turning a millstone |
| **After processing** | Resource converted before use | Wind turbine making electricity |
| **Availability** | Is the resource present in usable amounts? | Strong winds in Pincher Creek area |
| **Societal impact** | How does use affect people and communities? | Jobs created by wind farm construction |
| **Economic impact** | How much does it cost? | High install cost for solar; low running cost |
| **Environmental impact** | How does use affect the environment? | CO₂ from natural gas; land use from wind |
| **Responsible management** | Using resources wisely for the long term | Keeping options open for future generations |

---

### Renewable and Non-Renewable Resources
An **energy resource** is anything we use to do work, produce heat, or generate electricity.
> 📌 **energy resource** — source of usable energy

**Renewable** resources are replenished naturally. We do not run out.
> 📌 **renewable** — nature replaces it fast enough

**Non-renewable** resources took millions of years to form. Once used, they are gone.
> 📌 **non-renewable** — cannot be replaced in a human lifetime

| Renewable | Non-Renewable |
|---|---|
| Wind | Coal |
| Solar | Oil (petroleum) |
| Hydro (moving water) | Natural gas |
| Biomass (plant/animal material) | Uranium |

The small Alberta town in our scenario is choosing between natural gas (non-renewable), wind (renewable), and solar (renewable). Alberta has large natural gas deposits and strong southern winds. Both matter when making this decision.

> ⚠️ **Watch out:** "Renewable = clean."
> Renewable means it will not run out. It does not mean harmless. Large hydro dams flood ecosystems. Wind turbines require land and affect bird migration. Solar panel manufacturing uses toxic materials. Renewable and clean are separate ideas.

---

### Before and After Processing
Some resources are used **before processing** — directly in their natural form.
Some resources are used **after processing** — converted into another form first.

This has nothing to do with renewable vs. non-renewable. A resource can be renewable and still need processing.

| Resource | Before processing | After processing |
|---|---|---|
| Wind | Windmill grinds grain using wind directly | Wind turbine converts wind to electricity |
| Solar | South-facing window heats a room directly | PV cell converts sunlight to electricity |
| Wood | Burning logs heats a room | Wood converted to charcoal — burns hotter |
| Crude oil | Not used directly | Refined into gasoline, diesel, fuel |

For the Alberta town in our scenario: natural gas arrives through a pipeline — it is used after processing (refinement and compression). Wind can be used directly (mechanical windmill) or after processing (turbine generating electricity). Solar panels in the town would convert sunlight to electricity — after processing.

---

### Selecting Energy Resources — Five Factors
Choosing an energy resource requires weighing **five factors**.

**1. Availability** — Is the resource present in usable amounts, where and when needed?
**2. Societal impact** — How does use affect people, jobs, and communities?
**3. Economic impact** — What does it cost to build, maintain, and run?
**4. Environmental impact** — How does it affect air, land, water, and wildlife?
**5. Responsible management** — Will it still be available and affordable in 50 years?

**Applied to our Alberta town:**

| Factor | Natural gas | Wind | Solar |
|---|---|---|---|
| Availability | Pipeline already connects the town | Strong winds year-round | Good summer sun; limited winter daylight |
| Societal impact | Existing jobs; some air quality concerns | New industry jobs; some noise near turbines | Minimal disruption; quiet |
| Economic impact | Low install cost; rising long-term fuel costs | High install cost; very low running cost | High install cost; very low running cost |
| Environmental impact | CO₂ and methane emissions | Land use; bird migration concern | Panel manufacturing uses toxic materials |
| Responsible management | Non-renewable — supply will eventually decline | Renewable indefinitely | Renewable indefinitely |

No single option is perfect. Every choice involves trade-offs. The town must weigh all five factors using evidence.

Indigenous land stewardship principles say land and resources are held in trust for future generations — not simply extracted for present-day benefit. This aligns directly with the responsible management factor.

### Key Ideas — Energy Resources
- Renewable resources are replenished naturally. Non-renewable resources cannot be replaced in a human lifetime.
- Resources can be used before processing (directly) or after processing (converted first).
- These two classifications are independent — renewable does not mean before processing.
- Five factors guide energy decisions: availability, societal impact, economic impact, environmental impact, and responsible management.
- No resource is perfect. Every choice involves trade-offs.

## Unit 6: Space
**Guiding question:** In what ways can the solar system be explored?

### Big Ideas
- The solar system contains many types of objects. Stars emit light. Planets and moons reflect it.
- The solar system is almost entirely empty space. Textbook diagrams are not to scale.
- Different exploration tools answer different questions. No single tool does everything.
- Science updates its classifications when new evidence arrives. Pluto's reclassification is an example.

### Key Vocabulary
| Word | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Star** | Massive gas body producing its own light | The Sun |
| **Planet** | Large body orbiting the Sun; clears its orbit | Earth, Jupiter |
| **Dwarf planet** | Orbits the Sun; round; does NOT clear its orbit | Pluto |
| **Moon** | Natural satellite orbiting a planet | Earth's Moon; Titan (Saturn) |
| **Asteroid** | Rocky body smaller than a planet | Objects in the asteroid belt |
| **Comet** | Icy body with a long elliptical orbit | Develops a tail near the Sun |
| **Emit** | Produce and send out light | The Sun emits light |
| **Reflect** | Bounce back light from another source | Planets reflect sunlight |
| **Natural satellite** | Body orbiting another body — formed naturally | Earth's Moon |
| **Artificial satellite** | Human-built object placed into orbit | Ex-Alta 1 (University of Alberta) |
| **Orbit** | Curved path of one body around another | Ex-Alta 1 orbits Earth |
| **Microgravity** | Near-weightlessness felt in orbit | ISS astronauts float |

---

### Solar System Objects and Scale
The solar system is the Sun and everything that orbits it.

**The eight planets (in order from the Sun):**
[SVG: eight planets in order from the Sun — Mercury through Neptune with sized coloured circles and Saturn's ring, with mnemonic text]

Mnemonic: **M**y **V**ery **E**xcellent **M**other **J**ust **S**erved **U**s **N**achos

Inner planets (Mercury–Mars) are small and rocky. Outer planets (Jupiter–Neptune) are large and made mostly of gas.

**Solar system objects — emit or reflect?**

| Object | Emit or reflect light? | Key feature |
|---|---|---|
| Star (Sun) | EMITS | Produces light through nuclear fusion |
| Planet | REFLECTS | Reflects sunlight — not self-luminous |
| Dwarf planet (Pluto) | REFLECTS | Round, orbits Sun, does not clear its orbit |
| Moon | REFLECTS | Natural satellite of a planet |
| Asteroid | REFLECTS | Rocky; most orbit between Mars and Jupiter |
| Comet | REFLECTS | Develops a glowing tail near the Sun |

The **Sun emits** light. Everything else **reflects** it. When you see Venus in the night sky, you are seeing reflected sunlight — not light Venus generates itself.

**Scale:** Textbook diagrams of the solar system are not to scale.

> If the Sun were a basketball (24 cm across), Earth would be a pinhead about 30 metres away. Neptune would be about 1 kilometre away.

The solar system is almost entirely empty space. A true-to-scale diagram would be mostly blank.

> ⚠️ **Watch out:** "Planets produce their own light, like stars."
> Planets do not produce light. They reflect sunlight. A star is massive enough for nuclear fusion — that is what generates light. Planets are not. This is a fundamental difference between stars and planets.

---

### Exploration Technologies
We explore space using different tools. Each tool answers a different kind of question.

| Tool | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Optical telescope | Collects visible light to observe distant objects | Ground-based observatories |
| Radio telescope | Detects radio waves — sees through dust and gas | Studying distant galaxies |
| Space-based telescope | Above the atmosphere — sharper images | Hubble; James Webb |
| Space probe | Travels directly to other bodies; no crew | Voyager 1 (now beyond the solar system) |
| Rover | Lands and moves on a surface; collects samples | Curiosity and Perseverance on Mars |
| Crewed spacecraft | Carries humans; allows flexible direct work | Apollo missions; ISS |
| Computer model | Simulates conditions we cannot visit | Modelling a star's interior |

**Ex-Alta 1** is a small satellite (CubeSat) built by students at the University of Alberta. It orbits Earth and collects data on the upper atmosphere — specifically on space weather events that affect Earth's magnetic field. Ex-Alta 1 is a **computational artifact**: a human-designed system that extends scientific sensing into a place humans cannot go directly.

### Natural vs. Artificial Satellites, and Living in Space
A **natural satellite** orbits another body — formed through natural processes.
> 📌 **natural satellite** — formed naturally, orbits another body

An **artificial satellite** is human-built and placed into orbit.
> 📌 **artificial satellite** — designed and launched by humans

| Artificial satellite type | What it does |
|---|---|
| GPS satellites | Determine location using signal timing |
| Weather satellites | Image Earth's atmosphere; track storms |
| Communication satellites | Relay phone, internet, and TV signals |
| Scientific satellites | Collect research data (Ex-Alta 1) |

Satellites stay in orbit because they move sideways so fast that as they fall toward Earth, Earth's surface curves away beneath them at the same rate. They are always falling — always missing the ground.

**Living in space — challenges:**

| Challenge | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| No breathable air | Space is a vacuum — all air must be supplied |
| Radiation | Earth's atmosphere normally protects us |
| Microgravity | Muscles weaken and bones lose density over time |
| Extreme temperature swings | From +120°C in sunlight to −150°C in shadow |
| Food and water | All must be carried or recycled on board |

**Pluto's reclassification — science updating itself:**

Pluto was called a planet from 1930 until 2006. When astronomers discovered many similar-sized icy objects in the Kuiper Belt, it became clear Pluto was one of a large group — not a unique planet. Scientists updated the definition of planet to three conditions: orbits the Sun, is round, and has cleared its orbital neighbourhood. Pluto meets the first two but not the third. So it was reclassified as a dwarf planet.

This is not a mistake. This is science working correctly. New evidence → better classification → more accurate understanding.

> ⚠️ **Watch out:** "Scientists changed the rules to be unfair to Pluto."
> The reclassification was based on new evidence — dozens of similar-sized icy objects found beyond Neptune. If Pluto were a planet, so would all of them. The new definition is more precise and applies consistently. Updating a classification when evidence demands it is exactly what science is supposed to do.

### Key Ideas — Space
- Stars emit light. Planets, moons, and asteroids reflect light from the Sun.
- The solar system is almost entirely empty space. Textbook diagrams are not to scale.
- Different exploration tools (telescopes, probes, rovers, models) answer different questions.
- Ex-Alta 1 is a student-built CubeSat that gathers data on Earth's upper atmosphere.
- Science updates its classifications when evidence changes. Pluto's reclassification is a healthy example.
