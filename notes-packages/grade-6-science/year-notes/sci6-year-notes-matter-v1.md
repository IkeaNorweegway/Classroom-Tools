## Section 1: The Particle Model

### In this section you will learn:
- What matter is made of
- The four ideas of the particle model
- How temperature changes particle movement
- How this explains real-world observations

---

### What is matter?

**Matter** is anything that has mass and takes up space.

> 📌 **matter** — anything with mass and volume

The steel railway tracks near Lethbridge are matter. The air around them is matter. The water in the rock cracks beside the tracks is matter. Everything you can touch or weigh is matter.

---

### The particle model

Scientists use a model to explain matter. It is called the **particle model of matter**.

> 📌 **particle model** — a simple model of how matter behaves

The particle model has four key ideas:

**Idea 1:** All matter is made of tiny **particles**.

> 📌 **particle** — a tiny building block of matter, too small to see

The steel rail is made of particles. You cannot see them. They are far too small. But they are real.

**Idea 2:** There is **space** between particles.

Particles are not pressed together with zero space. There are gaps between them. This is important. The space is what allows matter to expand and contract.

**Idea 3:** Particles are always **moving**.

They never stop completely. Even in a cold steel rail on a winter night, the particles are still moving. They just move slowly.

**Idea 4:** Temperature changes how **fast** particles move.

When the rail gets hotter, the particles move faster. When it gets colder, they slow down.

---

### Temperature and particle movement

**Temperature** measures how fast particles move on average.

> 📌 **temperature** — measure of average particle speed

Higher temperature = faster particles.
Lower temperature = slower particles.

The railway worker near Lethbridge feels the rail in summer. It is hot. The particles inside are moving fast. She feels the rail in January. It is cold. The particles are moving slowly.

---

### Particle diagrams — the steel rail

```
COLD RAIL (winter, -20°C)          HOT RAIL (summer, +35°C)

  o  o  o                            o       o
    o  o  o                      o       o
  o  o  o                            o       o
    o  o                         o       o
  o  o  o                            o      o

Particles: slow, closer together    Particles: fast, further apart
```

Notice: the particles in the hot rail are further apart. The same number of particles takes up more space. The rail gets longer.

This is why railway engineers leave small gaps between rail sections. The gaps allow room for expansion in summer heat. You will learn more about this in Section 4.

---

> ⚠️ **Common mistake:** "A particle is just a tiny piece of the material — like a tiny steel shaving."
>
> This is not correct. A particle is not a mini version of the steel. Particles are separate units. There is empty space between them. That space is real. It is what allows the rail to expand and contract with temperature.

---

### Key ideas — Section 1

- All matter is made of tiny particles that are too small to see.
- There is space between particles. The space is real.
- Particles are always moving. They never fully stop.
- Higher temperature means faster particles and more space between them.
- Lower temperature means slower particles and less space between them.

## Section 2: States of Matter and Phase Changes

### In this section you will learn:
- How particles are arranged in solids, liquids, and gases
- The names of the six phase changes
- Why mass stays the same during a phase change
- How water near the railway tracks changes state with the seasons

---

### The three states of matter

The particle model explains the three **states of matter**.

> 📌 **states of matter** — the three forms matter can take: solid, liquid, gas

What makes them different is how particles are arranged and how fast they move.

---

### Solid

A **solid** has a fixed shape and a fixed volume.

> 📌 **solid** — particles packed tightly, fixed shape

The steel rail is a solid. Its particles are packed close together. They sit in a regular arrangement — like a grid. They vibrate back and forth. They do not move past each other. That is why the rail keeps its shape.

```
  SOLID — steel rail particles:

  | o | o | o | o |
  | o | o | o | o |
  | o | o | o | o |
  | o | o | o | o |

  Tightly packed. Regular rows. Particles vibrate but stay in place.
```

---

### Liquid

A **liquid** has a fixed volume but no fixed shape. It takes the shape of its container.

> 📌 **liquid** — particles close but flowing, no fixed shape

Water in the rock cracks beside the tracks is a liquid in spring and summer. Its particles are close together but not in a fixed arrangement. They slide past each other. That is why water flows.

```
  LIQUID — water in rock crack:

      o  o  o
    o    o  o
      o  o    o
    o    o  o

  Close together. Random arrangement. Particles flow past each other.
```

---

### Gas

A **gas** has no fixed shape and no fixed volume. It fills all available space.

> 📌 **gas** — particles far apart, fast-moving, fill any container

Water vapour in the air is a gas. Its particles are far apart. They move fast and in random directions. They fill whatever space is around them.

```
  GAS — water vapour in the air above the tracks:

  o               o
        o
             o
     o                o
         o       o

  Far apart. Fast and random. Fill the full space.
```

---

### States of matter — comparison table

| Property | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Particle spacing | Tightly packed | Close together | Far apart |
| Particle movement | Vibrate in place | Slide past each other | Fast and random |
| Fixed shape? | Yes | No — takes container shape | No — fills all space |
| Fixed volume? | Yes | Yes | No — can be compressed |

---

### Phase changes

A **phase change** happens when matter changes state.

> 📌 **phase change** — matter changing from one state to another

Temperature drives phase changes. When particles get enough energy, they can break free from their arrangement and move into a new state.

The six phase changes have names:

| Starting state | Ending state | Direction | Name |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solid | Liquid | Heating | **Melting** |
| Liquid | Solid | Cooling | **Freezing** |
| Liquid | Gas | Heating | **Evaporation** |
| Gas | Liquid | Cooling | **Condensation** |
| Solid | Gas | Heating (fast) | **Sublimation** |
| Gas | Solid | Cooling (fast) | **Deposition** |

---

### Phase change diagram

```
                        SUBLIMATION
              ┌──────────────────────────────┐
              │  (solid → gas, heating)      │
              ↑                              ↓ DEPOSITION (gas → solid)
              │                              │
           SOLID  ──melting──►  LIQUID  ──evaporation──►  GAS
                  ◄─freezing─           ◄─condensation─

     Arrows going right and up = HEATING
     Arrows going left and down = COOLING
```

The water in the rock cracks near the tracks goes through phase changes every year. In winter, it **freezes** (liquid to solid). In spring, it **melts** (solid to liquid). On warm summer days, some of it **evaporates** (liquid to gas). Dew on the rails in early morning is **condensation** (gas to liquid).

---

### Mass is conserved during phase changes

This is one of the most important ideas in this unit.

When water in the rock crack freezes, the water does not disappear. When it melts, it does not gain mass from somewhere. The particles are the same particles. They just rearrange.

**100 g of water freezes → still 100 g of ice.**
**100 g of ice melts → still 100 g of liquid water.**

The **volume** does change. Ice takes up more space than the same mass of water. But the mass stays the same.

> ⚠️ **Common mistake:** "When ice melts, some mass disappears — there is less water than ice."
>
> This is not correct. Mass does not disappear. The particles are all still there. Only their arrangement changes. The mass before and after a phase change is always the same. This is called conservation of mass.

---

### Key ideas — Section 2

- Solids have tightly packed, regularly arranged particles that vibrate in place.
- Liquids have close particles that flow past each other. No fixed shape.
- Gases have widely spaced, fast-moving particles. No fixed shape or volume.
- Phase changes happen when temperature changes enough to change particle arrangement.
- Mass is always conserved during a phase change. Only volume and arrangement change.
- The six phase changes: melting, freezing, evaporation, condensation, sublimation, deposition.

## Section 3: Thermometers and Temperature

### In this section you will learn:
- How the Celsius scale works and what its reference points are
- How a liquid thermometer uses particle behaviour to measure temperature
- How a railway worker uses a thermometer correctly and safely

---

### The Celsius scale

Scientists measure temperature in **degrees Celsius** (°C).

> 📌 **Celsius scale** — temperature scale based on water's phase changes

The Celsius scale uses two **reference points** — temperatures that always stay the same.

> 📌 **reference point** — a fixed temperature used to set up a scale

| Reference point | Temperature |
|---|---|
| Water freezes (liquid → solid) | 0°C |
| Water boils (liquid → gas) | 100°C |
| Comfortable room temperature | ~20–22°C |
| Normal human body temperature | ~37°C |
| Cold winter day near Lethbridge | ~−20°C |
| Hot summer day near the tracks | ~35°C |

These reference points were chosen because they are **reliable** — water always freezes and boils at the same temperatures under the same conditions. Any lab in the world can reproduce them.

The railway worker near Lethbridge measures air temperature near the tracks. She reads 38°C in August — well above comfortable. She reads −22°C in January. The scale lets her compare readings across seasons.

---

### How a liquid thermometer works

A traditional **thermometer** has liquid sealed inside a thin glass tube.

> 📌 **thermometer** — tool for measuring temperature using liquid expansion

The liquid is usually coloured alcohol. Here is what happens when temperature changes:

**When temperature goes up:**
- Liquid particles gain energy. They move faster.
- Faster particles push further apart. The liquid **expands**.
- The liquid rises up the tube. The reading goes up.

**When temperature goes down:**
- Liquid particles lose energy. They slow down.
- Slower particles pull closer together. The liquid **contracts**.
- The liquid falls down the tube. The reading goes down.

The thermometer works because of **thermal expansion** — the liquid gets bigger when it heats up.

> 📌 **thermal expansion** — matter getting bigger as temperature rises

---

### Thermometer diagram — cold and hot

```
  COLD thermometer (−20°C):     HOT thermometer (+38°C):
  |   |                          |   |
  |   |                          | ■ | ← liquid level (higher)
  | ■ | ← liquid level           | ■ |
  | ■ |                          | ■ |
  | ■ |                          | ■ |
  | ■ |                          | ■ |
  |___|                          | ■ |
                                 |___|

  Particles: slow, close          Particles: fast, spread apart
  Volume: smaller                 Volume: larger — liquid rises
```

The railway worker holds the thermometer still in the air. She waits for the reading to stop moving. Then she reads the level at eye height. This is how to use a thermometer correctly.

---

### Thermometer safety — four rules

1. **Never squeeze the thermometer.** Glass breaks under pressure.
2. **Never use it to stir.** It is not a stirring rod. It will crack.
3. **Read at eye level.** Looking from above or below gives the wrong reading. This error is called parallax.
4. **Wait for it to settle.** The liquid needs time to reach the temperature of what it is measuring.

If a thermometer breaks, do not touch the glass or liquid. Tell the teacher right away.

---

### The thermometer and the particle model

The thermometer is a direct application of the particle model. Faster particles = bigger volume = liquid rises. Slower particles = smaller volume = liquid falls. The scale on the glass connects the liquid level to a temperature number. The particle model predicts exactly how the thermometer will behave.

---

### Key ideas — Section 3

- Temperature is measured in degrees Celsius (°C) in science.
- The Celsius scale has two reference points: 0°C (water freezes) and 100°C (water boils).
- A liquid thermometer works because the liquid expands when heated and contracts when cooled.
- This is direct evidence of the particle model — particles move faster at higher temperatures and push further apart.
- Always read thermometers at eye level. Always wait for the reading to settle.

## Section 4: Expansion, Contraction, and the Water Anomaly

### In this section you will learn:
- The general rule for how matter behaves when heated or cooled
- How expansion joints in railway tracks solve a real engineering problem
- Why water breaks the general rule — and why this matters
- How the particle model explains the water anomaly

---

### The general rule: expansion and contraction

For almost every material, the rule is simple:

**Heating → particles move faster → particles push further apart → material gets bigger → EXPANSION**

**Cooling → particles slow down → particles pull closer → material gets smaller → CONTRACTION**

> 📌 **expansion** — matter getting bigger as temperature rises
> 📌 **contraction** — matter getting smaller as temperature drops

This rule applies to solids, liquids, and gases. Gases expand and contract far more than solids or liquids. But all matter follows this rule — except one important case.

---

### Expansion in the steel railway track

The steel rail near Lethbridge sits in full Alberta sun in summer. The temperature goes up. The particles in the steel move faster. They push further apart. The rail gets slightly longer.

In winter, the temperature drops to −20°C or colder. The particles slow down. They pull closer. The rail gets slightly shorter.

This is not a small effect. A 25 m steel rail section can expand by about 1.5 cm between winter and summer. That may not sound like much. But multiply it across kilometres of track, and it becomes a major engineering problem.

---

### Expansion joints — the engineering solution

Engineers left gaps between rail sections on purpose. These gaps are called **expansion joints**.

> 📌 **expansion joint** — a gap that allows materials to expand without buckling

```
  RAILWAY TRACK — viewed from above:

  [  Rail section A  ]____gap____[  Rail section B  ]____gap____[  Rail section C  ]
                      ↑                              ↑
               Expansion joint                Expansion joint

  Summer (hot): sections expand → gap gets SMALLER
  Winter (cold): sections contract → gap gets LARGER
```

The gap is sized so the rail has room to expand without pressing against the next section. Without the gap, the expanding steel would press against the next section with enormous force. The rail would buckle sideways — this is called **thermal buckling**, and it causes derailments.

Modern continuous welded rail is designed differently. It is welded into one long piece. It is installed under tension so that the steel wants to contract — but is held in place. This pre-stressing prevents both buckling in summer and cracking in winter. Engineers calculate the exact pre-stress needed for Alberta's temperature range.

The same idea applies to concrete bridges, highway sections, and even the sidewalk outside your school. All of them have expansion joints.

---

### Expansion diagram — gap size changes with season

```
  WINTER (−20°C):
  [  Rail A  ]────── GAP (larger) ──────[  Rail B  ]
              Particles: slow, pulled in

  SUMMER (+35°C):
  [  Rail A  ]── GAP (smaller) ──[  Rail B  ]
              Particles: fast, pushed apart

  If no gap:
  [  Rail A  ][pressed hard][  Rail B  ]
               ↑ Compression builds up → rail buckles
```

---

### The water anomaly

Almost every material follows the general rule. When cooled, the solid is **denser** than the liquid. Particles pull closer in a solid. Smaller volume for the same mass = higher density.

> 📌 **density** — how much mass is packed into a given volume

Water does not follow this rule. Water is called an **anomaly**.

> 📌 **anomaly** — a result that breaks the expected pattern

When water freezes, it becomes **less dense** than liquid water. Ice takes up **more space** than the same mass of liquid water. This is why ice floats.

---

### Why water is different — the crystal structure

In liquid water, particles move freely. They are close together but randomly arranged. When water freezes, the particles slow down and lock into a **hexagonal crystal structure**.

> 📌 **hexagonal crystal** — a six-sided ring arrangement that has more space inside

This ring structure actually pushes the particles **further apart** than they were in the liquid. More space between particles = same mass in a bigger volume = **lower density**.

```
  LIQUID WATER particles:            ICE particles:

      o  o  o                     o       o
    o    o   o                  o    o       o
       o   o                      o    o   o
    o   o    o                  o    o       o
                                   o       o

  Close, random flow             Hexagonal rings — MORE space
  Density: HIGHER                Density: LOWER → ice floats
```

This is not what the particle model would predict if you applied the general rule. Scientists were surprised when they first studied this carefully. The hexagonal structure explanation came **after** the observation. This is how science works — you observe, you are surprised, and then you build a better explanation.

---

### Expansion comparison — rail vs. water in rock crack

The water that seeps into rock cracks beside the tracks in summer is liquid. When winter comes, it freezes. Liquid water → ice is NOT like most phase changes. The ice takes up **more space** than the liquid water did.

The crack is full of water. The water freezes and expands. The expanding ice pushes outward against the rock walls. The rock cannot stretch. Over many freeze-thaw cycles — winter to spring, year after year — the cracks get larger. Eventually, chunks of rock break away from the cliff or embankment beside the track. This is called **freeze-thaw weathering**.

Railway maintenance crews in Alberta inspect embankments every spring for this reason. The water anomaly is not just a science curiosity. It directly causes infrastructure damage.

---

> ⚠️ **Common mistake:** "Ice is denser than liquid water — solids are always denser."
>
> This is not correct. Water is the exception. Ice is **less** dense than liquid water. That is why ice floats on the surface, not sinks. The hexagonal crystal structure that forms when water freezes pushes particles further apart than in liquid water. This is unusual — almost no other material does this.

---

### Why the water anomaly matters for life

Because ice floats, it forms at the **surface** of a lake or river. The ice layer insulates the water beneath it. The liquid water below stays at about 4°C — cold, but liquid. Fish, plants, and insects survive the winter in that liquid layer.

If ice were denser than liquid water, ice would sink. Ponds and rivers could freeze **solid** from the bottom up. Almost no aquatic life would survive Alberta winters.

The fact that ice floats is not a minor curiosity. It is the reason rivers and lakes in cold climates still have life in them.

Cree, Dene, and Métis communities across northern Alberta have observed this pattern for generations. Their knowledge of when ice forms, when it is safe to travel on, and when early melt signals broader ecological shifts is a form of long-term systematic observation. It describes the same anomalous behaviour of water from a different framework — and is now recognized as a valuable source of ecological data alongside instrument-based scientific measurement.

---

### Expansion and contraction — summary table

| Situation | What happens | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Steel rail heated (summer) | Expands — gets longer | Particles move faster, push apart |
| Steel rail cooled (winter) | Contracts — gets shorter | Particles slow, pull closer |
| Water in rock crack heated (liquid) | Expands slightly | Particle spacing increases |
| Water in rock crack cooled to 0°C and frozen | **Expands** (anomaly) | Crystal structure pushes particles further apart |
| Expansion joint in rail (summer) | Gap gets smaller | Rail sections expand toward each other |
| Expansion joint in rail (winter) | Gap gets larger | Rail sections contract away from each other |

---

### Key ideas — Section 4

- When most materials heat up, they expand. When they cool, they contract.
- This happens because particles move faster and push further apart when hot, and slow and pull closer when cold.
- Expansion joints in railway tracks leave room for the steel to expand without buckling.
- Water breaks the general rule. Ice is less dense than liquid water.
- This is because freezing water forms a hexagonal crystal structure that takes up more space.
- Ice floats because it is less dense. This protects aquatic life in winter.
- Freeze-thaw weathering near railway tracks is caused by the same water anomaly.

## Full Unit Summary

### The four big ideas of the Matter unit

**1. The particle model**
All matter is made of tiny particles. There is space between them. They always move. Hotter = faster.

Solid particles are close and vibrate. Liquid particles flow. Gas particles are far apart and fast. Phase changes happen when temperature changes particle arrangement. Mass is always conserved.
