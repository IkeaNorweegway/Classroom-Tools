# Section 1: Biotic and Abiotic Components

## In this section you will learn:
- What biotic and abiotic mean
- How to tell living things from non-living things
- The three roles living things play in an ecosystem
- Why every part of an ecosystem matters

---

## 1.1 — What Is an Ecosystem?

An **ecosystem** is a community of living and non-living things.

> 📌 **ecosystem** — living and non-living things interacting

All parts of an ecosystem work together. They affect each other.

Think of the boreal forest near Fort McMurray. It has spruce trees, wolves, insects, and moss. It also has rocks, soil, sunlight, and water. All of these parts interact.

An ecosystem can be very large. A boreal forest is an ecosystem. An ecosystem can also be small. A single pond is an ecosystem.

The key idea is this: **living and non-living things interact together.**

---

## 1.2 — Biotic: The Living Parts

**Biotic** means living, or once living.

> 📌 **biotic** — living or once-living things

Biotic things include organisms. An organism is any living thing.

**In our boreal forest near Fort McMurray:**

| Biotic thing | What it is |
|---|---|
| Spruce trees | Living plants |
| Wolves | Living animals |
| Insects | Living animals |
| Moss | Living plants |
| Fungi | Living decomposers |
| Bacteria | Living decomposers |

Fallen logs are also biotic. They were once living. They are still part of the ecosystem.

---

### The Three Roles in an Ecosystem

Every living thing plays one of three roles.

**Comparison Table: Producers, Consumers, and Decomposers**

| Role | What they do | Boreal forest example |
|---|---|---|
| **Producer** | Makes its own food using sunlight | Spruce trees, moss, grass |
| **Consumer** | Gets energy by eating other things | Insects, birds, wolves |
| **Decomposer** | Breaks down dead things | Fungi, bacteria |

> 📌 **producer** — makes food from sunlight
> 📌 **consumer** — eats other organisms for energy
> 📌 **decomposer** — breaks down dead organisms

Producers are the base of every food web. Without them, nothing else can survive.

Consumers eat producers or other consumers. A wolf eats other animals. It is a consumer. An insect eats moss. It is also a consumer.

Decomposers are different. They break down dead things. A fallen spruce tree slowly rots. Fungi and bacteria do this work. When the tree breaks down, nutrients go back into the soil. Then new plants can grow.

> ⚠️ **Common mistake:** "Decomposers are not really part of the ecosystem — they just clean up."
> This is not correct. Decomposers are essential. Without them, nutrients stay locked in dead material. Plants cannot grow without those nutrients. Remove decomposers and the whole ecosystem collapses.

---

## 1.3 — Abiotic: The Non-Living Parts

**Abiotic** means non-living.

> 📌 **abiotic** — non-living parts of an ecosystem

Abiotic things are not organisms. They have never been alive. But they still affect every living thing in the ecosystem.

**In our boreal forest near Fort McMurray:**

| Abiotic thing | How it affects living things |
|---|---|
| Sunlight | Powers photosynthesis in spruce trees and moss |
| Water | All living cells need water to survive |
| Soil | Holds nutrients and anchors plant roots |
| Rocks | Shape the land; affect drainage and temperature |
| Temperature | Sets which organisms can survive here |
| Air (CO₂) | Plants use CO₂ to make food |

---

**Boreal Forest near Fort McMurray — Sorting Practice**

| Thing | Biotic or Abiotic? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Spruce tree | Biotic | It is a living plant |
| Wolf | Biotic | It is a living animal |
| Insect | Biotic | It is a living animal |
| Moss | Biotic | It is a living plant |
| Fallen log (rotting) | Biotic | It was once a living tree |
| Rock | Abiotic | Never alive |
| Soil | Abiotic | Non-living mineral material |
| Sunlight | Abiotic | Not an organism |

| Air | Abiotic | Non-living gas mixture |

---

## 1.4 — Biodiversity

**Biodiversity** means the variety of living things in an ecosystem.

> 📌 **biodiversity** — variety of life in an area

A healthy boreal forest has high biodiversity. Many types of trees, insects, birds, and mammals live there.

When biodiversity is high, the ecosystem is more stable. If one species disappears, others can fill its role.

Abiotic factors affect biodiversity. The boreal forest near Fort McMurray has cold winters, short summers, and acidic soil. Only certain species can live there. This is why biodiversity is different in different ecosystems.

---

## Key Ideas — Section 1

> **Section 1 Summary**
>
> - An ecosystem includes all living and non-living things in an area — and their interactions.
> - **Biotic** = living or once-living. Examples: spruce trees, wolves, insects, moss, fungi.
> - **Abiotic** = non-living. Examples: sunlight, water, soil, rocks, temperature.
> - Three roles in every ecosystem: **producers** (make food), **consumers** (eat food), **decomposers** (break down dead matter and return nutrients).
> - Decomposers are essential — they recycle nutrients back into the soil.
> - **Biodiversity** = variety of life. More biodiversity = more stable ecosystem.

# Section 2: Photosynthesis

## In this section you will learn:
- Where plants get their food (not the soil)
- What goes into photosynthesis and what comes out
- Where in the plant photosynthesis happens
- Why plants are net producers of oxygen

---

## 2.1 — The Big Idea: Plants Make Their Own Food

Here is the most important idea in this unit.

**Plants do not get their food from the soil.**

Plants make their own food. They make it inside their leaves. They use light energy to do this. This process is called **photosynthesis**.

> 📌 **photosynthesis** — the process plants use to make food using light

> ⚠️ **Common mistake:** "Plants get their food from the soil through their roots."
> This is not correct. Soil gives plants minerals — not food. Food means energy. Plants make their own energy-containing food. They build it from carbon dioxide gas and water, using sunlight. The soil does not provide this.

**The evidence:** A spruce tree can grow to thousands of kilograms. The soil it grows in barely loses any mass. Where did all that mass come from? It came from CO₂ in the air — captured by photosynthesis.

---

## 2.2 — The Photosynthesis Equation

Photosynthesis has three inputs and two outputs.

**Inputs (what goes in):**
1. Carbon dioxide (CO₂) — a gas from the air
2. Water (H₂O) — absorbed through the roots
3. Light energy — from the sun

**Outputs (what comes out):**
1. Glucose — the food the plant makes
2. Oxygen — released into the air

### Photosynthesis Flow Diagram

```
          SUNLIGHT
              ↓
  CARBON DIOXIDE (from air)
              +          ─────────────────►  GLUCOSE (food for plant)
     WATER (from roots)
                         ─────────────────►  OXYGEN (released to air)
              ↑
        [Inside the green leaves of the spruce tree]
```

**In simple words:**

Carbon dioxide + water + light energy → glucose + oxygen

**In the boreal forest:** Spruce trees pull CO₂ from the air. Their roots pull water from the soil. Sunlight hits their needles. Inside the needles, glucose is made. Oxygen is released.

---

## 2.3 — Where Each Ingredient Comes From

| Ingredient | Where the plant gets it | In the boreal forest |
|---|---|---|
| **Carbon dioxide (CO₂)** | Absorbed through tiny pores in leaves | Spruce needles absorb CO₂ from forest air |
| **Water (H₂O)** | Absorbed through roots, moved up the stem | Spruce roots draw water from soil and snowmelt |
| **Light energy** | Captured by green parts of the plant | Spruce needles face upward to catch sunlight |

---

## 2.4 — Where Photosynthesis Happens

Photosynthesis happens in the **green parts** of the plant. Mostly in the **leaves** (or needles, in the case of spruce trees).

The green parts are green because of **chlorophyll**.

> 📌 **chlorophyll** — the green substance that captures light for photosynthesis

Chlorophyll captures light energy. It uses that energy to run photosynthesis. No chlorophyll = no photosynthesis.

### Plant Diagram — Where Photosynthesis Happens

```
         ╔══════════════════════════════╗
         ║  SPRUCE NEEDLE / LEAF        ║
         ║  ← GREEN = chlorophyll here  ║
         ║  ← PHOTOSYNTHESIS happens    ║
         ║     here, inside the leaf    ║
         ╚══════════════════════════════╝
                      │
                      │  stem carries water upward
                      │  stem carries glucose downward
                      │
              ════════╪════════
                      │
                 [ROOTS below]
                      │
              absorb water from soil
              ← CO₂ enters through leaf pores
              ← Sunlight hits the leaf surface
              → Oxygen exits through leaf pores
              → Glucose stays inside the plant
```

The white or yellow parts of a plant (no chlorophyll) cannot do photosynthesis. Only the green parts do this work.

---

## 2.5 — What the Plant Does with Glucose

The plant makes glucose as its food. Glucose gives the plant energy.

> 📌 **glucose** — sugar the plant makes; its source of energy

The plant uses glucose two ways:

**1. For energy right away:** The plant uses glucose to grow, repair cells, and carry out life processes.

**2. For storage:** Extra glucose is converted to **starch** and stored.

> 📌 **starch** — stored form of glucose in plants

Starch is stored in:
- Roots (example: a carrot stores starch)
- Seeds (example: a grain of wheat stores starch)
- Fruits

When animals eat plants, they are eating this stored glucose and starch. Every carbohydrate you eat started as photosynthesis.

---

## 2.6 — Photosynthesis and Oxygen

Plants release oxygen as a byproduct of photosynthesis.

**In the boreal forest:** Every spruce tree releases oxygen into the air. Wolves, insects, birds, and humans breathe this oxygen. Plants are the source of the oxygen animals depend on.

> ⚠️ **Common mistake:** "Photosynthesis and respiration cancel each other out. Plants don't really add oxygen to the air."
> This is not correct. During the day, a healthy plant makes far more oxygen than it uses. The net result is oxygen out. Earth's atmosphere is 21% oxygen. All of that came from billions of years of photosynthesis. If photosynthesis and respiration cancelled perfectly, there would be no oxygen in the air at all.

**The key point:** During daylight, plants are net oxygen producers. The oxygen they release is available for animals to breathe.

---

## 2.7 — Three Experiments That Prove Photosynthesis

Scientists have tested photosynthesis three ways. Here is what each experiment shows.

**Experiment 1 — Does light matter?**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Question** | Does a spruce seedling need light to make food? |
| **Setup** | One seedling in full light. One seedling in complete darkness. |
| **What we test** | Presence of starch in the leaves after one week |
| **Result** | The seedling in light contains starch. The dark seedling does not. |
| **Conclusion** | Light is required for photosynthesis. Without light, no food is made. |

**Experiment 2 — Does photosynthesis produce oxygen?**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Question** | Does a plant release oxygen during photosynthesis? |
| **Setup** | Aquatic plant placed under a lamp. Bubbles form on the plant. |
| **Result** | Bubbles increase when the lamp is brighter. Bubbles stop in darkness. |
| **Conclusion** | The bubbles are oxygen. Photosynthesis produces oxygen as a byproduct. |

**Experiment 3 — Do only green parts photosynthesize?**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Question** | Does chlorophyll (the green pigment) matter for photosynthesis? |
| **Setup** | A leaf with green sections and white/yellow sections. Test both for starch. |
| **Result** | Only the green sections contain starch. White sections have none. |
| **Conclusion** | Chlorophyll is required. Only green parts can run photosynthesis. |

---

## Key Ideas — Section 2

> **Section 2 Summary**
>
> - Plants make their own food through **photosynthesis**. They do not absorb food from the soil.
> - **Inputs:** carbon dioxide (from air) + water (from roots) + light energy (from sun)
> - **Outputs:** glucose (food) + oxygen (released to air)
> - Photosynthesis happens in the **green parts** of the plant — where **chlorophyll** is.
> - Glucose is used for energy or stored as **starch** in roots, seeds, and fruits.
> - Plants are **net oxygen producers** during daylight. Photosynthesis does not cancel out with respiration.
> - Three experiments confirm this: light experiment, bubble experiment, green-parts experiment.

# Section 3: Plant-Animal Relationships

## In this section you will learn:
- How animals and plants depend on each other
- How food chains and food webs work in the boreal forest
- What happens when one part of an ecosystem is removed
- How Cree and Dene traditional knowledge connects to ecosystem health

---

## 3.1 — Interdependence: More Than Just Food

**Interdependence** means that living things need each other to survive.

> 📌 **interdependence** — living things depending on each other

Most students know that animals eat plants. That is true. But it is only one layer of a much deeper relationship.

**Animals depend on plants for:**
- Food (glucose stored as starch and sugars)
- Oxygen (released during photosynthesis)
- Habitat (shelter, nesting sites, structure)
- Soil stability (plant roots hold soil in place)

**Plants depend on animals for:**
- Pollination (animals carry pollen between flowers)
- Seed dispersal (animals spread seeds to new places)
- CO₂ (animals release CO₂, which plants use in photosynthesis)
- Nutrients (when animals die, decomposers return nutrients to soil)

> ⚠️ **Common mistake:** "Animals only depend on plants for food. Everything else is independent."
> This is not correct. Animals also depend on plants for **oxygen**. Without photosynthesis, there would be no oxygen in Earth's atmosphere. Animals cannot survive without oxygen. This link goes far beyond food.

**In the boreal forest:** Wolves depend on spruce trees. Not for food — wolves do not eat trees. But wolves breathe oxygen that spruce trees release. Spruce trees use CO₂ that wolves breathe out. This cycle runs constantly.

---

## 3.2 — Food Chains in the Boreal Forest

A **food chain** shows one pathway of energy moving through an ecosystem.

> 📌 **food chain** — a single path of energy from producer to consumer

Each arrow means "is eaten by" or "provides energy to."

**Boreal forest food chain:**

```
Moss/Grass  →  Insects  →  Song Birds  →  Wolves
(producer)    (primary    (secondary     (apex
               consumer)   consumer)      predator)
```

> 📌 **producer** — makes its own food (always first in the chain)
> 📌 **primary consumer** — eats the producer
> 📌 **secondary consumer** — eats the primary consumer
> 📌 **apex predator** — top of the food chain; not eaten by others

The arrow always points toward the consumer — in the direction energy flows.

---

## 3.3 — Food Webs in the Boreal Forest

A **food web** shows the full network of feeding relationships. Real ecosystems are not simple chains. Most animals eat more than one thing.

> 📌 **food web** — the full network of feeding relationships in an ecosystem

**Boreal Forest Food Web — near Fort McMurray**

```
              [Sun]
                ↓
   [Spruce Trees / Moss / Grass]
       ↙          ↓          ↘
  [Deer]       [Insects]    [Snowshoe Hare]
      ↘           ↓          ↙
       [Birds (e.g., ravens, owls)]
              ↓       ↘
           [Wolves]   [Foxes]
              ↘          ↙
           [Decomposers: Fungi + Bacteria]
                  ↓
              [Nutrients]
                  ↓
   [Spruce Trees / Moss / Grass]  ← cycle continues
```

Decomposers are at every level. When any organism dies, decomposers break it down and return nutrients to the soil. Then producers can grow again. The cycle continues.

---

## 3.4 — What Happens When You Remove a Part?

Ecosystems are systems. Remove one part and the whole system changes.

**Case study: Wolves in the boreal forest**

Imagine wolves are removed from the boreal forest near Fort McMurray.

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| Wolves removed | Deer and snowshoe hare populations grow unchecked |
| Too many deer | They overeat moss, grass, and young spruce seedlings |
| Vegetation damaged | Riverbanks erode — roots no longer hold the soil |
| Bird habitat lost | Fewer trees and shrubs = fewer nesting sites |
| Insect populations shift | Less vegetation means fewer insects |
| Whole ecosystem changes | Biodiversity drops across many species |

This is called a **trophic cascade** — a change at one level of the food web that spreads through the whole system.

> 📌 **trophic cascade** — one change in a food web ripples through many levels

When wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone in 1995, vegetation returned. Rivers stabilised. Bird populations recovered. This is direct evidence that apex predators matter to whole ecosystems.

---

## 3.5 — Cree and Dene Sacred Plants and Ecosystem Knowledge

Indigenous peoples of this region — including Cree and Dene communities near Fort McMurray — have observed and cared for this boreal forest for thousands of years. This is **traditional ecological knowledge**.

> 📌 **traditional ecological knowledge** — knowledge about ecosystems passed down through generations

This knowledge is a valid scientific source. Knowledge Keepers track plant health. They notice changes across generations. This long-term view is something scientific instruments cannot easily replicate.

**Sacred plants with ecological roles in the boreal forest:**

| Sacred plant | Ecological role | Cultural significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Sweetgrass** | Stabilises wetland soil near streams and bogs; indicator of wetland health | Braided and used in ceremony; symbol of connection and kindness |
| **Cedar** | Large trees provide habitat for birds and insects; long-lived carbon sink; rot-resistant | Used in medicine and ceremony; protective significance |
| **Sage** | Drought-resistant ground cover; provides insect habitat; nitrogen-fixing | Used in smudging and ceremony; cleansing and healing |
| **Tobacco** | Fast-growing soil pioneer; attracts pollinators | Offered as a gift of respect; used in prayer |

When Cree and Dene Knowledge Keepers notice that sweetgrass is declining in a wetland area, this tells scientists something important: the wetland ecosystem is stressed. The health of sacred plants is an indicator of whole-ecosystem health.

This knowledge has been built over far longer periods than most scientific data collection. It is a complementary and essential source of information about the boreal forest.

---

## 3.6 — Humans and Plants

Humans are animals. We depend on plants for everything animals depend on plants for — and more.

**Beyond food and oxygen, humans rely on plants for:**

| Human need | Plant connection |
|---|---|
| Clothing | Cotton, linen, and other plant fibres |
| Building materials | Wood, bamboo, plant-based composites |
| Medicine | Many medicines come from plant compounds |
| Climate regulation | Forests absorb CO₂ and regulate regional rainfall |
| Soil stability | Tree roots prevent erosion of farmland and riverbanks |

In the boreal forest near Fort McMurray, spruce trees absorb CO₂ from the atmosphere. This slows the buildup of greenhouse gases. A healthy boreal forest benefits all of Alberta — and the whole planet.

> ⚠️ **Common mistake 1:** "Plants get their food from the soil."

# Unit Summary — The Big Picture

The boreal forest near Fort McMurray is a complete ecosystem. Every part connects to every other part.

**The flow of matter and energy:**

```
SUNLIGHT
    ↓
SPRUCE TREES / MOSS (producers)
— use CO₂ + water + light → glucose + oxygen
    ↓ (energy moves up the food web)
INSECTS / DEER / SNOWSHOE HARE (primary consumers)
    ↓
BIRDS / FOXES (secondary consumers)
    ↓
WOLVES (apex predators)
    ↓
DECOMPOSERS (fungi, bacteria)
— break down all dead material
    ↓
NUTRIENTS return to soil
    ↓
SPRUCE TREES / MOSS (cycle continues)
```

**Non-living parts that make this possible:**

Sunlight → powers photosynthesis
Water → carried into roots, used in photosynthesis
Soil → holds nutrients, anchors roots
CO₂ → raw material for photosynthesis
Temperature → determines which species survive

**The human and cultural layer:**

Cree and Dene communities have cared for this forest for thousands of years. Their knowledge of sacred plants — sweetgrass, cedar, sage, tobacco — tracks ecosystem health across generations. This traditional knowledge and scientific data together give the most complete picture of how this ecosystem works and how it is changing.
