# Section 1: What Is a Force?

## In this section you will learn:
- What a force is and what it does
- That forces have size and direction
- How to read a force arrow
- What a force diagram shows

---

## 1.1 Forces Push or Pull

A **force** is a push or a pull.

> 📌 **force** — a push or a pull on something

Forces act on objects. A force can make an object move. It can slow an object down. It can change the direction of an object. It can change the shape of an object.

**Bridge example:** A student walks onto the deck of the timber bridge at Riverside School. The student's feet push down on the wooden planks. That push is a force. It acts on the bridge deck.

---

## 1.2 Forces Have Two Properties

Every force has two things you must know.

**1. Magnitude** — the size or strength of the force.

> 📌 **magnitude** — how big or strong a force is

We measure magnitude in **Newtons**.

> 📌 **Newton (N)** — the unit for measuring force

A gentle push might be 5 N. A hard shove might be 50 N. A student weighing 400 N walks across the bridge.

**2. Direction** — the way the force is pointing.

> 📌 **direction** — which way a force acts

A 10 N push to the right is different from a 10 N push to the left. The size is the same. The direction is different. Both properties matter every time.

---

## 1.3 Force Arrows

We draw forces as arrows. The arrow shows both properties at once.

- The **direction the arrow points** shows the direction of the force.
- The **length of the arrow** shows the magnitude. A longer arrow means a larger force.

**Reading a force arrow:**

```
     ————————►  20 N (pushing right)

     ◄————————  20 N (pushing left)

     ————►  8 N (pushing right, smaller force — shorter arrow)
```

The 20 N arrow is longer than the 8 N arrow. The longer arrow shows the bigger force.

---

## 1.4 Force Diagrams

A **force diagram** (also called a free body diagram) shows all the forces acting on one object.

> 📌 **force diagram** — a drawing showing all forces on one object

It strips away everything that is not needed. No colour. No texture. No shape details. Just the object and its force arrows.

**Bridge example — student standing on the bridge:**

```
                  ↑ Normal force (500 N, upward)
                  |
    ══════════════╪══════════════  (bridge deck)
                  |
                  ↓ Applied force (500 N, downward)
              [STUDENT]
```

This is an abstraction. It keeps only what matters: direction and size.

> *Connection to Computer Science:* Force diagrams are **abstractions**. They keep only the important information. They remove everything else. Programmers do the same thing when they design a system — they model only what matters.

---

## 1.5 Net Force

When more than one force acts on the same object, we find the **net force**.

> 📌 **net force** — the overall combined force on an object

**Example — two forces on the bridge deck:**

```
     20 N →   [BRIDGE DECK]   ← 8 N
```

The 20 N force pushes right. The 8 N force pushes left. They are in opposite directions. We subtract the smaller from the larger. The net force is 12 N to the right.

If the net force is zero, the object does not accelerate. It stays still or keeps moving at the same speed.

---

> ⚠️ **Common mistake:** "If an object is not moving, there are no forces on it."
> This is not correct. A still object can have many forces acting on it. When all the forces balance, the net force is zero. The object does not move. Forces are still there.

---

## Key Ideas — Section 1

| Idea | What it means |
|---|---|
| A force | A push or a pull |
| Magnitude | The size of the force, in Newtons (N) |
| Direction | Which way the force acts |
| Force arrow | Arrow length = size; arrow direction = direction |
| Force diagram | Shows all forces on one object; removes extra detail |
| Net force | The combined result of all forces on an object |

# Section 2: External Forces

## In this section you will learn:
- What external forces are
- The three main external forces: applied force, friction, normal force
- Which direction each external force acts
- How friction acts opposite to motion

---

## 2.1 What Is an External Force?

**External forces** act on an object from outside.

> 📌 **external force** — a force that comes from outside an object

External forces come from people. They come from other objects. They come from the environment. They push or pull the object they act on.

**Bridge example:** A student walks across the timber bridge. The student's weight pushes down on the bridge deck. That is an external force acting on the bridge from outside — from the student's body pressing down on the deck.

---

## 2.2 Applied Force

An **applied force** is a push or pull put directly on an object.

> 📌 **applied force** — a push or pull put on an object by something touching it

Direction: the same direction as the push or pull.

**Bridge examples:**
- A student presses down on the bridge deck with 500 N. That is an applied force pushing down.
- A student pushes sideways on the rope railing with 40 N. That is an applied force pushing sideways.

---

## 2.3 Normal Force

When a surface holds up an object, it pushes back. This push is the **normal force**.

> 📌 **normal force** — the push a surface gives to an object resting on it

Direction: always perpendicular (at 90°) to the surface. For a flat horizontal surface, the normal force points straight up.

**Bridge example:** The student stands on the bridge deck. The student pushes down on the deck with 500 N (applied force). The deck pushes back up on the student with 500 N (normal force). The student does not fall through the deck because the normal force holds them up.

---

## 2.4 Friction

**Friction** is a force between two surfaces that are touching.

> 📌 **friction** — a force that resists sliding between two surfaces

Direction: always **opposite** to the direction of motion (or the attempted direction of motion).

Friction slows moving objects down. Friction also stops stationary objects from sliding when a force is applied to them.

**Bridge example:** A student walks across the wooden planks. Their shoes push backward against the plank surface. Friction acts forward on the shoe — opposite to the backward push. This friction is what allows the student to walk without slipping.

If the bridge deck were icy, friction would be very small. The student's shoe would slide backward. Less friction means less grip.

---

> ⚠️ **Common mistake:** "Friction is not a real force — it just slows things down."
> This is not correct. Friction IS a force. It has a direction. It has a size measured in Newtons. You can measure friction with a spring scale. It acts between any two surfaces that are touching. It always acts opposite to motion. It is just as real as a push or a pull.

---

## 2.5 External Forces on the Bridge — Diagram

The diagram below shows three external forces acting on a student standing on the bridge.

```
              ↑ Normal force (500 N, upward)
              |            — deck pushing up on student
              |
    ←————————[STUDENT]
    Friction               — acts backward, opposing forward motion
    (if walking)
              |
              ↓ Applied force / Weight (500 N, downward)
                           — student's weight pressing down
```

*Notes on this diagram:*
- The normal force and applied force (weight) are equal in size. The student is not accelerating up or down.
- Friction only appears when the student moves (or tries to move) horizontally.
- Each arrow is labelled beside the arrow, not in a separate legend.

---

## 2.6 External Forces Lead to Internal Forces

External forces do something important. They create forces **inside** the material they act on. These are called internal forces. We cover them in Section 3.

When the student's 500 N weight pushes down on the bridge deck, the wood inside the plank is squeezed and pulled at the same time. The plank bends slightly. Those forces inside the wood are internal forces.

---

## Key Ideas — Section 2

| External force | Direction | Bridge example |
|---|---|---|
| Applied force | Same as the push or pull | Student's weight pressing on the deck |
| Normal force | Perpendicular to the surface, away from it | Deck pushing up on the student |
| Friction | Opposite to motion or attempted motion | Shoe-plank contact, opposing slipping |

# Section 3: Internal Forces

## In this section you will learn:
- What internal forces are
- The four types: tension, compression, shear, torsion
- What each internal force does to the particles inside the material
- Where each internal force appears in the bridge

---

## 3.1 What Is an Internal Force?

**Internal forces** act inside a material.

> 📌 **internal force** — a force that acts between particles inside a material

When an external force acts on an object, it pushes or pulls the particles inside. Those particles are forced to move relative to each other. The result is an internal force.

**Bridge example:** The student's 500 N weight pushes down on the bridge deck (external force). Inside the wooden plank, the top layer is being squeezed. The bottom layer is being pulled apart. These squeezing and pulling forces inside the wood are internal forces.

There are four types of internal force. Each one does something different to the particles inside.

---

## 3.2 Tension

**Tension** is an internal pulling force.

> 📌 **tension** — particles being pulled apart, away from each other

It happens when forces pull on both ends of a material at the same time. The particles inside are stretched. They are pulled away from each other.

**Force arrow diagram — tension:**

```
  ←————[    MATERIAL    ]————→
  Pull (outward)        Pull (outward)

        ← ● ● ● ● ● ● →
          particles stretched apart
```

**Bridge examples:**
- The rope railing on the bridge is tied at both posts. The deck's weight and the tension of the posts pull on both ends. The rope is under tension — its particles are being pulled apart along its length.
- The bottom edge of a walking plank is under tension when a student stands in the middle. The middle bends down. The bottom surface is being pulled apart.

Other examples: a guitar string, a tug-of-war rope, a suspension bridge cable.

---

## 3.3 Compression

**Compression** is an internal pushing force.

> 📌 **compression** — particles being pushed together, closer to each other

It happens when forces push on both ends of a material at the same time. The particles inside are squeezed. They are pushed toward each other.

**Force arrow diagram — compression:**

```
  ————→[    MATERIAL    ]←————
  Push (inward)         Push (inward)

        → ● ● ● ● ● ● ←
          particles squeezed together
```

**Bridge examples:**
- The top edge of a walking plank is under compression when a student stands in the middle. The middle bends down. The top surface is squeezed together.
- The vertical wooden posts supporting the handrail are under compression. The weight of the railing and any sideways force pushes down on the posts. The ground pushes up. The particles inside are being squeezed.

Other examples: the legs of a chair, a concrete pillar, a spring being squeezed.

---

## 3.4 Shear

**Shear** is an internal sliding force.

> 📌 **shear** — layers of particles sliding past each other in opposite directions

It happens when forces push or pull different layers of a material in opposite directions at the same time. The layers try to slide past each other.

**Force arrow diagram — shear:**

```
  ————————→  [ TOP LAYER    ]
             [ BOTTOM LAYER ]  ←————————
  (top pushed one way, bottom pushed the other)
```

**Bridge examples:**
- A diagonal brace in the bridge frame resists the deck twisting sideways in the wind. The top of the brace is pushed one direction. The bottom is pushed the opposite direction. The brace is under shear.
- The wooden pegs or bolts connecting the planks to the frame experience shear when the deck bends.

Other examples: scissors cutting paper, a fault line in the earth, slicing bread with a knife.

---

## 3.5 Torsion

**Torsion** is an internal twisting force.

> 📌 **torsion** — particles rotating in opposite directions around a central axis

It happens when forces twist opposite ends of a material in opposite directions. The particles inside rotate relative to each other.

**Force arrow diagram — torsion:**

```
     ↺ [===========] ↻
     (one end twisted one way, other end twisted the other)
```

**Bridge examples:**
- A strong crosswind hits the bridge from one side. The frame wants to rotate. The corner posts resist this. They experience torsion — twisted in opposite directions from top to bottom.
- If someone swings from the rope railing, they may twist the rope. The rope experiences torsion.

Other examples: wringing water from a wet cloth, a screwdriver turning a screw, a car's drive shaft.

---

## 3.6 Internal Forces Comparison Table

| Internal force | What happens to particles | How to picture it |
|---|---|---|
| Tension | Pulled apart, stretched | Rope in a tug of war |
| Compression | Pushed together, squeezed | Spring being pressed |
| Shear | Layers sliding past each other in opposite directions | Scissors cutting paper |
| Torsion | Twisted in opposite directions around a centre | Wringing a wet cloth |

---

> ⚠️ **Common mistake:** "Compression and tension cannot happen in the same object at the same time."
> This is not correct. In the bridge deck plank, the top is under compression and the bottom is under tension at the same time. The student's weight bends the plank. The top gets squeezed. The bottom gets stretched. Both internal forces happen in the same plank at the same moment.

---

## Key Ideas — Section 3

- Internal forces are created by external forces acting on a material.
- There are four types: **tension** (stretch), **compression** (squeeze), **shear** (slide), **torsion** (twist).
- In the bridge plank: top = compression, bottom = tension when a student stands on it.
- The rope railing is under tension. The posts are under compression.
- Diagonal braces experience shear or torsion, depending on the direction of the force.

# Section 4: Plasticity and Elasticity

## In this section you will learn:
- What deformation means
- The difference between elastic and plastic deformation
- What the elastic limit is
- Why engineers care about both types

---

## 4.1 What Is Deformation?

When a force acts on a material, the material may change shape. This change in shape is called **deformation**.

> 📌 **deformation** — a change in shape caused by a force

Not all deformation is permanent. Some materials spring back. Some materials stay in their new shape. This difference matters a great deal in building and engineering.

**Bridge example:** When a student steps onto the middle of a walking plank, the plank bends slightly downward. The plank has been deformed by the student's weight.

---

## 4.2 Elastic Deformation

**Elastic deformation** is a temporary change in shape.

> 📌 **elastic deformation** — the material springs back to its original shape after the force is removed

The material deforms while the force is acting. When the force is removed, the material returns to its original shape. Nothing is permanently changed.

**Bridge example:** The walking planks on the Riverside School bridge flex slightly when a student stands on them. They bend down a little. When the student walks off, the planks spring back to their original flat position. This is elastic deformation. The planks behave like elastic — they return to their original shape.

Other examples: stretching an elastic band and releasing it; squeezing a rubber ball; compressing a spring.

---

## 4.3 Plastic Deformation

**Plastic deformation** is a permanent change in shape.

> 📌 **plastic deformation** — the material keeps its new shape after the force is removed

The material deforms while the force is acting. When the force is removed, the material does NOT return to its original shape. The change is permanent.

**Bridge example:** A bicycle was ridden too fast across the bridge and slammed into a railing post. The post bent sideways. When the bicycle was removed, the post stayed bent. This is plastic deformation. The wood fibres were permanently rearranged. The post will not straighten itself out.

Other examples: squishing playdough; bending a metal wire into a new shape; crumpling a piece of aluminum foil.

---

## 4.4 Elastic vs. Plastic Deformation — Sequence Diagram

```

Step 1 — Before force:   [— — — — — —]   original shape

Step 2 — During force:   [— — — — — — — — —]   stretched or compressed
                                             (force is applied)

Step 3 — After force:    [— — — — — —]   returns to original shape
                                         (force removed, springs back)


PLASTIC DEFORMATION:

Step 1 — Before force:   [— — — — — —]   original shape

Step 2 — During force:   [— — — — — — — — —]   stretched or compressed
                                             (force is applied)

Step 3 — After force:    [— — — — — — — —]   stays in new shape
                                              (force removed, does NOT spring back)
```

---

## 4.5 The Elastic Limit

Every material has an **elastic limit**.

> 📌 **elastic limit** — the maximum force a material can take and still spring back

Below the elastic limit: the material deforms elastically. It springs back.
Above the elastic limit: the material deforms plastically. It stays deformed.

**Bridge examples:**
- The walking planks are designed to stay below their elastic limit under the weight of a student. They flex and spring back every time. This is intentional — it means no permanent damage from normal use.
- The railing post that was hit by a bicycle was pushed past its elastic limit. The wood fibres could not spring back. The post stayed bent. This is permanent structural damage.

**Another example:** A paperclip bent gently → springs back (below elastic limit). A paperclip bent sharply into a right angle → stays bent (above elastic limit).

---

## 4.6 Why This Matters in Engineering

Engineers choose materials based on whether they want elastic or plastic deformation — or neither.

| Situation | What you want | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Bridge cables | Elastic only | Cables must spring back under load. If they stretch permanently, the bridge sags. |
| Bicycle helmet foam | Plastic | The foam must crush and stay crushed in a collision. This absorbs the force. If it bounced back, the force would transfer straight to your head. |
| Bridge planks (normal use) | Elastic | Flex and spring back under student weight — no permanent damage. |
| Bridge posts in a collision | Should stay elastic — but often go plastic | If a post is bent permanently, it must be replaced. |

---

> ⚠️ **Common mistake:** "Elastic means strong. Plastic means weak."
> This is not correct. Elastic and plastic describe the **type of deformation**, not how strong something is. A very strong steel cable is elastic — it stretches slightly under enormous loads and springs back. A soft piece of clay is plastic — it changes shape easily and stays changed. Strength is a separate property from the type of deformation.

---

## Key Ideas — Section 4

| Term | Meaning | Bridge example |
|---|---|---|
| Deformation | Shape change caused by a force | Plank bending under a student's weight |
| Elastic deformation | Temporary — springs back | Walking planks flexing and returning |
| Plastic deformation | Permanent — stays deformed | Railing post bent by a bicycle |
| Elastic limit | Maximum force before deformation becomes permanent | The point where the plank would not spring back |

# Section 5: Newton's Third Law

## In this section you will learn:
- What Newton's Third Law says
- The three properties of every action-reaction pair
- Why action and reaction forces do NOT cancel out
- How to identify action-reaction pairs in the bridge scenario

---

## 5.1 Newton's Third Law — The Statement

**Newton's Third Law** states:

*When one object exerts a force on a second object, the second object exerts a force on the first object that is equal in size and opposite in direction.*

> 📌 **Newton's Third Law** — for every action force, there is an equal and opposite reaction force

In shorter form: **For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.**

This is always true. It has no exceptions. It does not matter how big or small the objects are. It does not matter how fast they are moving. The forces are always equal in size.

---

## 5.2 Action and Reaction Forces

When Newton's Third Law applies, we talk about an **action force** and a **reaction force**.

> 📌 **action force** — the first force one object applies to another

> 📌 **reaction force** — the force the second object applies back to the first; equal in size, opposite in direction

These two forces together are called an **interaction pair**.

> 📌 **interaction pair** — the action force and reaction force together; they always come as a pair

---

## 5.3 Three Properties of Every Action-Reaction Pair

Every action-reaction pair has all three of these properties — always.

**1. Equal in magnitude**
The action force and the reaction force are the same size. Always. No exceptions.

**2. Opposite in direction**
The action force and the reaction force point in opposite directions.

**3. Acting on different objects**
The action force acts on one object. The reaction force acts on a different object. This is the most important property.

All three must be true. If two forces act on the **same** object, they are not an action-reaction pair. They might be balanced forces — but they are not a Newton's Third Law pair.

---

## 5.4 Action-Reaction Pair Diagram — Bridge Example

The diagram below shows the Newton's Third Law pair when a student stands on the bridge.

```
         ┌─────────────────┐
         │    STUDENT      │ ◄──── Reaction force:
         └────────┬────────┘       Deck pushes UP on student
                  │                500 N, upward
                  │
         Action ▼ │ ▼ (student pushes DOWN on deck, 500 N)
   ═══════════════╪══════════════ (bridge deck)
```

- **Action force:** Student pushes DOWN on the bridge deck — 500 N, downward — acts on the DECK.
- **Reaction force:** Bridge deck pushes UP on the student — 500 N, upward — acts on the STUDENT.

These are two different objects. The forces are equal in size. The forces are opposite in direction. This is a valid Newton's Third Law pair.

---

## 5.5 More Bridge Examples — Action-Reaction Table

| Situation | Action force | Reaction force |
|---|---|---|
| Student stands on bridge deck | Student pushes DOWN on deck (500 N) | Deck pushes UP on student (500 N) |
| Student pushes on rope railing | Student pushes OUTWARD on rope (40 N) | Rope pushes INWARD on student's hands (40 N) |
| Student's foot pushes backward on plank | Foot pushes BACKWARD on plank surface | Plank pushes FORWARD on student's foot (this is friction — it moves the student forward) |

---

## 5.6 Non-Bridge Examples — Action-Reaction Table

| Situation | Action force | Reaction force |
|---|---|---|
| You jump off the ground | You push DOWN on the ground | Ground pushes UP on you |
| You row a boat | Paddle pushes BACKWARD on the water | Water pushes FORWARD on the paddle |
| You lean on a wall | You push on the wall | Wall pushes back on you |
| Truck collides with bicycle | Truck exerts force on bicycle | Bicycle exerts EQUAL force on truck |

---

## 5.7 Why Action-Reaction Forces Do NOT Cancel

This is one of the most important ideas in this section.

Action and reaction forces are equal in size and opposite in direction. It might seem like they should cancel out. They do not. Here is why:

**Forces only cancel when they act on the same object.**

The action force acts on Object 1. The reaction force acts on Object 2. They are on different objects. They cannot cancel each other. Each object only responds to the forces acting on IT.

**Bridge example:** The student pushes down on the deck with 500 N. The deck pushes up on the student with 500 N.

- The student's motion is determined by forces on the student — including the deck pushing UP on them. The student does not fall through the deck.
- The deck's behavior is determined by forces on the deck — including the student pushing DOWN on it. The deck bends slightly under that load.

These two forces do not cancel. They act on different objects. Each object responds to what acts on it.

---

> ⚠️ **Common mistake:** "The heavier object pushes harder in a collision."
> This is not correct. When a truck and a bicycle collide, the force the truck puts on the bicycle is **exactly equal** to the force the bicycle puts on the truck. Newton's Third Law has no exception for mass. The bicycle and the truck push on each other with the same force. What is different is what happens to each object — because the bicycle has much less mass, it responds more dramatically to the same force. Equal forces, unequal effects.

---

> ⚠️ **Common mistake:** "Action and reaction forces cancel out, so nothing ever moves."
> This is not correct. Action and reaction forces act on **different objects**. They cannot cancel. Forces only cancel when they act on the **same** object. When you jump, the ground pushes up on YOU. You push down on the GROUND. The force on you (from the ground) makes you accelerate upward. It has nothing to cancel with — because it is the only upward force acting on you. You move because of that unbalanced force on you alone.

---

> ⚠️ **Common mistake:** "Friction does not have a direction — it just slows things down."
> This is not correct. Friction IS a force. It has a specific direction: always opposite to the direction of motion or attempted motion. When a student walks forward across the bridge, their foot pushes backward on the plank. Friction acts forward on the student's foot. Without that directional friction force, the student could not walk forward. Friction acts opposite to motion — that is its direction.

---

## 5.8 How to Identify a Newton's Third Law Pair

Use this checklist:

- [ ] Force 1 acts on Object A.
- [ ] Force 2 acts on Object B — a DIFFERENT object.
- [ ] Force 1 and Force 2 are equal in size.
- [ ] Force 1 and Force 2 are opposite in direction.

If all four are true, you have a Newton's Third Law pair.

**Quick test:** If both forces act on the same object — it is NOT a Newton's Third Law pair.

---

| Idea | What it means |
|---|---|
| Newton's Third Law | For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction |
| Action force | The force one object applies to another |
| Reaction force | The force the second object applies back — equal size, opposite direction |
| Interaction pair | Action + reaction together; always come as a pair |
| Why they don't cancel | They act on DIFFERENT objects — forces only cancel on the SAME object |
| Mass and Newton's Third Law | Forces are always equal — mass affects the effect, not the force |

# Unit Summary — Forces at the Riverside School Bridge

The timber pedestrian bridge at Riverside School shows every big idea from this unit.

---

**What is a force?**
When a student walks across the bridge, they push down on the deck. That push is a force. It has magnitude (about 500 N for an average student) and direction (downward). We can draw it as a force arrow.

---

**External forces on the student standing on the deck:**

```
          ↑ Normal force (500 N, upward)
          |    — deck pushes up on the student
          |
    ←    [STUDENT]    (friction acts backward if student moves forward)
          |
          ↓ Weight / Applied force (500 N, downward)
               — student's weight presses on the deck
```

The normal force and weight are equal. The student does not fall through the deck or float upward.

---

**Internal forces in the bridge deck plank:**

When the student stands on the middle of a plank, the plank bends slightly.

```
         ↓ Student's weight (external force)

  ═══════════════════════════════════
  → → → TOP of plank → → →    COMPRESSION — top is squeezed
  ─────────────────────────────────
  ← ← ← BOTTOM of plank ← ← ←  TENSION — bottom is stretched
  ═══════════════════════════════════
```

The rope railing: under **tension** (pulled tight between the posts).
The vertical posts: under **compression** (weight pressing down, ground pushing up).
The diagonal braces: under **shear** (resisting the frame sliding sideways in the wind).

---

**Elastic and plastic deformation:**

- Walking planks flex when a student steps on them → spring back when the student walks off → **elastic deformation** (below the elastic limit).
- A railing post bent by a bicycle → stays bent permanently → **plastic deformation** (past the elastic limit).

---

**Newton's Third Law at the bridge:**

Student pushes DOWN on the deck with 500 N → **action force** (acts on the deck).
Deck pushes UP on the student with 500 N → **reaction force** (acts on the student).

Equal in magnitude. Opposite in direction. Different objects. This is a complete Newton's Third Law pair.

## Scenario Notes for Year Review Alignment

The timber pedestrian bridge at Riverside School is the central scenario for this Forces unit. It also appears in the year review workbook, but several questions could be tightened or enriched using the richer bridge details established in these notes.

**Specific year review question notes:**

**Question 1 (internal forces in a beam):** This question is well-aligned. It uses "a student walks across the middle of the beam" — which matches the bridge scenario. The question distinguishes top (compression) and bottom (tension), which aligns exactly with Section 3.3 and 3.2 above. No changes needed; this is a strong alignment.

**Question 9 (elastic vs. plastic deformation):** This question uses the bridge — walking planks and a railing post. It aligns perfectly with Section 4.2 and 4.3 above. However, the question currently says "railing post hit by a bicycle" without naming the bicycle as the external force cause. A small improvement: part (c) could add "What external force caused the plastic deformation of the railing post?" — this would ask students to connect internal and external forces, and would map back to both Section 2 and Section 4.

**Question 12 (Newton's Third Law at the bridge):** This question has a student pushing on the railing with 40 N. It is well-aligned with the bridge scenario. However, it does not ask students to identify the objects involved in the action-reaction pair — only the force magnitude and properties. A stronger version of part (a) would be: "What force does the railing push back on the student's hands? State the size, direction, and which object each force acts on." This would require students to demonstrate the full three-property test for a Newton's Third Law pair, not just recall the equal magnitude.

**Question 19 (Newton's Third Law diagram at the bridge):** This is the strongest question in the Forces set. The diagram shows a student on the bridge deck with a 500 N downward force. Students draw the reaction force. This matches the exact scenario and diagram from Section 5.4 above. Students who have these notes will find this question a direct application. No changes needed.

**Question 24 (Newton's Third Law — mass misconception):** This question addresses the heavier-student misconception directly — Student A (80 kg) and Student B (40 kg) both push the railing with 60 N each. The question asks whether the railing pushes back harder on the heavier student. This aligns with the misconception addressed in Section 5.7 above (the truck-and-bicycle example). The question is strong as written. Consider adding a sentence starter in the student workbook version: "Newton's Third Law tells us that the reaction force depends on..." — this would scaffold the reasoning step students often skip.

**Question 28 (three internal forces in the bridge — extended):** This question identifies a horizontal rope (tension), a vertical post (compression), and a diagonal brace (torsion or shear). This aligns exactly with Section 3.6 above. However, part (c) labels the diagonal brace as experiencing torsion from wind — in the notes above, the diagonal brace is described as resisting twisting and can experience either shear or torsion depending on the force direction. For consistency, the year review question could specify the direction: "A diagonal brace resists the bridge twisting sideways — the top is pushed one way and the bottom the other." This wording would unambiguously indicate shear, avoiding the shear/torsion ambiguity and matching the specific language in Section 3.4.

**Stretch Zone S1 (Matter + Forces — railway track and compression):** This question asks about compression at the contact point between two expanding rail sections. This is a strong cross-unit question. It does not use the bridge scenario — and it should not, because it is specifically about matter + forces. No change needed.

**Stretch Zone S4 (Forces + Living Systems — spruce tree bending in wind):** This question asks about internal forces in a living tree trunk and whether the trunk's return to upright is elastic deformation. This is a strong question but uses a different scenario than the bridge. Students who have these notes will be able to transfer the elastic/plastic concepts from the plank and railing post to the tree trunk. No alignment change needed, but the notes package and workbook scenarios are complementary rather than identical here.
