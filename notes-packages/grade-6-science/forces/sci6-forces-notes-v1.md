### Notes

A force is a _______ or a _______ acting on an object.

Forces have two essential properties:

1. **Magnitude** — the _______ or _______ of the force, measured in units called **Newtons (N)**
2. **Direction** — the _______ the force is acting

A force of 10 N pushing right is different from a force of 10 N pushing left, even though the magnitude is the same. Both properties matter.

**Representing forces with arrows:**

We draw forces as arrows. The arrow tells us two things:
- The _______ the force points shows the **direction**
- The _______ of the arrow represents the **magnitude** — a longer arrow means a larger force

**Force diagrams — a tool from abstraction:**

A force diagram (also called a free body diagram) is a simplified drawing that shows only the _______ acting on an object, removing all unnecessary detail.

> *Connection to Computer Science:* Force diagrams are **abstractions** — they keep only what matters (force direction and size) and remove everything else (the object's shape, colour, material). This is the same thinking used in programming and design: represent only the information you need.

---

Since the forces are in opposite directions, the overall (net) effect is a push of _______ N to the right.

### Notes

**External forces** act on an object _______ — they come from a person, another object, or the environment acting on the object from outside.

There are three main external forces at the Grade 6 level:

**1. Applied force**
A force applied directly to an object by a person or another object.
- Direction: _______ you push or pull
- Example: pushing a shopping cart forward

**2. Friction**
A force that _______ motion between two surfaces in contact.
- Direction: always _______ to the direction of motion (or attempted motion)
- Example: a book sliding across a desk slows down because friction acts _______ the direction of sliding

**3. Elastic (spring) force**
A force produced when an elastic material is _______ or _______.
- Direction: acts to _______ the material to its original shape
- Example: stretching an elastic band — it pulls back toward its resting length

---

> **MISCONCEPTION**
>
> *"Friction isn't really a force — it just slows things down."*
>
> Friction IS a force. Like all forces, it has a **direction** (opposite to motion) and a **size** (measured in Newtons). It acts between surfaces whenever one surface moves — or tries to move — against another. You can measure friction with a spring scale. The reason it feels invisible is that it acts in the opposite direction to what we're already focused on, but it is just as real as any push or pull.

---

**Key relationship:** External forces acting on an object cause forces to develop _______ the material — called internal forces. (We'll explore this in Section 3.)

### Notes

When a force acts on a material, the material may change shape — this is called _______.

There are two types of deformation:

**Elastic deformation** — the material _______ to its original shape when the force is removed.
This is a _______ change.
Example: stretching an elastic band; squeezing a rubber ball

**Plastic deformation** — the material _______ its new shape even after the force is removed.
This is a _______ change.
Example: squishing playdough; bending a metal wire into a new shape

---

**The elastic limit:**

Every material has an **elastic limit** — the maximum force it can experience before elastic deformation becomes plastic deformation.

- Below the elastic limit: the material springs _______ (elastic)
- Above the elastic limit: the material stays _______ (plastic)

A paper clip bent slightly → springs back (below elastic limit).
A paper clip bent sharply → stays bent (_______ the elastic limit).

## Section 5: Newton's Third Law

**Learning target:** I can state Newton's Third Law, identify action-reaction pairs, and explain why these forces do not cancel each other.

---

### Vocabulary

| Term | Definition in your own words | One example |
|---|---|---|
| Newton's Third Law | | |
| Action force | | |
| Reaction force | | |
| Interaction pair | | |

---

### Notes

**Newton's Third Law:**
When one object exerts a force on a second object, the second object exerts a force on the first object that is _______ in size and _______ in direction.

Shorter version: For every _______, there is an equal and opposite _______.

---

**The three properties of every action-reaction pair:**

1. Equal in _______
2. Opposite in _______
3. Acting on _______ objects

All three must be true. If the forces act on the same object — they are not an action-reaction pair.

---

**Examples:**

| Situation | Action force | Reaction force |
|---|---|---|
| You jump off the ground | You push _______ on the floor | Floor pushes _______ on you |
| You row a boat | Paddle pushes _______ on the water | Water pushes _______ on the paddle |
| You lean on a wall | You push _______ on the wall | Wall pushes _______ on you |

---

> **MISCONCEPTION 1**
>
> *"The heavier object always pushes harder in a collision."*
>
> When a truck and a bicycle collide, the force the truck exerts on the bicycle is **exactly equal** to the force the bicycle exerts on the truck. Newton's Third Law does not have exceptions for size or mass. The difference in what happens to each object (the bicycle moves more) is because of the difference in mass — a small mass responds more to the same force. The forces are equal; the effects are not.

---

> **MISCONCEPTION 2**
>
> *"Action and reaction forces cancel out, so nothing ever moves."*
>
> Action-reaction forces **cannot** cancel each other because they act on **different objects**. Forces only cancel when they act on the **same** object. When you jump, the ground pushes up on *you* and you push down on the *ground* — these are two different objects. The force on you (from the ground) and the force on the ground (from you) have nothing to cancel with, because each object's motion is governed by forces acting *on that object only*.
