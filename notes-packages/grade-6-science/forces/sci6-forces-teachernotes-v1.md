## How to use this document

Each teaching beat has three parts:

| Label | What it contains |
|---|---|
| **SHOW** | What is visible to students — draw on the board or project. One thing per beat. |
| **SAY** | Key phrases and questions. Not sentences to read — prompts for fluent delivery. Answers to blanks shown in **[brackets]**. |
| **CUE** | The exact signal for students to write. Four types: ★ Write / ✎ Draw / ⊡ Think / ↩ Check |

Flags appear in callout blocks:
> **FLAG — Misconception:** Red alert. Students will get this wrong without a direct address.
> **FLAG — Pacing:** Warning for where lessons tend to lose momentum.
> **FLAG — CS Connection:** Embedded cross-curricular moment — worth lingering on.

**The screen and the voice carry different things.** What is SHOW stays visual — labels, diagrams, key terms. What is SAY carries the reasoning. Never put your SAY on the screen.

## Section 1: What Is a Force?
*Learning target: I can explain what a force is, describe its direction and size, and represent a force using a force arrow.*
*Timing: ~20 minutes direct instruction*

---

**Beat 1 — Vocabulary setup**
**SHOW:** Board: The five vocabulary terms in a column — Force / Newton (N) / Magnitude / Direction / Force diagram
**SAY:**
- "Before we go into the notes — these five terms are on your vocab table."
- "Don't fill them in yet. We'll build them as we go."
- "By the end of this section you'll have your own definition and example for each one."
**CUE:** ⊡ Think: "Read the five terms. Which one do you think you already know? Keep it in your head."

---

**Beat 2 — What is a force?**
**SHOW:** Board: *FORCE = push or pull*. Draw a simple arrow pushing a box. Draw a simple arrow pulling a box.
**SAY:**
- "A force is a push or a pull acting on an object."
- "That's the whole definition. Push. Pull. Acting on an object."
- "Kick a ball — that's a push. Pull a door open — that's a pull."
- Pause 3 seconds.
**CUE:** ★ Write: "A force is a **[push]** or a **[pull]** acting on an object."

> **FLAG — Pacing:** Students often want to add "energy" or "movement" to the definition. That's coming later. Hold the line: push or pull, that's it for now.

---

**Beat 3 — Magnitude**
**SHOW:** Board: *MAGNITUDE = size / strength (measured in Newtons)*. Write: *N* large.
**SAY:**
- "Forces have two properties — magnitude is the first."
- "Magnitude means the size or strength of the force."
- "Unit: Newtons. Named after Isaac Newton. Symbol: capital N."
- "A gentle push might be 2 N. A hard shove might be 50 N. Picking up a textbook is about 10 N."
- "Bigger number = bigger force. That's all magnitude means."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Magnitude = **[size]** or **[strength]** of the force, measured in **[Newtons (N)]**"

---

**Beat 4 — Direction**
**SHOW:** Board: Draw two arrows — same length, pointing opposite directions. Label: *10 N →* and *← 10 N*. Same magnitude. Different direction.
**SAY:**
- "Second property — direction."
- "These two forces are both 10 N. Are they the same force?"
- Pause 3–4 seconds.
- "No. One pushes right. One pushes left. Direction changes everything."
- "A force of 10 N pushing right is completely different from 10 N pushing left."
- "Both properties matter — always."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Direction = the **[way]** the force is acting"

---

**Beat 5 — Force arrows**
**SHOW:** Board: Draw a single arrow. Label: *Direction = which way the arrow points*. Below it, draw a short arrow and a long arrow side by side. Label: *Length = magnitude*.
**SAY:**
- "We represent forces as arrows. The arrow gives us both properties at once."
- "Which way the arrow points → direction."
- "How long the arrow is → magnitude. Longer arrow = bigger force."
- "This is a convention — an agreed-upon language. All scientists use the same system."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "The **[way]** the force points shows the direction. The **[length]** of the arrow represents the magnitude."

> **FLAG — Pacing:** Students sometimes confuse arrow length with actual distance or movement. Clarify: length here is a proportional representation, not a measurement of how far the object moves.

---

**Beat 6 — Force diagrams and abstraction**
**SHOW:** Board: Draw a simple box. Label it with the shape, colour, texture. Then erase everything except the box outline and draw force arrows. Label: *Force diagram — forces only.*
**SAY:**
- "A force diagram strips away everything except the forces."
- "Not the colour of the box. Not what the box is made of. Not whether it's on wheels."
- "Just: what forces are acting, and which direction."
- "This is called abstraction — keeping only what you need, removing everything else."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "A force diagram shows only the **[forces]** acting on an object, removing all unnecessary detail."

> **FLAG — CS Connection:** Pause here. "This is exactly what happens in programming and design. When you write code to model a problem, you keep only the properties that matter. A force diagram is an abstraction. A variable in code is an abstraction. Same thinking." *Allow 30–60 seconds for students to record this in the connection note.*

---

**Beat 7 — Worked example: reading a force diagram**
**SHOW:** Board: Draw the worked example — box in the centre, *20 N →* on the left side of the box, *← 8 N* on the right side. Make the 20 N arrow visibly longer. Label both forces directly beside each arrow.
**SAY:**
- "Two forces on this box. Look at the diagram."
- "The 20 N arrow points right. The 8 N arrow points left."
- Pause 3 seconds.
- "They're working against each other. How do we find the net force?"
- Pause 3–4 seconds.
- "Subtract. Both on the same line, opposite directions: 20 minus 8 equals 12."
- "Net force: 12 N to the right. The box would move right."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "20 N pushes **[right]**, 8 N pushes **[left]**. Net force = **[12]** N to the right."

> **FLAG — Misconception:** Students frequently say the net force is 28 N (they add instead of subtract because there are two numbers). Pre-empt this: "Some people add them. Why would that be wrong?" Wait for a response. The key idea: forces in opposite directions partially cancel each other.

---

**Beat 8 — Vocabulary table fill-in**
**SHOW:** Board: The five terms still on the board. Nothing else.
**SAY:**
- "Now go back to your vocabulary table."
- "Fill in each definition in your own words — not the textbook definition."
- "Your definition should be something you could explain to a Grade 4 student."
- "Add one example you thought of yourself."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Complete your vocab table now — own words, own examples."

*Allow 3–5 minutes. Circulate. Watch for: students copying the notes text verbatim (redirect), students leaving 'Newton' blank (prompt: what did we say it measures?).*

---

**Beat 9 — Questions Now Answerable**
*Teacher: Q1, Q6, Q11, Q22*

**CUE:** ⊡ Think: "Scan the workbook. Find 4 questions you think you could now answer. Put a tick beside each one — do not answer yet."

*Allow 60–90 seconds. Then as a class: have students share which questions they found. For each one — ask why it connects to this section before moving on.*

---

### Section 1 — Retrieval Check Facilitation

**Before releasing:**
**SAY:** "Close your notes. Everything closed. I'm going to ask you some questions — you write the answers from memory."
**CUE:** ↩ Check: "Notes closed. Retrieval check begins now."

**After students finish (4–5 min):**
**SAY:** "Open your notes. Find the answer in your notes. Fix anything that was wrong or incomplete — use a different colour if you have one."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Open your notes. Check and correct now."

**Answer guide:**
1. Magnitude (size/strength) and direction
2. Newtons (N)
3. The arrow represents a larger force — greater magnitude
4. Right arrow twice as long as left arrow, both labeled
5. Incorrect — forces can act on an object without causing movement. If the box isn't moving, the forces are balanced, not absent.

*Q5 is the stretch — if students finish early, prompt them: "The box isn't moving. What does that tell you about the forces?" Don't give the answer before the retrieval check ends.*

## Section 2: External Forces
*Learning target: I can identify applied force, friction, and elastic force, describe their direction, and explain how external forces affect objects.*
*Timing: ~20 minutes direct instruction*

*Bring to class: spring scale, elastic band.*

---

**Beat 1 — External forces intro**
**SHOW:** Board: *EXTERNAL FORCE = acts from outside the object*
**SAY:**
- "External — where does that prefix come from? External means outside."
- "External forces come from outside the object — a person pushing, the floor pressing up, the air."
- "The object is being acted on. The force source is outside it."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "External forces act on an object **[from outside]** — from a person, another object, or the environment."

---

**Beat 2 — Applied force**
**SHOW:** Board: *APPLIED FORCE → direction: wherever you push or pull*. Draw: stick figure pushing a box.
**SAY:**
- "Applied force — you apply it. Direct contact."
- "A person pushes a cart. An engine drives a car forward. You kick a ball."
- "Direction: wherever you push or pull. The force arrow follows the push."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Applied force direction: **[wherever]** you push or pull"

---

**Beat 3 — Friction (with demonstration)**
**SHOW:** Board: *FRICTION → opposes motion → direction: OPPOSITE to motion*. Draw: box moving right, friction arrow pointing left.
**SAY:**
- "Friction acts between two surfaces in contact."
- "Hold up spring scale. Drag an object across the desk. Watch the scale — that number is the friction force."
- "Friction always opposes motion. If the box moves right, friction points left. Always."
- "Friction never acts in the direction of motion."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Friction **[opposes]** motion. Direction: always **[opposite]** to the direction of motion."

> **FLAG — Misconception: "Friction isn't really a force — it just slows things down."**
> Address directly before moving on. SAY: "Here's something students often get wrong — they say friction isn't really a force. Is it? What makes something a force?" Pause for responses. Then: "Friction has a direction — opposite to motion. Friction has a size — measurable in Newtons, right here on this spring scale. It acts between surfaces. It IS a force. It happens to act in the direction that slows things, which is why we notice it as 'slowing down' rather than as a push in a direction — but it is a force." Point to the force diagram arrow as you say this. Do not skip this. Students who leave with the misconception intact will miss all friction questions on the unit test.

---

**Beat 4 — Elastic force (with demonstration)**
**SHOW:** Board: *ELASTIC FORCE → stretched or compressed → direction: back to original shape*. Hold up elastic band, stretch it, release.
**SAY:**
- "Elastic force comes from stretching or compressing an elastic material."
- "Stretch this elastic band — what happens when I let go? It returns to its original shape."
- "The force that pulls it back is the elastic force."
- "A spring, a rubber ball, a trampoline — all elastic forces."
- "Direction: always back toward the original shape."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Elastic force — material is **[stretched]** or **[compressed]**. Acts to **[return]** the material to its original shape."

---

**Beat 5 — Four-force diagram**
**SHOW:** Board: Draw a box. Add four arrows: up (Normal force), down (Gravity/weight), right (Applied force), left (Friction). Label each arrow directly beside it.
**SAY:**
- "Here's a box on a floor being pushed to the right."
- "Four forces — name them with me."
- Point up: "Normal force — floor pushing up. Always perpendicular to the surface."
- Point down: "Gravity — weight pulling down."
- Point right: "Applied force — the push."
- Point left: "Friction — opposing motion."
- "Notice: labels are beside each arrow, not in a legend on the side. That's the split-attention rule — we keep the label next to what it describes."
**CUE:** ✎ Draw: "Draw this force diagram in your notes. Include all four labels directly beside each arrow."

> **FLAG — Pacing:** Normal force is introduced here but not defined in the student notes as a vocabulary term — it appears in the diagram only. Don't go deep on it. It's scaffolding for the diagram, not a tested term at Grade 6. A brief "the floor pushes up just as hard as you push down — we'll explore this more in Newton's Third Law" is enough.

---

**Beat 6 — Bridge to internal forces**
**SHOW:** Board: Same four-force diagram. Add a thought bubble inside the box: *"What's happening inside?"*
**SAY:**
- "All these forces are pushing and pulling from the outside."
- "What do you think is happening inside the material of that box when those forces act on it?"
- "That's Section 3. Hold that thought."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "External forces cause forces to develop **[inside / within]** the material — called internal forces."

---

**Beat 7 — Connection prompt: Bike friction**
**SHOW:** Board: *"Where was friction helpful on a bike? Where did it work against you?"*
**SAY:**
- "Before we move on — think about riding a bike."
- "Write your answer first. Don't wait to think and then write — write while you think."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Answer the bike question in your notes now — before reading ahead."

*Allow 60–90 seconds. Circulate. Good answers: helpful = brakes, tyres gripping road; against = air resistance, chain friction (energy loss). Students who give only one example need a prompt: "Now find the other side."*

---

**Beat 8 — Questions Now Answerable**
*Teacher: Q2, Q12, Q18*

**CUE:** ⊡ Think: "Scan the workbook. Find 3 questions you think you could now answer. Put a tick beside each one — do not answer yet."

*Allow 60–90 seconds. Then as a class: have students share which questions they found. For each one — ask why it connects to this section before moving on.*

---

### Section 2 — Retrieval Check Facilitation

**Before releasing:**
**CUE:** ↩ Check: "Notes closed. Three external forces. I want you to be able to name them, describe their direction, and give an example."

**Answer guide:**
1. Applied force, friction, elastic force
2. Opposite to the direction of motion (leftward if the puck moves right)
3. Elastic force
4. Applied force pushes right; friction acts left (opposite to direction of motion)
5. Friction must be 15 N acting to the left — equal in magnitude to the applied force, opposite in direction. The net force is zero, which is why the box doesn't move. *This is the stretch — it previews Newton's Third Law logic. Students who get this are ready for Section 5.*

## Section 3: Internal Forces
*Learning target: I can identify and distinguish tension, compression, shear, and torsion, and explain how external forces produce internal forces in a material.*
*Timing: ~20 minutes direct instruction*

*Bring to class: foam block or sponge (shear and torsion demonstrations).*

---

**Beat 1 — Internal forces intro**
**SHOW:** Board: *INTERNAL FORCE = acts inside the material*. Draw external arrows pushing on a box → question mark inside the box.
**SAY:**
- "Section 2 was about forces acting on the outside of an object."
- "Section 3 is about what happens inside the material when those outside forces act."
- "Push on a foam block. The outside force is your hands. The inside force is what the foam does in response."
- "Internal forces act within the material — particle against particle."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Internal forces act **[within / inside]** a material — the result of external forces acting from outside."

---

**Beat 2 — Tension**
**SHOW:** Board: Draw the tension diagram — block labeled MATERIAL, arrows pulling left and right simultaneously. Label: *TENSION — pulling / stretching force*.
**SAY:**
- "Tension — the material is being pulled apart from both ends."
- "Two external forces pulling outward. Inside, the particles are being pulled away from each other."
- "Rope in a tug of war. Suspension bridge cable. A stretched elastic band."
- "Think: when you pull a rope tight, the rope is in tension."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Tension = **[pulling / stretching]** internal force. Particles are being pulled **[apart]**."

---

**Beat 3 — Compression**
**SHOW:** Board: Draw the compression diagram — block, arrows pushing in from both ends. Label: *COMPRESSION — pushing / squeezing force*.
**SAY:**
- "Compression — opposite of tension. Forces pushing inward from both ends."
- "Particles are being pushed together."
- "Chair leg when you sit. Spring being squeezed. Concrete pillar holding up a floor."
- "Your weight compresses the chair legs right now."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Compression = **[pushing / squeezing]** internal force. Particles are being pushed **[together]**."

---

**Beat 4 — Shear (with demonstration)**
**SHOW:** Board: Draw the shear diagram — two layers, top layer arrow pointing right, bottom layer arrow pointing left. Label: *SHEAR — sliding layers*.
**SAY:**
- "Shear — layers of the material slide past each other in opposite directions."
- "Hold foam block. Push the top to the right with one hand. Push the bottom to the left with the other. That's shear."
- Demonstrate with foam block.
- "Scissors cutting paper — top blade goes down, bottom blade goes up. The paper is being sheared."
- "Geological fault lines — one side of the earth moves one way, the other side moves the other way."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Shear = **[sliding]** internal force. Layers slide past each other in **[opposite]** directions."

---

**Beat 5 — Torsion (with demonstration)**
**SHOW:** Board: Draw a cylinder with curved arrows indicating twist — clockwise at top, counterclockwise at bottom. Label: *TORSION — twisting force*.
**SAY:**
- "Torsion — the material is being twisted."
- "Hold the foam. Twist both ends in opposite directions. That's torsion."
- Demonstrate.
- "Wringing water from a cloth. A wrench turning a bolt. A car's drive shaft."
- "The key feature: opposite rotation at each end around a central axis."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Torsion = **[twisting]** internal force. Particles rotate in opposite directions around a central axis."

> **FLAG — Pacing:** Students often confuse shear and torsion. The clearest distinction: shear is linear (layers sliding side to side in a straight direction), torsion is rotational (layers rotating around an axis). A quick board diagram with both side by side helps. Ask: "Which one would a wet cloth experience when you wring it? Why is it not shear?" Give 30 seconds for a partner discussion.

---

**Beat 6 — Connection prompt: Look around**
**SHOW:** Board: *"Find: (a) one object under tension, (b) one object under shear. Write the external force causing each."*
**SAY:**
- "Look around the room right now."
- "Don't tell me the answer — write it first."
- "Start with compression — you already have one: your chair legs."
- "Now find tension. Now find shear."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Write your tension and shear examples in your notes. Include the external force causing each."

*Allow 90 seconds. Good answers: tension = backpack straps, suspension cables on a bridge visible in a photo, a stretched rope; shear = scissors cutting paper, a bolt being tightened, a knife cutting food. Avoid: torsion examples labeled as shear — distinguish again if you see it.*

---

**Beat 7 — Questions Now Answerable**
*Teacher: Q3, Q8, Q13, Q17*

**CUE:** ⊡ Think: "Scan the workbook. Find 4 questions you think you could now answer. Put a tick beside each one — do not answer yet."

*Allow 60–90 seconds. Then as a class: have students share which questions they found. For each one — ask why it connects to this section before moving on.*

---

### Section 3 — Retrieval Check Facilitation

**Before releasing:**
**CUE:** ↩ Check: "Notes closed. Four internal force types. I want the name, what the particles are doing, and one example each."

**Answer guide:**
1. External = acts from outside the object (a push or pull from another object). Internal = acts inside the material (what happens to the particles within as a result).
2. Tension
3. Compression
4. Torsion
5. Shear. External forces: one hand pushes top face right, other hand pushes bottom face left. Internal force type: shear — layers slide past each other in opposite directions.

## Section 4: Plasticity and Elasticity
*Learning target: I can explain the difference between elastic and plastic deformation, identify examples of each, and explain what the elastic limit means for a material.*
*Timing: ~15–20 minutes direct instruction*

*Bring to class: 2 paper clips — demonstrate both below and beyond elastic limit. Optional: helmet photo + bridge cable photo.*

---

**Beat 1 — Deformation intro**
**SHOW:** Board: *DEFORMATION = change in shape due to a force*
**SAY:**
- "When a force acts on a material, the material may change shape."
- "That change is called deformation. De-form — losing its form."
- "Two types — they're completely different in what they mean for the material."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "When a force acts on a material, the material may change shape — this is called **[deformation]**."

---

**Beat 2 — Elastic deformation**
**SHOW:** Board: *ELASTIC DEFORMATION → temporary change → returns to original shape*. Draw: before/during/after sequence with spring returning.
**SAY:**
- "Elastic deformation — the material changes shape during the force, then springs back."
- "This is a temporary change."
- "Squeeze a rubber ball — it deforms. Let go — it returns. Elastic deformation."
- "Stretch an elastic band — same thing."
- "The key: remove the force → original shape returns."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Elastic deformation = material **[returns]** to its original shape when the force is removed. This is a **[temporary]** change."

---

**Beat 3 — Plastic deformation**
**SHOW:** Board: *PLASTIC DEFORMATION → permanent change → stays deformed*. Draw before/during/after sequence where the 'after' shape is different from 'before'.
**SAY:**
- "Plastic deformation — the material changes shape and stays there."
- "This is a permanent change."
- "Squish playdough — it stays squished."
- "Bend a metal wire into an L-shape — it stays as an L."
- "Permanent. The force changed the material."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Plastic deformation = material **[keeps]** its new shape even after the force is removed. This is a **[permanent]** change."

> **FLAG — Vocabulary:** The word "plastic" here does NOT mean the material plastic (like a plastic bottle). It means "able to be permanently shaped" — from the Greek plastikos. Many students will make this connection incorrectly. "Is playdough plastic? Is silly putty plastic?" Walk through a few quick examples to distinguish plastic-deformation from plastic-the-material.

---

**Beat 4 — Elastic limit**
**SHOW:** Board: *ELASTIC LIMIT = the point where elastic deformation becomes plastic deformation*. Draw a number line with "below elastic limit → elastic" on the left and "above elastic limit → plastic" on the right.
**SAY:**
- "Every material has an elastic limit — a maximum force before it stops bouncing back."
- "Below the limit: elastic. The material returns."
- "Above the limit: plastic. The material stays deformed."
- Hold up paper clip. Bend it slightly: "Below the elastic limit — springs back."
- Bend it sharply: "Above the elastic limit — stays bent. Permanently deformed."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Below the elastic limit: springs **[back]** (elastic). Above the elastic limit: stays **[deformed]** (plastic)."

---

**Beat 5 — Engineering applications**
**SHOW:** Board: Two columns — *HELMET (plastic foam)* | *BRIDGE CABLE (steel)*. Under each: why?
**SAY:**
- "Why does this matter in engineering? Two examples."
- Point to HELMET: "Helmet foam deforms plastically in a collision. It absorbs the force by staying crushed. If it sprang back elastically, it would bounce the force into your skull."
- Point to BRIDGE CABLE: "Bridge cables must stay elastic — they stretch under load and spring back to original length. A cable that permanently deforms will fail."
- "Same physics. Opposite design requirement. The engineer's job is choosing the right type."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Helmets use plastic foam — deforms **[plastically]** to absorb force. Bridge cables must stay **[elastic]** — return to original length under load."

---

**Beat 6 — Questions Now Answerable**
*Teacher: Q4, Q9, Q20*

**CUE:** ⊡ Think: "Scan the workbook. Find 3 questions you think you could now answer. Put a tick beside each one — do not answer yet."

*Allow 60–90 seconds. Then as a class: have students share which questions they found. For each one — ask why it connects to this section before moving on.*

---

### Section 4 — Retrieval Check Facilitation

**Before releasing:**
**CUE:** ↩ Check: "Notes closed. Two types of deformation, what the elastic limit is, and the engineering applications."

**Answer guide:**
1. Elastic = temporary change, material returns. Plastic = permanent change, material stays deformed.
2. Elastic deformation — the spring returns to its original length, which is the definition of elastic behaviour.
3. Plastic deformation — stays at 90°, which is a permanent change.
4. The force exceeds the elastic limit — the material no longer returns to its original shape; the change becomes permanent.
5. Plastically deforming material — the bumper should absorb the force by crushing (staying deformed), not bounce the force back to the passenger compartment. Elastic deformation would transfer the force to the car body and occupants. (Some students will argue "elastic so it can be reused" — acknowledge this is practically appealing, then redirect to the safety physics.)

## Section 5: Newton's Third Law
*Learning target: I can state Newton's Third Law, identify action-reaction pairs, and explain why these forces do not cancel each other.*
*Timing: ~20–25 minutes direct instruction*

---

**Beat 1 — The law statement**
**SHOW:** Board: *Newton's Third Law: For every ACTION, there is an equal and opposite REACTION.*
**SAY:**
- "Newton's Third Law. This one sounds simple and it will confuse students for years."
- "Let me say it precisely: when object A exerts a force on object B, object B exerts a force on object A that is equal in size and opposite in direction."
- "Action. Reaction. Equal in size. Opposite in direction."
- Pause 4–5 seconds.
**CUE:** ★ Write: "When one object exerts a force on a second object, the second object exerts a force on the first that is **[equal]** in size and **[opposite]** in direction."

---

**Beat 2 — The three properties**
**SHOW:** Board: Three bullet points — (1) Equal in magnitude / (2) Opposite in direction / (3) Acting on DIFFERENT objects
**SAY:**
- "Three properties — all three must be true."
- "Equal in magnitude: same size."
- "Opposite in direction: one goes left, one goes right."
- "Acting on different objects: THIS is the one students miss."
- "The action force acts on object B. The reaction force acts on object A. Two different objects."
- "If both forces act on the same object, they are NOT an action-reaction pair."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Action-reaction pairs: equal in **[magnitude]** / opposite in **[direction]** / acting on **[different]** objects."

> **FLAG — This is the heart of Section 5.** Property 3 — different objects — is what makes the law coherent and what prevents the misconception "they cancel out." Spend 60 extra seconds here. Ask: "Why does it matter that they act on different objects?" Don't answer — wait for a student response. Then move to the misconception beat.

---

**Beat 3 — Examples table**
**SHOW:** Board: Draw the three-scenario table — leaving the reaction force column blank. Fill in examples live as you discuss.

| Situation | Action force | Reaction force |
|---|---|---|
| You jump off the ground | You push **down** on the floor | Floor pushes **up** on you |
| You row a boat | Paddle pushes **back** on the water | Water pushes **forward** on the paddle |
| You lean on a wall | You push **toward** the wall | Wall pushes **back** on you |

**SAY:**
- "Let's go through each one. I want you to think of the reaction before I write it."
- Scenario 1: "You push down on the floor when you jump. What does the floor push?"
- Pause. Get responses. Write: "up on you."
- Scenario 2: "Paddle pushes backward on water. Water pushes..."
- Pause. Write: "forward on the paddle."
- Scenario 3: "You push toward the wall. Wall pushes..."
- Pause. Write: "back on you."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Complete your examples table — three scenarios, both forces."

---

**Beat 4 — Misconception 1: The heavier object always pushes harder**
**SHOW:** Board: Sketch: large truck and small bicycle. Label: collision.
**SAY:**
- "Here's something almost everyone gets wrong — and it's not obvious."
- "A truck hits a bicycle. Which object exerts a larger force on the other?"
- Pause for student responses. Most will say the truck.
- "Newton's Third Law says: the force the truck exerts on the bicycle is exactly equal to the force the bicycle exerts on the truck. Equal."
- "Then why does the bicycle get destroyed and not the truck?"
- Pause 4–5 seconds.
- "Mass. The same force on a small mass produces a large acceleration. On a large mass, a small acceleration. The forces are equal. The effects are not."
- "Newton's Third Law has no exceptions for size."

> **FLAG — Do not rush through this.** This is the most counterintuitive moment in the unit. Students who "understand" it in class will revert to "heavier = more force" on the test. Suggest using the spring scale: two students pull a shared spring scale from opposite ends — they will both read the same force, regardless of who is stronger. If time allows, demonstrate.

**CUE:** *This beat is conceptual — no note blank. Let students listen and discuss. Then:*
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Complete the truck/bicycle misconception box in your notes — why are the forces equal even though the effects are different?"

---

**Beat 5 — Misconception 2: Action and reaction forces cancel out**
**SHOW:** Board: Draw two separate figures — a person and the ground. Label force on person (ground pushes up on person). Label force on ground (person pushes down on ground). Circle each figure separately.
**SAY:**
- "Second misconception: if every force has an equal and opposite reaction, why does anything ever move?"
- "If I push right with 10 N and something pushes back left with 10 N — doesn't that cancel to zero?"
- Pause. Get student responses.
- "Look at the diagram. The force on you comes from the ground — it acts ON YOU. The force on the ground comes from you — it acts ON THE GROUND."
- "Forces only cancel when they act on the same object."
- "These two forces are on different objects. They have nothing to cancel with."
- "Your motion is governed by forces acting on you. The ground's motion is governed by forces acting on the ground."

> **FLAG — The diagram is doing the heavy lifting here.** The physical separation of the two circles (one for each object) makes the argument visual. Students who are still confused: have them name each force and name the object it acts ON. If they can do that, they understand why the forces don't cancel.

**CUE:** ★ Write: "Action-reaction forces cannot cancel because they act on **[different]** objects. Forces only cancel when they act on the **[same]** object."

---

**Beat 6 — Connection prompt: Swimming**
**SHOW:** Board: *"When you swim, you push backward against the water. What reaction force moves you forward? Where does it act, and on what object?"*
**SAY:**
- "Write the answer before we discuss. Think through the action-reaction pair."
- "Action force first. Then the reaction."
**CUE:** ★ Write: "Write your swimming answer in your notes — both forces, both objects — before reading ahead."

*Allow 60–90 seconds. Expected answer: you push backward on the water (action — acts on water); water pushes you forward (reaction — acts on you). Students who say "friction" or "the water resisting" are conflating this with Section 2. Redirect: "That's external forces from Section 2. In Section 5 we're identifying the action-reaction pair."*

### Section 5 — Retrieval Check Facilitation

**Before releasing:**
**CUE:** ↩ Check: "Notes closed. State the law. Identify action-reaction pairs. Explain why they don't cancel."

**Answer guide:**
1. When one object exerts a force on another, the second exerts a force on the first that is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction. (Key elements: equal, opposite, two objects.)
2. Action: you push forward on the cart. Reaction: the cart pushes backward on you. Two different objects.
3. Water pushes forward on the fish (reaction to fish's tail pushing backward on water).
4. The error: they act on different objects, so they cannot cancel. Forces cancel only when acting on the same object.
5. Pair 1: Earth's gravity pulls the book down (acts on book) → book's gravity pulls the Earth up (acts on Earth). Pair 2: Book pushes down on table (acts on table) → table pushes up on book (acts on book). *Students often get one pair and miss the other. The Earth pair surprises them — the book pulls the Earth. Credit any two correctly identified pairs with both forces and both objects named.*
