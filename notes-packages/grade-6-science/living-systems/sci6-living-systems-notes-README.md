# README — Living Systems Notes Package
`sci6-living-systems-notes-gr6-v1.md`

This document explains the design rationale behind every major decision in the notes package: why the sections are in this order, why these examples and contexts, how each section connects to the metacognition and evidence-based design frameworks in `_meta-context.md`.

---

## Section Order and Why It Matters

The four sections follow a deliberate conceptual sequence — not the curriculum's listed order of outcomes, but the order that builds the right schema at the right time.

### Section 1 first: Biotic and Abiotic (the container)
Students need a mental container — the idea of an ecosystem as an interacting whole — before they can understand any specific process within it. Starting with photosynthesis before ecosystems is like explaining how an engine works before establishing that a car has parts that work together.

The decomposer misconception is addressed here, not at the end, because it is a *structural* misconception: students who think decomposers are separate from the ecosystem will misread every food web they encounter. Correcting it early means every subsequent food web diagram lands correctly.

**Nuthall's three encounters applied:** Decomposers appear in Section 1.2 (first encounter — explanation), return in Section 3.1 when discussing soil nutrient cycling (second encounter — in context), and will appear again in the workbook's interleaved questions (third encounter — retrieval).

---

### Section 2 second: Photosynthesis (the engine)
Photosynthesis is the most important concept in this unit by importance score. It is the mechanism that makes all food webs possible — you cannot understand food chains without understanding where the energy in producers comes from. This is why it comes before the food web section, not after it.

**The misconception-first move (Section 2.1):** The package opens Section 2 with the soil misconception before teaching the correct process. This is intentional and backed by research on conceptual change (Chi, 2008; Posner et al., 1982): students who encounter a refutation before the explanation show better retention of the correct concept than those who receive the explanation first. The tree-in-a-pot thought experiment is a classic empirical refutation — it makes the misconception's prediction fail before a word of instruction is given.

**The Pre-Knowledge Check connection:** Students record their prior belief about where plants get food before any instruction. They are asked to revisit this on the final page. This is a deliberate metacognitive arc — the Zimmerman forethought → reflection loop stretched across the whole unit. Seeing their own wrong answer in their own handwriting at the end of the unit is a more powerful corrective experience than being told they were wrong by a teacher.

**Why the three experiments are in notes, not just in the lab:** The experiments are essential knowledge, not optional enrichment. A student who cannot explain the logic of the experiment — what was being tested, why the setup was designed that way, what the result tells us — has not understood photosynthesis, they have just watched it. The notes package requires students to fill in the experimental design and the meaning of the results, not just copy down "we did iodine."

**The respiration misconception (Section 2.6):** Placed after the photosynthesis content because students need to understand photosynthesis before they can understand why the "they cancel out" idea is wrong. The atmosphere argument (21% oxygen, built by photosynthesis over billions of years) gives students a concrete, scale-appropriate piece of evidence that outlasts the lesson.

---

### Section 3 third: Plant-Animal Relationships (the system)
Once students understand what an ecosystem is (Section 1) and what photosynthesis is (Section 2), they have the knowledge base to understand **why** plant-animal relationships are as deep as they are. Without Section 2, the statement "animals depend on plants for oxygen" is a fact to memorize. With Section 2, it is a logical consequence students can derive themselves.

**The Yellowstone case study:** Chosen for three reasons:
1. It is empirically grounded — this happened, was documented, is well-studied
2. It shows a trophic cascade affecting rivers and soil, not just other organisms — extends the idea of interdependence into abiotic components, looping back to Section 1
3. It is the exact kind of counter-intuitive, memorable example that Willingham identifies as necessary for lasting memory (the "hook" that the correct idea needs to compete with a student's prior belief)

**FNMI sacred plants:** These are placed in Section 3, not as an add-on or a separate "Indigenous content" section, because they belong here conceptually. The sacred plants are ecologically important species — their cultural significance and their scientific significance are both real and both worth understanding in relationship. Separating them would misrepresent both the knowledge and its holders.

The framing is explicit: traditional ecological knowledge is a valid scientific data source. This is not sentiment — it is methodologically true. Knowledge Keepers tracking plant health across decades have longitudinal data that academic science rarely achieves. The connection prompt asks students to think about what would be missed without this knowledge — a genuine epistemological question, not a box-checking one.

---

### Section 4 fourth: CS Connection (the abstraction layer)
CS comes last because:
1. The Scratch simulation makes no sense until students know what an ecosystem is and how it works
2. Conditional logic in code is most meaningful when the student already understands the conditional relationships in the actual ecosystem
3. Placing CS last means the abstraction concept has a full knowledge base to abstract from

The connection to Matter's abstraction lesson (flagged as optional, for classes that have taught Matter first) honours the silo design principle without requiring it. The note is invisible to students who haven't done Matter and useful for those who have.

## Format Decisions

### Guided notes with fill-in blanks (not Cornell or pure graphic organiser)
**Why not blank Cornell notes:** Grade 6 students at the start of instruction are novices. Blank note-taking formats impose dual cognitive load — students simultaneously try to figure out what to write and what it means. Cognitive load theory (Sweller, 1988) is clear: for novices, worked examples and guided structures outperform discovery formats. The fill-in structure reduces the extraneous load of format and directs attention to the content.

**Why not fully pre-filled notes:** Pre-filled notes produce passive reading, not active encoding. Willingham's principle — "memory is the residue of thought" — means students must do cognitive work to lay down memory. Blanks force students to hold information in working memory while writing, which is a retrieval event.

**The balance struck:** Key vocabulary and core concepts have blanks. Contextual framing, transitions, and misconception explanations are pre-written. This distributes the cognitive load: students think hardest about the precise content knowledge, not the scaffolding around it.

### Tables everywhere
Tables are used extensively because they serve a dual purpose: they structure information during encoding and they serve as ready-made retrieval frameworks afterward. A student who can reconstruct the photosynthesis table from memory understands the concept. A student who remembers "plants do photosynthesis" does not.

### Retrieval Check at every section end
Each section ends with 4–5 questions that students answer with notes covered. This is not assessment — it is the third part of the Zimmerman self-regulation loop (performance monitoring). The difficulty students experience at this stage is productive: it reveals gaps, motivates re-reading for specific information rather than vague re-exposure, and strengthens encoding through the testing effect (Roediger & Butler, 2011).

**The stretch question:** Each retrieval section includes one stretch question (marked explicitly) that requires transfer or multi-concept integration. This is for students who have consolidated the basic content and need a different challenge. It is not a faster path to the end — it is a different kind of thinking.

### Self-check rating after each section
Three-point self-check ("Yes, I could teach it / Getting there / Not yet") after each section builds metacognitive regulation (Zimmerman, Phase 1 and 3). The "I could teach it" standard is calibrated at a demanding level — being able to teach something requires a depth of understanding beyond being able to reproduce it. Students who set "could teach it" as their bar will push further into their notes than students who settle for "I think I get it."

**Attribution built into the reflection:** The unit synthesis page asks students to rate confidence *and* return to their Pre-Knowledge Check to see what changed. This surfaces the contrast between prior belief and current knowledge — exactly the metacognitive event that Flavell identifies as essential for students to update their self-model as a learner, not just their knowledge of the content.

## Vocabulary Design

Vocabulary boxes appear at the start of each section with three columns: term, "definition in your own words," example. This format is deliberate:

- **Definition in your own words** prevents copying (copying a definition from a textbook is a research-identified avoidance strategy — it produces familiarity, not knowledge)
- **One example** requires application at the moment of encoding, not after
- Vocabulary is introduced **in the context of its use**, not in a list before instruction — the box appears at the section start, to be filled as the student encounters each term in context

High-risk vocabulary (terms that overlap with everyday language) is flagged in teacher notes: *ecosystem* (students use it loosely), *food* (students conflate food with all nutrients), *energy* (students mean heat or activity, not stored chemical energy), *independent* (students think "independent of" means "not connected to"). These are the words most likely to produce confident-sounding wrong answers.

## Evidence Design Principles Checklist

Running the final checklist from `templates/evidence-design-principles.md`:

- [x] **Produce from memory at least once** — Retrieval Check after every section; Unit Synthesis from memory
- [x] **Explain or justify reasoning at least once** — "In your own words" prompts throughout; stretch retrieval questions; trophic cascade explanation; connection prompts
- [x] **Prior content mixed in** — Pre-Knowledge Check references prior knowledge; Section 4 explicitly connects to Matter's abstraction lesson (optional); Unit Synthesis loops back to the opening
- [x] **Formative signal** — Self-check rating after every section (teacher reads these); Pre-Knowledge Check revisit at end reveals belief change; retrieval checks surface gaps before the quiz
- [x] **Worked example before practice** — Photosynthesis equation is worked through fully (Section 2.2–2.3) before students are asked to use it in retrieval; food chain example given before students label the food web

**All five criteria met. Tier 1 standard — passes all five.**

## Misconception Tracking

| Misconception | Where addressed | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Plants get food from soil | Section 2.1 (explicit refutation first) + Pre-Knowledge Check revisit at end | Refutation before explanation; empirical evidence (tree-in-pot); student revisits own prior belief |
| Decomposers are not part of the ecosystem | Section 1.2 (callout box) + Section 3.1 (nutrient cycling context) | Consequence logic: "what would happen without them?" |
| Photosynthesis and respiration cancel out | Section 2.6 (after photosynthesis is understood) | Atmospheric evidence; quantitative reasoning about net effect |
| Animals are independent of plants beyond food | Section 3.1 (interdependence table) + Yellowstone case study | Table forces students to name specific non-food dependencies; case study shows what happens when dependencies break |

## What This Package Does Not Cover

- **Exit tickets:** These are separate artifacts (`sci6-living-systems-exits-v1.md`) — one per lesson, collected daily, not part of the notes package
- **Inquiry project:** Separate brief (`sci6-living-systems-project-v1.md`) — launches after Section 3 is complete
- **Workbook:** Separate interleaved practice set (`sci6-living-systems-workbook-v1.md`) — runs in parallel with instruction, not instead of notes
- **The three experiments in full:** Lab procedures are in teacher guide and separate lab sheets — the notes package covers the *logic* of each experiment, not the step-by-step procedure
