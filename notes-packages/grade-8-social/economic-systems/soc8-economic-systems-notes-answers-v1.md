# Economic Systems — Notes Answer Key
Grade 8 Social Studies · Teacher Copy — Not for Students

This key covers three things for each section: (1) fill-in-blank answers, (2) retrieval check model answers, and (3) misconception target labels. A high-risk vocabulary flag table for the whole unit is at the end.

## Section 1 — Scarcity and the Three Big Questions

**Misconception target:** none dedicated to this section (this section is the container — misconceptions begin in Section 2).

### Fill-in-blank answers

| Blank / Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| This problem is called ___ | scarcity |
| Because resources are ___, no economy can produce ___ | limited · everything |
| This is called ___ (opportunity cost) | opportunity cost |
| Library is the ___ of that choice | opportunity cost |

### Retrieval check — model answers

1. **Define scarcity in your own words.** The idea that there are never enough resources (land, workers, tools, money, time) to give everyone everything they want.
2. **What are the three basic economic questions?** What to produce, how to produce it, and for whom to produce it.
3. **What is opportunity cost? Give an example.** The next-best thing you give up when you choose one option over another. Example: choosing to spend an hour on homework means giving up an hour of free time.
4. **Application — the farmer's field.** She has a limited resource (the field). Growing wheat means she cannot also grow corn on that same land at the same time — scarcity forces her to choose one use of the field over the other.
5. **★ Stretch — the city's buses.** The city must decide *what* to produce (more buses vs. lower fares), effectively *for whom* (riders who need more service vs. riders who need affordability), using its limited budget. Whichever it picks, the other option is the opportunity cost — e.g., if it buys more buses, cheaper fares are what it gives up.

## Section 2 — Types of Economic Systems and the Economic Spectrum

**Misconception target:** [misconception target: pure market/command economies] — "Real-world economies are 'pure' market or command systems rather than mixed."

### Fill-in-blank answers

| Blank / Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| The plan a country uses to answer them is called an ___ | economic system |
| Most real economies are ___ | mixed |
| One end: little government control — a ___ economy | market |
| Other end: government decides almost everything — a ___ economy | command |
| Most countries sit in the ___ | middle |
| Command — who decides? | The government |
| Market — through buying, selling, and ___ | competition |
| Mixed — a blend of individuals, businesses, and ___ | government |
| Market economy — who owns businesses? | Private |
| Command economy — who owns businesses? | The government |

### Retrieval check — model answers

1. **What is an economic system?** The plan or set of rules a country uses to decide what, how, and for whom to produce.
2. **Name the four types and who decides.** Traditional (custom/tradition), Command (the government), Market (individuals and businesses through buying/selling), Mixed (a blend of individuals, businesses, and government).
3. **Error analysis — "Canada is a pure market economy."** Canada has government-run programs too (public healthcare, public education, social programs), so it is not purely a market economy — it is mixed.
4. **Application — government-owned factories, almost no private business.** This country sits near the command end of the spectrum, since the government makes almost all the production decisions.
5. **★ Stretch — no pure economies exist.** Every real country blends some government involvement with some individual/business choice, even ones usually labelled "market" (e.g., the U.S. has public schools) or "command" (e.g., China now allows private business). Accept any accurate real-world example.

## Section 3 — Measuring an Economy

**Misconception target:** none of the four listed misconceptions is dedicated here; this section builds the comparison tools used in Sections 4–5. Flag for teacher: watch for students treating GDP alone as a complete measure of economic health (addressed directly in retrieval Q3 and the Stretch question).

### Fill-in-blank answers

| Blank / Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| GDP growing → economy producing ___ than before | more |
| CPI rises quickly → there is ___ | inflation |
| GDP rising → economy is ___ | growing |
| CPI rising → ___ — money buys less | inflation |
| Unemployment rate rising → economy may be ___ | struggling |

### Retrieval check — model answers

1. **What does GDP measure?** The total dollar value of all goods and services a country produces in one year.
2. **Relationship between rising CPI and inflation.** A rising CPI (average prices of a basket of goods going up) is what inflation means — money buys less than it used to.
3. **Why look at all three together?** No single measure tells the whole story — e.g., GDP can grow while unemployment also rises, so looking at all three gives a fuller picture of who is actually benefiting.
4. **Application — GDP up, unemployment also up.** This could mean the growth is coming from fewer, more productive workers or automation rather than new jobs — ordinary workers may not be sharing in the benefit.
5. **★ Stretch — questioning the GDP headline.** Accept answers that ask: Is unemployment also changing? Is CPI/inflation rising too (so is the value of that growth being eaten by higher prices)? Is the GDP growth spread across the population or concentrated among a few?

## Section 4 — Government Revenue and Spending

**Misconception target:** [misconception target: taxes fund only one thing] — "Taxes only fund one visible thing (e.g., 'roads') rather than a broad basket of services."

### Fill-in-blank answers

| Blank / Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| Money a government collects is called ___ | revenue |
| Most government revenue comes from ___ | taxes / taxation |
| Healthcare pays for hospitals, doctors, ___ | medicine / medical equipment / mental health services (any reasonable healthcare item) |
| Education pays for schools, teachers, ___ | textbooks / school supplies / busing (any reasonable education item) |
| Infrastructure pays for roads, bridges, ___ | water systems / public transit / power grids (any reasonable infrastructure item) |
| Social programs support people who need help, such as ___ | income assistance / unemployment support / disability support / seniors' benefits |
| Public safety pays for police, firefighters, ___ | emergency services / courts / corrections |

### Retrieval check — model answers

1. **Where does most government revenue come from?** Taxes.
2. **Name three things tax dollars pay for.** Any three of: healthcare, education, infrastructure, social programs, public safety.
3. **Error analysis — "taxes just pay for roads."** Roads are only one small category. Taxes also fund healthcare, schools, social programs, and public safety — without tax revenue, all of these would lose funding, not just roads.
4. **Application — town raises taxes to build a hospital wing.** The tax increase is new revenue; the hospital wing is the spending choice made with that revenue — revenue and spending working together to expand a public service.
5. **★ Stretch — infrastructure vs. social programs trade-off.** Accept answers that identify this as an opportunity cost: spending more on one category means less money is available for the other, and reasonable people can disagree about which need is more urgent right now.

## Section 5 — Surplus, Deficit, and Debt

**Misconception targets:**
- [misconception target: deficit = debt] — "Deficit and debt are the same thing."
- [misconception target: deficit always bad] — "A deficit is always bad economic management, never a deliberate choice."

### Fill-in-blank answers

| Blank / Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| This year's deficit = $___ billion | 8 |
| Total debt is now $___ billion | 58 |

### Retrieval check — model answers

1. **Define surplus, deficit, and debt.** Surplus = revenue greater than spending in one year. Deficit = spending greater than revenue in one year. Debt = the running total of all past deficits (minus surpluses) added together over time.
2. **Calculation — $90B revenue, $95B spending, $40B prior debt.** This year's deficit = $5 billion. New total debt = $45 billion.
3. **Error analysis — "a deficit means bad management."** Not always true — governments sometimes run deficits on purpose, such as during an emergency or to invest in long-term infrastructure. Whether it is good or bad management depends on the reason and how the money is used, not just the fact that a deficit occurred.
4. **Application — flood recovery deficit.** This is a reasonable use of a deficit because the spending responds to an emergency that could not be predicted or delayed — rebuilding homes and roads quickly protects people and the local economy, which can be worth the short-term cost.
5. **★ Stretch — 15 years of deficits vs. one emergency year.** A single emergency-year deficit is a planned, temporary response with a clear reason. Fifteen years of deficits with no plan means debt keeps growing every year, so a larger and larger share of revenue must go to interest payments on that debt — leaving progressively less money for healthcare, education, and other services. The risk is the long, unplanned pattern, not the existence of any one deficit.

## High-Risk Vocabulary Flags

Terms students are likely to think they already understand from everyday use — flag these for extra attention, since prior (often inaccurate) informal meanings can interfere with the precise economic definition.

| Term | Everyday meaning students may bring | Precise meaning required in this unit |
|---|---|---|
| **Deficit** | Vaguely "bad" or "a problem," often used interchangeably with debt in the news and in casual conversation | A specific one-year gap where spending exceeds revenue — not inherently bad, and not the same as debt |
| **Debt** | "Owing money," often used interchangeably with deficit | The accumulated total of past deficits (minus surpluses) built up over multiple years |
| **Market** | Often means a physical place to buy things (a farmers' market) | An economic system where individuals and businesses, not government, make most production decisions |
| **Revenue** | Rarely used in casual speech; when it is, often confused with profit | Total money a government (or business) collects, before subtracting spending |
| **Investment** (used in Section 5's "invest long-term" framing) | Often means only personal investing (stocks) | Government spending now (often via a deficit) intended to produce a future benefit, e.g., infrastructure |
| **Economy** | Vague, often just "how much money there is" or "the stock market" | The whole system of how a society produces, measures, and distributes goods and services |
