### Notes

No country has enough of everything for everyone. Land, workers, tools, money, and time are all limited. This problem is called _____________.

Because of scarcity, every group of people — a family, a business, a whole country — must make choices. Every economy has to answer three questions:

1. **What** to produce? (Which goods and services should we make with our limited resources?)
2. **How** to produce it? (Who does the work? What tools or methods do we use?)
3. **For whom** to produce it? (Who gets the goods and services once they are made?)

Because resources are _____________, no economy can produce _____________ that people want.

> **A resource can be:** land, labour (workers), capital (money and tools), or time. All of these are limited — that is what makes them resources instead of an unlimited supply.

When you choose to use a limited resource one way, you give up the chance to use it another way. This is called _____________.

**Worked example:** A town has enough money to build a new hockey rink OR a new library — not both. If the town builds the rink, the library is the _____________ of that choice.

**Connection:** Think of a choice you made this week where picking one thing meant giving up another (spending money, choosing an activity). What did you give up?

### Notes

Every country must answer the three economic questions from Section 1. The plan a country uses to answer them is called an _____________.

> **MISCONCEPTION:** "Every country is either a total market economy or a total command economy. There is nothing in between."
>
> Why this fails: No real country works this way. The United States is often called a market economy — but it still runs public schools and programs like Social Security. China is a command economy in many ways — but it now allows many private businesses. If you tried to sort every real country into "pure market" or "pure command," almost none would fit cleanly into either box.
>
> **Correct understanding:** Most real economies are _____________ — a blend of individual choice and government control. They sit somewhere along a spectrum, not at one extreme end.

**The economic spectrum** is a line. It shows who decides, and how much control the government holds.

- One end: individuals and businesses decide almost everything. Little government control. This is a _____________ economy.
- The other end: the government decides almost everything. This is a _____________ economy.
- Most real countries sit somewhere in the _____________ — a mixed economy.

### Notes

Knowing what type of economic system a country has is not enough. We also need a way to check how well its economy is actually doing. Economists use three main tools.

**Tool 1 — GDP:** the total dollar value of all goods and services a country produces in one year. When GDP is growing, the economy is producing _____________ than before.

**Tool 2 — CPI:** tracks how the price of a "basket" of common goods and services changes over time. When CPI rises quickly, we say there is _____________ — prices are going up and money buys less than it used to.

**Tool 3 — Unemployment rate:** the percentage of people who want a job and are looking for one, but do not have one.

**Complete the table:**

| Measure | What it tracks | What a rising number usually means |
|---|---|---|
| GDP | Total value of goods and services made in a year | The economy is _____________ |
| CPI | Average prices of common goods and services | _____________ — money buys less |
| Unemployment rate | % of people who want work but don't have it | The economy may be _____________ |

> No single number tells the whole story. A country can have a growing GDP and still have high unemployment. Economists look at all three measures together, not just one.

**Worked example — compare two countries:**

| | Country A | Country B |
|---|---|---|
| GDP growth this year | 3% | 1% |
| Unemployment rate | 5% | 9% |
| CPI change (inflation) | 2% | 6% |

Which country's economy looks healthier right now? Explain using all three measures.

## Section 5: Surplus, Deficit, and Debt

**Learning target:** I can explain the difference between a deficit and debt, and describe why governments sometimes choose to run a deficit on purpose.

<!-- Implications section — the direct consequence of the revenue/spending mechanics from Section 4. Least-scaffolded section: fewer blanks, more full written explanation, per the progressive-blanking fade. -->

### Vocabulary

| Term | Definition in your own words | One example |
|---|---|---|
| Surplus | | |
| Deficit | | |
| Debt | | |
| Balanced budget | | |

### Notes

Every year, a government compares what it collected in revenue to what it spent. Three things can happen:

- **Surplus:** revenue is greater than spending — money is left over.
- **Balanced budget:** revenue equals spending exactly.
- **Deficit:** spending is greater than revenue — there is a shortfall.

> **MISCONCEPTION:** "A deficit and debt are the same thing."
>
> Why this fails: A deficit is what happens in *one single year* — when a government spends more than it collects. Debt is the *total amount built up* from every year the government has spent more than it collected, added together over time. Mixing them up is like confusing "I spent $50 more than I earned this month" with "I owe $2,000 total, added up from every month I've overspent for the last few years."
>
> **Correct understanding:** A deficit is one year's gap between spending and revenue. Debt is the running total of every past deficit added together (minus any surpluses that helped pay some of it down).

**Worked example:** A government collects $100 billion and spends $108 billion this year.

This year's deficit = $_____________ billion.

If the government already owed $50 billion from past years, its total debt is now $_____________ billion.

> **MISCONCEPTION:** "Running a deficit always means the government is being careless with money."
>
> Why this fails: Sometimes governments plan a deficit on purpose. During an emergency — a pandemic, a flood, a recession — a government may need to spend more than it collects right away, to help people and businesses survive. Governments also sometimes borrow money to build things like hospitals or transit systems that will help the economy for decades — an investment now that is expected to pay off later. These are deliberate choices, not always mistakes.
>
> **Correct understanding:** A deficit can be a planned, reasonable choice, depending on why it happens and what the money is used for. It becomes a real problem mainly when debt grows so large, for so long, that it limits what future governments can afford to do.

Write, in your own words, why a government running a deficit is not automatically a sign of bad management:

_______________________________________________

_______________________________________________

> A government that runs a deficit every single year, with no plan to ever balance the budget, keeps adding to its debt. Over time, more of its revenue must go toward *interest payments* on that debt — leaving less money for services like healthcare and education. That growing interest cost is the real long-term risk of debt, not the existence of any one year's deficit.
