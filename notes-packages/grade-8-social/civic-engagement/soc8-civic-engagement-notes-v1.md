## Section 1: Engaged Citizenship — What Does It Mean to Be Involved?

**Learning target:** I can explain the three parts of engaged citizenship — awareness, decision-making, and action — and use them to describe a real example.

### Vocabulary

| Term | Definition in your own words | One example |
|---|---|---|
| Citizen | | |
| Civic engagement | | |
| Democratic citizenship | | |
| Awareness | | |
| Issue | | |

### Notes

A **citizen** is a person who legally belongs to a country. Being a citizen comes with **rights** (like voting) and **responsibilities** (like following laws and staying informed).

**Democratic citizenship** means more than just living in a democracy. It means being an active member of one. A democratic citizen does not just follow the rules — they help shape them.

**Civic engagement** is any action a person takes to help their community, their country, or the world. Civic engagement is how democratic citizenship actually happens — it is citizenship in action.

Engaged citizenship has three parts. Each part needs the one before it.

```
   AWARENESS  ─────────────►  DECISION-MAKING  ─────────────►  ACTION
 (noticing and                (deciding what you              (actually doing
  learning about               think should happen,             something about
  an issue)                    and what you could do)            the issue)
```

An **issue** is a problem or a question that affects a group of people and does not have one easy answer. Issues can be local (your school or town), national (all of Canada), or global (the whole world).

**A real example — read this story, then fill in the blanks:**

Malik walks past Riverbend Park every day on his way to school. He notices that the playground equipment is cracked and unsafe. This is Malik becoming _____________ of the issue.

Malik learns that the town council controls the parks budget. He decides that the playground should be fixed, and he decides that writing to the council is worth trying. This is Malik's _____________.

Malik and two friends write a letter to the town council and start a petition at school. This is Malik taking _____________.

<div class="note-box">Notice that Malik's story has all three parts. A person who only notices a problem (awareness) but never decides what to do or acts on it has not yet reached engaged citizenship — they are still at the first step.</div>

## Section 2: Forms of Civic Engagement — Ways to Get Involved

**Learning target:** I can name different forms of civic engagement and explain why most of them do not require being an adult.

### Vocabulary

| Term | Definition in your own words | One example |
|---|---|---|
| Volunteer | | |
| Community initiative | | |
| Advocacy | | |
| Rally / protest | | |
| Ethical purchasing | | |
| Political party membership | | |
| Riding (constituency) association | | |

<div class="misconception">

**MISCONCEPTION:** "The only real way to help your community or country is to vote."

**Why this fails:** Riverbend's town council is deciding whether to fix the playground next month. The next election is not for two more years. If voting were the only real way to influence a decision, nobody could do anything about the playground until then — and by then, the decision will already be made. But Malik did not wait. He wrote a letter and started a petition. Voting also is not available to everyone: most students in this class cannot vote yet. If voting were the only meaningful action, none of them could be engaged citizens right now. That cannot be right, since young people influence issues all the time.

**Correct understanding:** Voting is one important form of civic engagement — but it is only one of many. The list below shows several others.

</div>

<div class="misconception">

**MISCONCEPTION:** "You have to be an adult (18 or older) to really get involved in issues."

**Why this fails:** Look at the list of forms of civic engagement below. Check which ones have an age requirement written next to them. Only one form on the entire list requires being 18 — voting, which you will learn about in the next section. Every other form on this list is open to a person of any age, including students in this class right now.

**Correct understanding:** Age is a barrier for voting specifically, not for civic engagement in general. Most forms of civic engagement are open to anyone who wants to take part.

</div>

### Notes

Here are the main forms of civic engagement named in this course. As you read each one, notice: **does it require being 18 or older?**

**Volunteering and joining service groups.** A **volunteer** is a person who works to help others without being paid. Example: helping at a food bank, or joining a community cleanup crew. *No age requirement.*

**Participating in community initiatives.** This means getting involved in a project your local community is running — like attending a town hall meeting, or joining a youth advisory council that gives input to the town. *No age requirement.*

**Attending rallies or protests.** A **rally** (or protest) is a public gathering where people show support for, or opposition to, an issue. Example: students at Riverbend Middle organized a rally asking the school board for more bike racks. *No age requirement.*

**Advocacy.** **Advocacy** means speaking up or writing to try to persuade people in power to make a change. Example: writing a letter to a town councillor, or speaking at a public meeting — exactly what Malik did in Section 1. *No age requirement.*

**Ethical purchasing decisions.** This means choosing what to buy (or not buy) based on how a company treats workers, animals, or the environment. Example: choosing to buy from Bright Threads, a clothing company that pays its factory workers a fair wage, instead of a competitor that does not. *No age requirement.*

**Political party membership and joining a riding (constituency) association.** A **riding**, also called a **constituency**, is the specific area where a person lives that elects one representative. Most major political parties let anyone become a member, including youth members, and each riding usually has a local riding association that plans and organizes locally. Example: joining the youth wing of a party, like the fictional Common Ground Party. *No age requirement in most parties — check the specific party's rules.*

**Election volunteering.** This means helping a candidate's campaign — door knocking, delivering flyers, making phone calls, preparing mailers, or entering data. Example: helping Amara Osei's campaign for Riverbend town council by knocking on doors in the neighbourhood. *No age requirement.*

<div class="note-box">Out of every form listed here, only <strong>voting</strong> has an age requirement. That is worth remembering going into the next section.</div>

## Section 3: Voting — Your Right and Responsibility

**Learning target:** I can list the four requirements to vote in Canada and explain why voting is a responsibility of democratic citizenship.

### Vocabulary

| Term | Definition in your own words | One example |
|---|---|---|
| Voter eligibility | | |
| Proof of residence | | |
| Proof of identity | | |
| Ballot | | |

<div class="note-box">Look back at Section 2. Voting was the one form of civic engagement with an age requirement. This section explains exactly what that requirement is — and what else is required.</div>

### Notes

**Voting** is one of the most basic responsibilities of democratic citizenship. When citizens vote, they choose who represents them and helps make decisions on their behalf.

To vote in a Canadian election, a person must meet **all four** of these requirements. This is called **voter eligibility**.

| Requirement | What it means |
|---|---|
| Be a **Canadian citizen** | Permanent residents and visitors cannot vote in Canadian elections, even if they live here |
| Be **at least 18 years old** on election day | This is the only age-based rule in this whole unit |
| Have valid **proof of residence** in the riding | You must show you live in the riding (constituency) where you are voting |
| Have valid **proof of identity** | A document like a driver's licence or other approved ID |

A person casts their vote on a **ballot** — the official paper or method used to record a choice in an election.

**Check each person against the four requirements:**

| Person | Citizen? | 18+? | Proof of residence? | Proof of ID? | Can vote? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deng, age 19, Canadian citizen, has ID, lives in the riding | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes | _____________ |
| Sofia, age 16, Canadian citizen, has ID, lives in the riding | Yes | _____________ | Yes | Yes | _____________ |
| Liam, age 20, permanent resident (not yet a citizen), has ID | _____________ | Yes | Yes | Yes | _____________ |

<div class="misconception">

**MISCONCEPTION:** "If someone meets most of the requirements, they should still be allowed to vote."

**Why this fails:** Look at Sofia and Liam in the table above. Sofia meets three of the four requirements — she is missing only age. Liam also meets three of the four — he is missing only citizenship. If "most" were good enough, both of them could vote. But Canadian law requires **all four**, with no exceptions. Missing even one requirement means a person cannot vote in that election.

**Correct understanding:** All four voter eligibility requirements must be met at the same time. There is no partial credit for voting.

</div>

### Vocabulary

| Term | Definition in your own words | One example |
|---|---|---|
| Global issue | | |
| Economic disruption | | |
| Drought and famine | | |
| Human migration | | |
| Civil unrest | | |
| War | | |

<div class="misconception">

**MISCONCEPTION:** "Global issues like migration, drought, or conflict happen far away and don't really affect life in Canada."

**Why this fails:** A drought overseas shrinks a country's wheat harvest. Wheat is bought and sold around the world, so a smaller harvest pushes the global price of wheat up — including the price Canadian grocery stores pay. Your family's bread costs more because of a drought you never even heard about. Or consider migration: in 1979, war and hardship pushed many people to leave Vietnam, often by boat. Canada welcomed close to 60,000 Vietnamese refugees over the next few years. Many settled in cities across the country, started businesses, and raised families. Their children and grandchildren are Canadian citizens today — they are neighbours, classmates, and coworkers. That is not "far away." That is Canada.

**Correct understanding:** Global issues connect to Canada in real, traceable ways — through the price of goods, through the people who come here, and through Canada's role in responding to problems that cross borders.

</div>

### Notes

A **global issue** is a problem big enough, or connected enough, to affect people in more than one country. This course names six global issues that affect life in Canada:

| Global issue | What it means | How it can reach Canada |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic disruption** | A sudden shock to trade, jobs, or prices | Disruptions elsewhere can raise or lower prices of goods Canadians buy |
| **Poverty** | Not having enough money or resources to meet basic needs | Global poverty affects trade partners and can drive migration |
| **Drought and famine** | Long dry periods and severe food shortages | Can shrink global food supply and raise grocery prices in Canada |
| **Human migration** | People moving from one country or region to another | People migrate to Canada, becoming new community members, workers, and citizens |
| **Civil unrest** | Widespread public disorder or conflict within a country | Can disrupt trade routes and supply chains that reach Canada |
| **War** | Large-scale armed conflict between groups or countries | Can disrupt trade, raise energy and food prices, and cause migration |

**Complete the chain below to see how one global issue can reach a Canadian grocery store:**

```
  A drought overseas    →     A smaller wheat      →     The global price     →     ______________________
  shrinks the wheat           harvest that year           of wheat goes up            (fill in what happens
  crop                                                                                 in a Canadian store)
```

<div class="note-box">Not every global issue reaches Canada the same way. Some raise prices (economic disruption, drought/famine). Some bring new community members (migration). Some do both, plus disrupt the flow of goods (civil unrest, war). Poverty is often connected to more than one of the others at once.</div>
