# Civic Engagement — Notes Fill-In Answer Key
Grade 8 Social Studies · Teacher Copy — Not for Students

## Section 1 — Engaged Citizenship

| Blank / Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| Malik notices cracked playground equipment — this is Malik becoming ___ | aware |
| Malik decides the playground should be fixed and writing is worth trying — this is his ___ | decision-making |
| Malik and friends write a letter and start a petition — this is Malik taking ___ | action |
| Engaged citizenship starts with ___ | awareness |
| Next comes ___ | decision-making |
| It finishes with ___ | action |
| Retrieval Q1 (three parts, in order) | Awareness → Decision-making → Action |
| Retrieval Q2 (citizen vs. engaged citizen) | A citizen simply belongs to a country legally. An engaged citizen actively notices issues, decides what they think, and takes action — citizenship in action, not just status. |
| Retrieval Q3 (why awareness comes first) | You cannot decide what you think about an issue, or act on it effectively, until you understand what the issue actually is. Acting without awareness risks acting on the wrong information. |
| Retrieval Q4 (Priya, recycling program) | She should decide what she thinks should happen and what action she could take — e.g., talk to the school, start a petition, write to the principal — then actually do it to reach the action stage. |
| Retrieval Q5 (Stretch — "I already know about climate change") | Knowing about an issue is only awareness — the first of three parts. Being an engaged citizen also requires deciding what you think should be done (decision-making) and actually doing something (action). Knowledge alone is not engagement. |

**High-risk vocabulary flag:** "Citizen" — students often think of it only as a legal/immigration status word. Make sure they also grasp "democratic citizenship" as an active role, not just a label. "Awareness" — students may conflate simply hearing about something in passing with genuine awareness (understanding why it matters); push for the deeper sense in retrieval marking.

## Section 2 — Forms of Civic Engagement

| Blank / Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| ___ means speaking or writing to persuade people in power | Advocacy |
| ___ means choosing what to buy based on how a company behaves | Ethical purchasing |
| A ___ is the area where you live that elects one representative | riding / constituency |
| Retrieval Q1 (three forms not requiring 18+) | Any three of: volunteering, community initiatives, rallies/protests, advocacy, ethical purchasing, political party membership, election volunteering |
| Retrieval Q2 (advocacy vs. rally/protest) | Advocacy is persuading people in power through speech or writing (e.g., a letter). A rally/protest is a public gathering that shows support or opposition, often to draw attention to an issue. Advocacy can happen quietly; a rally is public and collective. |
| Retrieval Q3 (define riding/constituency) | The specific area where a person lives that elects one representative to speak for it. |
| Retrieval Q4 (12-year-old, wetland) | Any two of: joining/starting a community initiative or petition, advocacy (writing to the town council), attending a rally, volunteering with an environmental group. Voting is NOT acceptable — she cannot vote. |
| Retrieval Q5 (Stretch — "nothing I can do without voting") | Response should name at least two non-voting forms (e.g., advocacy and volunteering, or a rally and ethical purchasing) and note that none of these require being 18 — the student is already able to act. |

**Misconception target labels:**
- [misconception target: voting is the only meaningful form of civic engagement] — refutation-first callout at the start of Section 2, before the forms list.
- [misconception target: civic engagement requires being 18+] — second refutation-first callout, same location, immediately after the first.

**High-risk vocabulary flag:** "Advocacy" — an unfamiliar academic word for most students; watch for confusion with "advertising." "Riding" and "constituency" — students may think these are two different things rather than two names for the same concept; the notes explicitly present them as synonyms. "Ethical purchasing" — students may reduce this to "buying expensive/eco brands" rather than understanding the underlying idea of consumer choice as a form of influence.

## Section 3 — Voting

| Blank / Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| Deng (19, citizen, ID, resident) — Can vote? | Yes — meets all four requirements |
| Sofia (16, citizen, ID, resident) — 18+? | No |
| Sofia — Can vote? | No — fails the age requirement only |
| Liam (20, permanent resident, ID) — Citizen? | No |
| Liam — Can vote? | No — fails the citizenship requirement only |
| Retrieval Q1 (four requirements) | Canadian citizen; at least 18 years old; valid proof of residence in the riding; valid proof of identity |
| Retrieval Q2 (define riding/constituency) | The specific area where a person lives; each riding elects one representative. |
| Retrieval Q3 (citizen + 18+ but still can't vote) | They might be missing proof of residence in that specific riding (e.g., moved recently and haven't updated it) or valid proof of identity. |
| Retrieval Q4 (17-year-old turning 18 on election day) | Yes — they meet the age requirement because they turn 18 on election day itself, and they already meet the other three requirements. |
| Retrieval Q5 (Stretch — compare voting to another form) | Voting is the only form with an age requirement (18+) and requires specific proof (residence + ID) tied to one riding. Other forms, like advocacy or volunteering, have no age or documentation requirements and are not tied to a specific riding. |

**Misconception target label:** [misconception target: meeting "most" of the voter eligibility requirements should be enough] — refutation-first callout after the eligibility table, using Sofia and Liam as near-miss cases.

**High-risk vocabulary flag:** "Proof of residence" vs. "proof of identity" — students often merge these into one idea ("ID"). Clarify: proof of identity confirms who you are; proof of residence confirms where you live, specifically within the riding.

## Section 4 — Global Issues

| Blank / Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chain: drought → smaller harvest → higher global wheat price → ___ | Higher bread/food prices in Canadian grocery stores |
| Retrieval Q1 (six global issues) | Economic disruption, poverty, drought and famine, human migration, civil unrest, war |
| Retrieval Q2 (drought → bread price chain) | A drought overseas shrinks that country's wheat harvest → less wheat is available worldwide → the global price of wheat rises → Canadian stores pay more for wheat/flour → bread costs more for Canadian shoppers. |
| Retrieval Q3 (real historical migration example) | The Vietnamese refugee resettlement beginning in 1979 — Canada welcomed close to 60,000 refugees who went on to become Canadian citizens, business owners, and community members. |
| Retrieval Q4 (civil conflict disrupts shipping route) | Goods may take longer or cost more to ship to Canada, which could raise prices or create shortages of certain products for Canadian consumers. |
| Retrieval Q5 (Stretch — "global issues are the government's problem") | Not entirely true — using Sections 1–2, an individual can still build awareness of a global issue, decide what they think, and act through forms like advocacy, ethical purchasing (e.g., choosing fair-trade goods), volunteering with a resettlement organization, or joining a community initiative that supports newcomers. |

**Misconception target label:** [misconception target: global issues are disconnected from daily life in Canada] — refutation-first callout at the start of Section 4, before the issue-by-issue content, using the wheat-price chain and the 1979 Vietnamese resettlement as evidence.

**High-risk vocabulary flag:** "Migration" — students may only associate this with crisis/negative framing; the 1979 example is used deliberately to show migration as also being a positive, historically celebrated part of Canadian civic life. "Civil unrest" and "war" — students may treat these as identical; keep the distinction that civil unrest is internal disorder within a country, while war is armed conflict (which can be between or within countries).

## Unit Synthesis — Suggested Answers

- **Engaged citizenship:** Being an engaged citizen means moving through three connected steps — becoming aware of an issue, deciding what you think and what could be done, and taking action.
- **Forms of civic engagement:** Civic engagement includes many forms beyond voting — volunteering, community initiatives, rallies/protests, advocacy, ethical purchasing, party membership, and election volunteering — and only voting requires being 18+.
- **Voting and voter eligibility:** Voting is a key responsibility of democratic citizenship; to vote in Canada, a person must be a citizen, at least 18, and have proof of both residence in their riding and identity.
- **Global issues affecting Canada:** Global issues — economic disruption, poverty, drought/famine, migration, civil unrest, and war — affect Canada through trade and prices, through migration and new community members, and through disrupted supply chains.
- **Three forms not requiring 18+:** Any three of volunteering, community initiatives, rallies/protests, advocacy, ethical purchasing, party membership, election volunteering.
- **Four voter eligibility requirements:** Canadian citizen; at least 18; proof of residence in the riding; proof of identity.
