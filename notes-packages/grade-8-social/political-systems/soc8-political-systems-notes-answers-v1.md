# Political Systems — Notes Fill-In Answer Key
Grade 8 Social Studies · Teacher Copy — Not for Students

## Section 1 — Types of Political Systems

| Blank / Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| A dictatorship is a system where ___ person holds total control | one |
| An oligarchy is a system where a small ___ of people hold power | group |
| A democracy is a system where citizens elect people to ___ them | represent |
| Anarchy means little or no ___ authority | central / centralized |
| Retrieval Q1: order most → least control | Dictatorship → Oligarchy → Monarchy → Democracy (anarchy is the "least control" endpoint, not itself part of this four-item order) |
| Retrieval Q4 (council of ten wealthy owners, no elections) | Oligarchy — small group, not one person, holds power without elections |
| Retrieval Q5 (Stretch — constitutional monarchy) | This is a constitutional monarchy: a democracy with a ceremonial monarch. It's hard to place in one box because it blends features of monarchy (a king/queen exists) and democracy (elected reps actually make the laws) |

**High-risk vocabulary flag:** "Democracy" and "monarchy" — students often think these are mutually exclusive. Flag that a country (e.g., the UK, Canada) can have both a monarch AND be a functioning democracy at the same time; the two are not opposites once you separate ceremonial role from real political power. Also flag "anarchy" — students confuse it with chaos/violence in casual usage; keep the definition strictly to "absence of centralized government authority."

## Section 2 — Models of Democracy

| Blank / Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| Direct democracy: citizens vote ___ on laws | directly |
| Representative democracy: citizens elect people to ___ them | represent |
| Comparison table — who votes on laws (direct) | All citizens, directly |
| Comparison table — who votes on laws (representative) | Elected representatives, on behalf of citizens |
| Comparison table — representative democracy best suited to | Large populations; national-level decisions |
| Comparison table — Canadian example (representative) | Federal and provincial general elections |
| Retrieval Q4 (small town of 800, new park) | Direct approach makes sense — small population, single local issue, easy to gather everyone's input |
| Retrieval Q5 (Stretch — "not a real democracy" claim) | The claim confuses direct and representative democracy. Canada IS a democracy — citizens elect representatives who vote on laws on their behalf. Not voting directly on every law does not make a system undemocratic; it makes it a representative democracy, which is the realistic model for a country of 40+ million people |

**Misconception target label:** [misconception target: democracy = one single system] — appears as refutation-first callout at the start of Section 2, before direct/representative are explained.

**High-risk vocabulary flag:** "Referendum" — students may confuse with a regular election. Clarify: a referendum is a vote on one specific question or law, not a vote for a person/representative.

## Section 3 — Parliamentary vs. Presidential Democracies

| Blank / Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| PM elected as leader by their own ___ first | party / caucus |
| President elected ___, in a nationwide vote | directly |
| Comparison table — Leader title (Presidential) | President |
| Retrieval Q1 (how PM vs President are chosen) | PM: becomes leader through party leadership, then PM only if their party wins the most seats. President: elected directly, nationwide, by all voters |
| Retrieval Q2 (two parliamentary terms) | Any two of: caucus (all elected members of a party), backbencher (MP without a Cabinet post), frontbencher (MP with a Cabinet post), whip (ensures party members vote as directed), Leader of the Opposition (leader of the second-largest party), shadow cabinet (opposition critics assigned to each government minister) |
| Retrieval Q3 (two presidential terms) | Any two of: Congress (the elected legislature), veto (President's power to reject a bill), elected Senate (unlike Canada's appointed Senate), elected judiciary (unlike Canada's appointed judges) |
| Retrieval Q4 (veto/override example) | The President rejected the bill using the veto power; Congress then voted again and got enough votes to pass the bill anyway, overriding the President's veto |
| Retrieval Q5 (Stretch — fixed term trade-off) | Parliamentary: advantage — a PM who loses support can be replaced without waiting years; disadvantage — less stability/predictability. Presidential: advantage — stable, predictable fixed term; disadvantage — a president who loses public support still serves the full term |

**High-risk vocabulary flag:** This section carries the heaviest vocabulary load in the unit (10 curriculum-mandated terms). Watch for students conflating "cabinet" (used in both systems, different meaning nuance) and "senate" (appointed in Canada vs. elected in the U.S. — this contrast is a common test-item target).

## Section 4 — Canada's Political System

| Blank / Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| Canada's three branches | Executive, legislative, judicial |
| Each riding elects one ___ | Member of Parliament (MP) |
| GG's role is mostly ___ | ceremonial |
| Retrieval Q1 (three branches + roles) | Executive (PM/Cabinet — carries out and enforces laws), legislative (Parliament — debates/passes laws), judicial (courts — interprets laws, settles disputes) |
| Retrieval Q2 (how someone becomes PM) | Wins leadership of a political party, then that party wins the most seats in a federal election — the PM is the leader of the winning party, not directly elected by the whole country |
| Retrieval Q3 (what is a riding) | An electoral district; each one elects one MP to represent it in the House of Commons |
| Retrieval Q4 ("voting for the PM" classmate) | Wrong: you cannot vote for the PM directly. Should say: "I'm voting for my local MP / for the party I want to form government." |
| Retrieval Q5 (Stretch — PM vs GG real power) | The PM holds real day-to-day power because they lead the party with the most elected support and make policy decisions with Cabinet. The GG's role is mostly ceremonial (Royal Assent, opening Parliament) and by convention acts on the PM/Cabinet's advice — the GG looks more official in ceremonies but does not hold independent political power |

**Misconception target labels:** [misconception target: PM directly elected like a U.S. president] and [misconception target: Governor General holds real power] — both appear as refutation-first callouts in this section.

**High-risk vocabulary flag:** "Riding" and "constituency" are used interchangeably — confirm students know these are the same thing, just different common names. "Senate" here is the APPOINTED Canadian Senate — do not let students merge this with the elected U.S. Senate from Section 3.

## Section 5 — Political Parties and Party Systems

| Blank / Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| Parties organize to turn ideas into ___ | law |
| Opposition's job is to ___ government decisions | question / scrutinize / challenge |
| Party systems table — One-party example | China |
| Party systems table — Two-party example | United States |
| Party systems table — Multiparty example | Canada, Alberta, many European countries |
| Retrieval Q1 (why parties form around ideology) | People with similar political beliefs organize together because it is more effective to pool support and resources to get elected than to run alone |
| Retrieval Q2 (government vs opposition) | Government = party with the most seats, forms the executive and sets policy. Opposition = next-largest party (or parties), whose job is to question and challenge government decisions — a required, legitimate role, not disloyalty |
| Retrieval Q3 (three party systems + example) | One-party (China), two-party (United States), multiparty (Canada / Alberta / Europe) |
| Retrieval Q4 (5 parties, no majority) | Multiparty system; challenge — no party has enough seats to govern alone, so parties may need to negotiate a coalition or minority government, which can slow decisions |
| Retrieval Q5 (Stretch — US vs Canada trade-off response) | A strong response acknowledges Canada's multiparty system offers more voter choice, but notes it can also produce minority governments and slower decision-making; the U.S. two-party system decides faster but offers fewer choices. Neither is simply "better" |

**Misconception target label:** [misconception target: more parties = healthier democracy] — appears as refutation-first callout in this section.

**High-risk vocabulary flag:** "Ideology" is introduced in the companion Foundations of Ideologies unit at deeper depth — here, keep it at "a shared set of political beliefs," not the left/right spectrum detail (that is out of scope for this unit; see scope boundary note in `_courses/grade-8-social/_context.md`).

## Section 6 — Canada's and Alberta's Parties

| Blank / Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|
| Retrieval Q1 (name the parties) | Federal: Liberal, Conservative, NDP, Green, Bloc Québécois. Alberta: United Conservative Party (UCP), New Democratic Party (NDP) |
| Retrieval Q2 (what is a platform, why it matters) | A document laying out what a party promises to do if elected; it matters because it lets voters compare parties' plans before deciding who to support |
| Retrieval Q3 (two things members vote on) | Any two of: local candidate selection, party priorities/policy direction, party leader |
| Retrieval Q4 (15-year-old getting involved) | Could join a party as a member (most parties don't require voting age to join), volunteer with a local riding association, or attend party events — membership and volunteering are open before voting age |
| Retrieval Q5 (Stretch — full path, 3+ terms) | Example: A citizen joins a party as a **member** → votes in their **riding association** to choose the local candidate → that candidate wins their **riding** in the general election → the party wins the most seats and forms **government** → the party's leader becomes **Prime Minister**. (Accept any answer using 3+ correct unit terms in a logical chain.) |

**Political neutrality check:** Confirm no editorializing crept into party descriptions during instruction — federal and Alberta parties are named factually only.

## Unit Synthesis — Sample Answers

| Cluster | Sample answer |
|---|---|
| Types of political systems | Political systems sit on a spectrum from total government control (dictatorship) to no central authority (anarchy), with oligarchy, monarchy, and democracy in between |
| Models of democracy | Democracy isn't one system — direct democracy has citizens vote on laws themselves; representative democracy has citizens elect people to vote for them, which is more realistic for large populations |
| Parliamentary vs. presidential | Canada's parliamentary system chooses its leader (PM) through party leadership and seat count; the U.S. presidential system elects its leader directly, for a fixed term, with executive and legislative branches kept separate |
| Canada's political system | Canada has three branches (executive, legislative, judicial); Parliament includes the GG (ceremonial), PM/Cabinet (executive), Senate (appointed), and elected MPs from each riding (House of Commons) |
| Political parties and party systems | Parties form around shared ideology to win seats and make law; government and opposition both play required roles; countries differ in one-party, two-party, or multiparty systems, each with trade-offs |
| Canada's/Alberta's parties and membership | Canada's federal parties include Liberal, Conservative, NDP, Green, and Bloc Québécois; Alberta's are UCP and NDP; citizens can join as members and influence candidates, priorities, and leadership through riding associations |
