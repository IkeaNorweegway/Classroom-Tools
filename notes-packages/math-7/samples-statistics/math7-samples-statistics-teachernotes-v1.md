## Overview
::: overview
**Big idea:** a statistical question has answers that vary, so we collect data. The population is everyone or everything the question is about (who, where, when). We ask the whole population (a census) when that is cheap and quick; otherwise we take a sample, and the sample is only worth summarising if it is representative, which a random method makes likely. From a sample we compute four statistics (mean, median, mode, range). A second sample from the same population gives slightly different statistics, so conclusions about the population are estimates ("about 3 to 4 hours"), never exact.
**Sequence:** statistical questions, naming the population, census vs. sample (too costly, too slow, too hard), discrete vs. continuous data (ST1) → representative vs. biased samples (the hockey team, friends, the mall at noon, one net by the dock), simple random sampling (names in a hat, a random number generator) and systematic random sampling (a random start, then every nth; jump = population ÷ sample size) (ST2) → the mean as levelling towers of cubes (sum ÷ count), the median after ordering (odd and even counts), the mode (none, one or two), the range as a spread, not an average (ST3) → two samples of the same population side by side on stacked dot plots and in a table; conclusions as estimates; combining samples (ST4).
**Materials:** linking cubes (about 20 per pair) for levelling, slips of paper and a hat or bag, a class list with numbers, a die (for a random start), mini whiteboards, calculators, sticky notes for a class dot plot. Optional: an online random number generator; a spreadsheet to pool samples.
**Depth ceiling:** population, census, sample, representative, biased, simple random and systematic random sampling. Mean, median, mode, range of small data sets (5–10 values). Discrete vs. continuous data. Dot plots and tables only: histograms, circle graphs and the shape of a distribution are Grade 8. Outliers are not an outcome; "one big value pulls the mean" appears only in a ★★ question. Stratified and cluster sampling, sampling error as a number, and standard deviation are out of scope.
**Reading level:** student pages are written at about Grade 3–4. The math words (statistical question, data, population, census, sample, discrete, continuous, representative, biased, random, simple random sampling, systematic random sampling, statistic, mean, median, mode, range, estimate, conclusion) are the learning; each has a picture or example beside it.
**Diagrams:** four new Grade 7 diagram types support this topic: `levelling` (towers of cubes evened out to the mean), `numstrip` (numbers in boxes, "as given" over "in order", with the middle ringed), `dotplot2` (two samples stacked on one scale) and `hat` (names drawn from a hat). The existing `people` type shows populations and samples (dark = picked; colours = groups).
:::

| Watch for | What to do |
|---|---|
| "A census is always better" | Ask: "How long to catch every fish in Lac La Biche? What happens to the fish?" A census is best only when the population is small and easy to reach. |
| Naming the sample as the population | Ask "Who is the question about?" before "Who did you ask?" The population is in the question, not in the method. |
| "Ask your friends — that's a fair sample" | Show the people picture: friends sit together. Ask "Did the student across the room have any chance?" A fair sample gives everyone the same chance. |
| Systematic sample with a start bigger than the jump | Start from 1 to the jump (1–5 for every 5th). Starting at 15 with a jump of 10 on a list of 120 gives only 11 names and leaves out 1–14. |
| "The median is the middle number in the list as given" | Write the list, then rewrite it in order underneath (the `numstrip` picture). Cross off one from each end until the middle is left. |
| Even count: taking one middle value | Ring both middle values; find their mean. (3 + 4) ÷ 2 = 3.5 is a fine median even if 3.5 goals can't happen. |
| "The mode is the biggest number" / "the range is the biggest number" | Mode = most often (count the dots in each column). Range = biggest − smallest: a gap, so it needs two numbers. |
| Range confused with the range of a function (topic 7) | Name the two meanings out loud: function range = all the outputs; data range = biggest − smallest. |
| Dividing by the wrong count (e.g. by the biggest value) | Count the values first and write "÷ 8" before adding. Levelling: the number of towers is the divisor. |
| "A sample tells us the exact answer for everyone" | Put two samples on stacked dot plots. Their means differ. Conclusions start "about…". |
| "Two samples disagree, so one is wrong" | Both are fair estimates; different people were picked. Pool them for a better estimate. |

## Lessons
### Lesson 1 — Populations, samples and census (60 min)
DO: Pre-knowledge check (5 min). Q1–Q2 preview the mean, Q3 ordering for the median, Q4 the halfway point (median of an even count), Q5 the range, Q6 reading a dot plot.
SHOW: Two questions on the board: "How tall is Sam?" and "How tall are Grade 7 students?" Ask which one needs data from many people.
SAY: "A statistical question has answers that vary. The population is everyone the question is about: who, where and when. A census asks all of them. A sample asks some of them."
DO: Sort cards: census or sample? (our class's favourite lunch, every fish in Lac La Biche, every car on the QE2, every Albertan, the 25 runners on the track team.) Students give a reason: too costly, too slow or too hard.
WATCH: "A census is always better." Misconception box (the walleye dot plot).
CUE: Worked Example 1 (Ridge School population), Worked Example 2 (census or sample, three cases), Worked Example 3 (discrete goals vs. continuous heights).
DO: Your turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 1.

### Lesson 2 — Fair samples and random sampling (60 min)
DO: Warm-up interleaved: name a population; census or sample for a lake; 25% of 80; discrete or continuous.
SHOW: The three people pictures: the population (10 of 40 play hockey), the hockey-team sample, a random sample. "Which sample looks like the school?"
SAY: "A representative sample has the same mix as the population. A biased sample leaves groups out. Random picking gives everyone the same chance."
DO: Names in a hat with the real class list: draw 6. Then a systematic sample: roll a die for the start, then every 5th on the numbered class list.
WATCH: "Ask your friends — that's a fair sample." Misconception box.
CUE: Worked Example 1 (Raj and the hockey team), Worked Example 2 (names in a hat), Worked Example 3 (every 5th from a list of 30, start 3), Worked Example 4 (one net by the dock).
DO: Your turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 2.

### Lesson 3 — Mean, median, mode and range (60 min)
DO: Warm-up interleaved: every 4th name from 2 on a list of 20; a biased sample to fix; (3.5 + 4.5) ÷ 2.
SHOW: Towers of linking cubes for 2, 5, 3 and 6 goals. Students move cubes until the towers are level: 4 each.
SAY: "The mean is the fair share: add them up, then share them out equally. Sum ÷ count."
CUE: Worked Example 1 (Edmonton highs: 60 ÷ 5 = 12 °C), Worked Example 2 (bus rides, median 15).
WATCH: "The median is the middle of the list as given." Misconception box: 12 vs. 15.
CUE: Worked Example 3 (Oilers goals, two middle values, median 3.5), Worked Example 4 (reading minutes: mean 25, median 25, mode 30, range 35).
WATCH: "The mode (or range) is the biggest number." Misconception box with the range arrow.
DO: Your turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 3. Quiz next class (ST1–ST3).

### Lesson 4 — Comparing samples and drawing conclusions (60 min)
DO: Mid-unit quiz (25 min) first. Then the lesson.
SHOW: Two stacked dot plots of screen time (Sample A, Sample B). "Same school. Why are they different?"
SAY: "A sample gives an estimate. A second sample gives a slightly different one. We say 'about', not 'exactly'. More data usually gives a better estimate."
CUE: Worked Example 1 (screen time: means 3 and 3.6, both ranges 4), then put the samples together: 66 ÷ 20 = 3.3.
WATCH: "A sample tells us the exact answer for everyone." Misconception box.
CUE: Worked Example 2 (walleye, Monday mean 44 cm and Tuesday mean 43 cm), Worked Example 3 (bus riders: which conclusions are fair?).
DO: Core worksheet; Challenge for early finishers. Exit ticket Section 4.

## Data and contexts used
- Lac La Biche is a large lake in northeast Alberta with a walleye fishery. The fish lengths (36–52 cm) are made up but realistic for adult walleye; the "one net by the dock catches small fish" story is a teaching simplification.
- Edmonton and Calgary daily highs (8–15 °C) are made-up spring or fall values. Oilers and Flames goals per game (1–6) are realistic but not from real games.
- The Banff hot springs and gift shop numbers, screen time, minutes of reading, bus ride times, pets and sleep data are all made up. Screen-time numbers are hours on a school night.
- Ridge School and Lakeview School are made-up schools. The Canadian census is run by Statistics Canada every 5 years; this is a good real-world census example if students ask.

## Connections
- **Fractions & Decimals (topic 4):** dividing for the mean often gives a decimal (14 ÷ 8 = 1.75 pets).
- **Ratios & Percents (topic 5):** a representative sample keeps the same ratio as the population (1 in 4 play hockey); a sample of 30 from 120 is 25%.
- **Functions (topic 7):** discrete vs. continuous returns (dots vs. a line); "range" has a different meaning there.
- **Probability (topic 12):** random sampling means each person has the same chance; experimental results vary from sample to sample, just as statistics do.
- **Grade 8:** histograms, circle graphs and the shape of a distribution.
