## Overview
::: overview
**Big idea:** multiplying by a fraction takes part of something, so a product can be smaller than the number you started with. Dividing by a fraction counts how many fraction-sized groups fit, so a quotient can be bigger. Decimals are fractions with denominators of 10 and 100, so the same two pictures work for them: the area model for × (0.3 × 0.2 is 3 tenths of 2 tenths, 6 of 100 squares) and "how many fit" for ÷ (6 ÷ 0.5 is twelve halves). The order of operations then pulls integers, squares and cubes, fractions and decimals into one expression.
**Sequence:** fraction × whole number and fraction × fraction on an area model, the rule a/b × c/d = ac/bd, simplest and equivalent forms, the product of two proper fractions is less than each factor, mixed numbers in recipes (FD1) → whole ÷ fraction and fraction ÷ fraction as "how many groups", fraction ÷ whole as sharing, common denominators (a/b ÷ c/b = a ÷ c), reciprocals and × the reciprocal (FD2) → decimal × whole, decimal × decimal on area models and by counting decimal places, decimal ÷ whole as sharing, whole ÷ decimal and decimal ÷ decimal by making an equivalent division (× 10 or × 100), calculator for long division with an estimate check (FD3) → order of operations with integers, squares and cubes, fractions and decimals (FD4).
**Materials:** fraction strips or fraction bars (halves to twelfths), square paper or transparent grid overlays for area models, two colours of pencil crayon, base-ten blocks or hundred grids, play coins (quarters, dimes, 50¢ is a nice prop even though it no longer circulates widely), a 6 m rope or skipping rope and a metre stick for the cutting demo, calculators for FD3.
**Depth ceiling:** positive fractions and decimals only (signed fractions and decimals are Grade 8). Mixed numbers appear in problems and are changed to improper fractions first. Multi-digit decimal division uses a calculator; by hand, keep divisors to one digit after the equivalent-division step. Order of operations uses brackets, squares and cubes, × ÷ and + −; no other exponents and no nested brackets beyond one level.
**Reading level:** student pages are written at about Grade 3–4. The math words (product, quotient, divisor, reciprocal, area model, proper fraction, simplest form, tenth, hundredth, decimal place, estimate, equivalent division, expression, evaluate, order of operations, brackets) are the learning; teach each with a picture on the board.
:::

| Watch for | What to do |
|---|---|
| "Multiplying always makes a number bigger" | Ask "half of 8?" before writing ½ × 8. Put 3/8, 1/2 and 3/4 on a number line after Worked Example 1. Contrast with 1½ × 2/3 = 1 (Worked Example 3), where the product does grow because 1½ > 1. |
| Common denominators before multiplying, then keeping the denominator (1/2 × 1/3 = 3/6 × 2/6 = 6/6) | Draw the area model: the overlap is one small piece, not the whole square. "Common denominators are for + and −." |
| "Dividing always makes a number smaller" | Hold up 3 strips and ask how many half-strips there are. Write 3 ÷ ½ = 6 beside it. Ask students to predict "more or less than 3?" before every fraction ÷. |
| Flipping the first fraction, or both | Say the steps in words: keep, change, flip *the divisor*. Check with a groups picture: 1/2 ÷ 1/4 must be 2, because two quarters fit in a half. |
| 0.3 × 0.2 = 0.6 | Shade the 10 × 10 area model: 3 columns, 2 rows, 6 small squares out of 100. Write 3/10 × 2/10 = 6/100. "Part of a part must be smaller than both." |
| 6 ÷ 0.5 = 3 (halving instead of dividing by a half) | Read it aloud as "how many halves in 6?" Count jumps of 0.5 on the number line. Then show 60 ÷ 5 or 600¢ ÷ 50¢. |
| Misplacing the decimal point | Estimate first, every time: 30 × $1.45 is about $45, so 4350 must be $43.50. |
| Strict left to right (2 + 3 × 4 = 20) | The Stampede receipt: the hot dogs are 3 × $4 before you add the drink. |
| × before ÷ always (12 ÷ 2 × 3 = 2), from reading BEDMAS as a strict letter order | Teach the four steps as levels: × and ÷ share a level and go left to right; + and − share a level. Avoid the acronym, or write it as B, E, DM, AS. |

## Lessons
### Lesson 1 — Multiplying fractions (60 min)
DO: Pre-knowledge check (5 min). Q2–3 (simplest form, mixed numbers) predict FD1 fluency. Q5 (decimals × whole numbers) and Q6 (6 + 4 × 2) preview FD3 and FD4.
SHOW: Fraction strips: three groups of 2/5. "How many fifths? How many wholes?" Then 8 pieces with half shaded: ½ of 8.
SAY: "Of means times. Half of 8 is ½ × 8. Two thirds of three quarters is 2/3 × 3/4."
DO: The area model for 1/3 × 1/2 on square paper. Columns in blue for the second factor, rows in yellow for the first; the overlap is the product. Count the parts: rows × columns.
CUE: "How many parts in the whole square? How many in the overlap?" The bottom is rows × columns; the top is shaded rows × shaded columns. The rule comes from the picture.
DO: Worked Example 1 (half of 3/4 cup of milk). Then the "multiplying makes bigger" misconception box with the number line.
WATCH: Common denominators. Use the second misconception box: 1/2 × 1/3 with the area model shows one sixth, not a whole.
DO: Worked Example 2 (simplify first or last, both give 1/3) and Worked Example 3 (1½ batches of pancakes, partial area model). Your turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 1.

### Lesson 2 — Dividing fractions (60 min)
DO: Warm-up interleaved: 2/3 × 3/4, ½ × 10, 5 × 3/10, (−4) × 3.
SHOW: Three paper strips. "How many quarter-strips can I cut?" Cut and count 12. Then a 6 m rope cut into ¾ m pieces (or a skipping rope marked in quarters).
SAY: "3 ÷ ¼ asks how many quarters fit in 3. Dividing by a small piece gives a big count."
WATCH: "Dividing makes smaller." Use the misconception box right after the opening picture; ask for a prediction (more or less than 3?) before each division.
DO: Worked Example 1 (rope, 8 pieces). Then sharing: ½ pan of bannock among 3 on an area model (½ ÷ 3 = ⅓ of ½), and Worked Example 2 (¾ pizza for 3 people).
DO: Same denominators: 3/4 ÷ 1/8 = 6/8 ÷ 1/8 = 6. Worked Example 3 (shelves from a 1½ m board: 12/8 ÷ 3/8 = 4).
SAY: "How many quarters make one whole? Four. ¼ × 4 = 1. Four is the reciprocal of a quarter." Fill the reciprocal table.
CUE: Keep, change, flip *the divisor*. Show it agrees with the groups picture: 3 ÷ ¼ = 3 × 4 = 12.
WATCH: Flipping the first fraction. Misconception box, then check with the groups picture.
DO: Worked Example 4 (2/3 ÷ 1/6 = 4, by reciprocal and by picture). Your turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 2. Quiz next class (FD1–FD2).

### Lesson 3 — Multiplying and dividing decimals (60 min)
DO: Mid-unit quiz (25 min) first. Then the lesson.
SHOW: The ribbon receipt (3 × $1.25). Then a 10 × 10 grid: shade 3 columns (0.3) and 2 rows (0.2). "How many small squares in the overlap? What is one small square worth?"
SAY: "0.3 is 3/10 and 0.2 is 2/10. 3/10 × 2/10 = 6/100 = 0.06. Count the decimal places: one plus one is two."
WATCH: 0.3 × 0.2 = 0.6. Use the misconception box with the hundred grid.
DO: Worked Example 1 (30 L of gas at $1.45: estimate first) and Worked Example 2 (2.4 m × 1.5 m rug, four-part area model, checked with 24 × 15).
SHOW: $4.50 shared by 3 (tape diagram). Then "how many halves in 6?" with jumps of 0.5 on a number line.
WATCH: 6 ÷ 0.5 = 3. Misconception box: count the 12 jumps.
SAY: "Multiply both numbers by 10 and the answer stays the same. $6 in 50-cent coins is 600 cents ÷ 50 cents."
DO: Worked Example 3 (4.8 m of ribbon, 0.6 m bows: 48 ÷ 6 = 8) and Worked Example 4 (quarters in $3: 300 ÷ 25 = 12, the same as 3 ÷ ¼ from Lesson 2).
CUE: Calculators are fine for long divisions like 52.20 ÷ 36, but an estimate must come first.
DO: Your turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 3.

### Lesson 4 — Order of operations (60 min)
DO: Warm-up interleaved: 0.4 × 0.2, 3 ÷ ½, 2/3 × 3/4, 5².
SHOW: The Stampede receipt. Ask half the class to work 2 + 3 × 4 left to right and half to multiply first. Compare 20 and 14; the receipt decides.
SAY: "Brackets first. Then squares and cubes. Then × and ÷ from left to right. Then + and − from left to right."
WATCH: Strict left to right, and "× before ÷". Both misconception boxes. Have students say "× and ÷ are a team."
DO: Worked Example 1 (integers: −3 + 4 × (−2) = −11) and Worked Example 2 ((5 − 2)² × 2 = 18 vs 5 − 2² × 2 = −3).
DO: Worked Example 3 (fractions: ½ + ½ × ¼ = 5/8) and Worked Example 4 (West Edmonton Mall change: 30 − 2 × 12.50 = 5).
CUE: Students rewrite the whole expression after each step (the flow-chart pictures model this). Skipping the rewrite is where sign and order errors creep in.
DO: Core worksheet; Challenge for early finishers. Exit ticket Section 4.

## Assessment
::: box Mid-unit quiz (24 marks)
FD1 (Q1–5), FD2 (Q6–10). Given at the start of Lesson 3, after multiplying and dividing fractions. Q10 is the explain-your-thinking item: full marks need the "how many groups fit" reasoning or a correct reciprocal step, not just "dividing makes it bigger." On Q4 accept any correct simplest-form route; a student who simplifies first and one who simplifies last should both get full marks.
:::
::: box Worksheets and exits
Core (★) covers FD1–FD4 with an area model, groups bar, hundred grid, receipt or flow chart on most questions. Challenge (★★) adds mixed-number recipes, multi-step sharing and cutting problems, money problems with estimates, equivalent division with × 100, and order-of-operations error analysis and bracket placement. Each misconception has at least one targeted question; the key names it.
:::

## Data and contexts used
- Bannock and pancake recipes: the amounts (¾ cup of milk, ⅔ cup of milk, ¾ cup of flour) are made up and kept simple. Recipes vary widely by family and community; if students bring their own family recipes, use those amounts instead.
- Gas at $1.45 per litre is a made-up but plausible Alberta pump price (prices in 2025–26 have mostly been in the $1.30–$1.60 range). Update it to today's local price if you like; 30 L and 40 L keep the arithmetic clean.
- Ribbon at $1.25 per metre, the 2.4 m by 1.5 m rug, the 6 m rope and the 1½ m shelf board are made up.
- Calgary Stampede food truck ($2 drink, $4 hot dogs) and West Edmonton Mall shirts ($12.50) are made-up prices in real places. Real Stampede food costs more.
- Quarters ($0.25) and the 50¢ coin are real Canadian coins; the 50¢ coin is rarely seen, so present it as "50 cents" if students don't know it.

## Connections
- **Integers (topic 2):** sign rules for × and ÷ return in FD4 (−3 + 4 × (−2)). Integer retrieval appears in each section.
- **Squares & Cubes (topic 3):** squares and cubes are step 2 of the order of operations; (5 − 2)² vs 5 − 2² shows why brackets matter.
- **Positive & Negative Numbers (topic 1):** fraction–decimal conversion (0.5 = ½, 0.25 = ¼) links 6 ÷ 0.5 to 6 ÷ ½ and 3 ÷ 0.25 to 3 ÷ ¼.
- **Ratios & Percents (next topic):** scaling a recipe is multiplying by a fraction; unit rates (price per litre) are decimal division.
- **Circles and Volume:** π ≈ 3.14 means decimal × decimal (A = 3.14 × r²), and V = Bh with decimal dimensions.
- **Grade 8:** operations with negative fractions and decimals (rational numbers).
