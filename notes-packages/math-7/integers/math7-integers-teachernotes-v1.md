## Overview
::: overview
**Big idea:** the four operations with integers all come from two models: chips (yellow +1, red −1, a zero pair is 0) and jumps on a number line. Adding puts chips together; subtracting takes chips away (add zero pairs when there is nothing to take), which is why a − b = a + (−b). Multiplying is groups of chips; a negative first factor means "take away groups." Dividing undoes multiplying. The sign rules come last, from the models and the pattern table, never as "because I said so."
**Sequence:** adding (IN1) → subtracting as adding the opposite (IN2) → multiplying as groups and the pattern (IN3) → dividing as the related multiplication, with estimation and two-step problems (IN4). Subtraction needs adding; × is repeated adding; ÷ undoes ×.
**Materials:** two-colour counters (yellow +, red −), at least 20 per pair; a floor number line from −15 to 15; desk number lines; a large vertical number line or thermometer poster; calculators for 3-digit ÷ 1-digit.
**Depth ceiling:** integers only. Products of three or more integers for the sign pattern. 2-digit ÷ 1-digit by hand; larger with a calculator. No order of operations here (that is FD4 in Fractions & Decimals) and no negative fractions or decimals in operations (Grade 8).
**Reading level:** student pages are written at about Grade 3–4. Read word problems aloud when needed. The math words (integer, zero pair, sum, difference, product, factor, quotient) are the learning; teach each with the picture beside it.
:::

| Watch for | What to do |
|---|---|
| −3 + (−4) = 7 ("two negatives make a positive") | Build it with chips: 3 red and 4 more red. "Did any chips turn yellow?" Keep −3 + (−4) and (−3)(−4) side by side all week. |
| "Adding always makes it bigger" | Walk 5 + (−3) on the floor line. Adding a negative is a step left. |
| "Subtracting always makes it smaller" | Chips for 2 − (−3): add 3 zero pairs, take 3 red. Five yellow are left. Ask: "Did the value go up or down?" |
| Saying "minus" for every − sign | Insist on "negative" for the sign of a number and "minus" for the operation. Read every expression aloud this week. |
| Sign of the "bigger" number used for × | "Count the negatives." Make students say it before they multiply. |
| Dropping the sign in ÷ (−12 ÷ 3 = 4) | Check with the related multiplication: 3 × 4 = 12, not −12. |
| A − b changed to a + b but b's sign not flipped (7 − (−4) → 7 + (−4)) | "Two changes: the operation AND the sign of the second number." Point to steps 2 and 3 of the flow chart. |

## Lessons
### Lesson 1 — Adding integers (60 min)
DO: Pre-knowledge check (5 min). Look for students who say 4 − 6 = 2 (Q6) or that −7 is warmer than −2 (Q1).
SHOW: A zero pair on the document camera. "One yellow, one red. What are they worth together?"
SAY: "Adding means put the chips together. Then take out the zero pairs. What is left is the answer."
DO: Worked Examples 1–2 with chips at desks: −3 + (−4) (cold night), then −5 + 3. Every student builds, then draws, then jumps on the number line.
WATCH: −3 + (−4) = 7. Use the misconception box before Worked Example 2.
DO: Worked Example 3 (Sam's −$20 bank balance, deposit $50) on the vertical number line. Then the rule box; students test the rule against their chips.
CUE: "Same signs: add and keep. Different signs: subtract the sizes, keep the sign of the one further from 0." The Calgary chinook line (−12 + 15) is the check.
DO: Exit ticket Section 1.

### Lesson 2 — Subtracting integers (60 min)
DO: Warm-up interleaved: −8 + 3, −6 + (−6), and "which is further from 0: −9 or 4?"
SHOW: The number line with two jobs for the − symbol. Students read the four expressions in the table aloud.
SAY: "Subtract means take away. If there is nothing to take, add zero pairs. They are worth 0, so the value doesn't change."
DO: Worked Examples 1–3 with chips: −6 − (−2), 2 − (−3), −2 − 3. Students record the before and after pictures.
SAY: "Look at 2 − (−3) and 2 + 3. Same answer. Subtracting is adding the opposite." Then the four-step flow chart.
WATCH: "Subtracting makes it smaller." Use the misconception box after Worked Example 3.
DO: Worked Example 4 (Calgary chinook, −15 °C to 9 °C). Stress "end − start" for a change. Then Your turn, including the elevator question.
DO: Exit ticket Section 2. Quiz next class (IN1–IN2).

### Lesson 3 — Multiplying integers (60 min)
DO: Mid-unit quiz (25 min) first. Then the lesson.
SHOW: The four ways to write ×. Point out that −5 = −1(5).
SAY: "3 × (−2) means 3 groups of −2." Build it with chips and jump it on the line.
DO: Worked Example 1 (Red Deer overnight drop). Then the pattern table: students predict each row before you fill it, and plot the dots on the grid.
SHOW: (−3) × (−2) with chips: start with 6 zero pairs, take away 3 groups of −2.
WATCH: The sign of the bigger number, and two-negatives-for-adding. Use both misconception boxes before practice.
DO: The three-or-more-factors table, Worked Example 2, then Worked Example 3 (many ways to make −12). Ask for one more way from each student.
CUE: "Count the negatives. Even means positive. Odd means negative."
DO: Exit ticket Section 3.

### Lesson 4 — Dividing integers and problems (60 min)
DO: Warm-up interleaved: −4 + 9, −4 − 9, (−4)(−9).
SHOW: Sharing a $12 pizza debt among 3 friends (chips in 3 groups). Then "how many jumps of −3 make −12?"
SAY: "Division undoes ×. Ask the related multiplication. The sign rules are the same as for ×."
WATCH: "Dividing makes it smaller." −12 ÷ (−3) = 4 is bigger than −12.
DO: The problem flow chart (draw, write, estimate, solve, check). Worked Example 3 (Rockies hike, −720 ÷ 3) and Worked Example 4 (6 °C to −14 °C in 5 hours: subtract, then divide).
CUE: Estimate before every ÷ with 2- or 3-digit numbers. The estimate catches a wrong sign and a wrong place value.
DO: Core worksheet; Challenge for early finishers. Exit ticket Section 4.

## Assessment
::: box Mid-unit quiz (23 marks)
IN1 (Q1–4), IN2 (Q5–9). Given at the start of Lesson 3, after adding and subtracting. Q9 is the explain-your-thinking item. When giving feedback, mark the sign and the size separately; a correct size with a wrong sign usually means a model problem, not a fact problem.
:::
::: box Worksheets and exits
Core (★) covers IN1–IN4, with a chip mat, number line or vertical line on most questions. Challenge (★★) adds missing numbers, sign reasoning with many factors, "always, sometimes, never" and two-step problems (chinook, golf, hockey plus/minus). Each misconception has at least one targeted question; the key names it.
:::

## Data and contexts used
- Chinook temperatures (Calgary −15 °C to 9 °C in one day, Lethbridge 18-degree rise) are realistic but made up. Real chinooks can raise the temperature 20–30 degrees in a few hours.
- The Rockies hike (2280 m down to 1560 m) is made up; it is in the range of real trailheads and passes.
- Golf scores are relative to par (−2 means 2 strokes under par). Hockey plus/minus: +1 when your team scores while you are on the ice at even strength, −1 when the other team does. Both are explained in a sentence on the page.
- Bank balances, pizza debts and elevator floors are made up.

## Connections
- **Positive & Negative Numbers (last topic):** opposites and absolute value. "Keep the sign of the number further from 0" uses absolute value.
- **Fractions & Decimals:** order of operations with integers (FD4).
- **Linear Equations:** solving −3x = 12 and x − 5 = −2 needs every operation in this topic.
- **Grade 8:** the same rules for negative fractions and decimals (rational numbers).
