# Rubric Design — Research Summary
*Research session: October 2026. Covers the rubric reviews and meta-analyses (Jonsson & Svingby 2007 → Panadero et al. 2023 and Jonsson et al. 2025), descriptor-writing guidance (Popham 1997, Tierney & Simon 2004, Brookhart 2018, Goldberg 2014), self-assessment and exemplar research, the main critiques (Kohn, Wilson, Sadler), and Alberta reporting language.*

---

## How to Read This File

This file supports the **Evaluate** phase in `_meta-context.md`: the extended-response rubrics on tests, the project rubrics (Math 9 inquiry projects, CEFL), and the Phys Ed skill rubrics not yet built. The ready-to-use wording lives in `templates/rubric-phrase-bank.md`; this file is the evidence behind it.

The key findings that matter most:
1. **Rubrics help, moderately, and only when students use them.** The 2023 meta-analysis found g = 0.45 on academic performance. The successful studies had teachers explain the rubric, then had students draft → self-assess or get feedback → revise. Handing out a rubric is not the intervention.
2. **The descriptors carry the learning.** Describe what the work looks like at each level ("explains using the particle model"). Don't judge it ("good explanation") or count it ("3 sources").
3. **Criteria should describe learning, not following directions.** "Uses relevant, credible sources" teaches; "has a title page" does not.
4. **Rubrics + exemplars + self-assessment work together.** Exemplars help students see what the words in a rubric actually mean.
5. **The critiques are real but mostly anecdotal.** Rubrics can narrow what students attempt and turn into a compliance checklist. Good design (few general criteria, several different exemplars, room for quality the rubric didn't foresee) is the response, not abandoning rubrics.

Related files: `hattie-visible-learning-research.md` (feedback levels, success criteria), `metacognition-lasting-knowledge-research.md` (Wiliam's five strategies, feedback that is acted on), `answer-key-design-research.md` (foresight bias in self-assessment), `project-based-learning-research.md` (K/U/S rubric mapping, critique cycles).

## Topic and Why It Matters

A rubric is a scoring guide with two parts: **criteria** (what counts) and **descriptions of performance at each level** (what the work looks like at each level of quality). Without the descriptions it is a rating scale or checklist, not a rubric (Brookhart 2018).

It matters for this workspace because rubrics are already required in several places, but each was written on its own terms:
- **Tests and quizzes:** every extended-response item needs a rubric (`tests-quizzes/_context.md`, rule 4). The test template uses a 6-level scale (Full / Substantial / Developing / Partial / Beginning / Not demonstrated).
- **Math 9 inquiry projects:** 4 criteria × 3 levels = 12 (`templates/inquiry-project-template.md`).
- **CEFL:** single-point rubric with K / U / S&P rows (`_courses/cefl/_context.md`).
- **Phys Ed 5–6 and 7–9:** skill rubrics listed, not yet built.
- **Report cards** use a 4-point scale: **Excellent / Proficient / Meets / Has yet to meet**. None of the current rubrics is built to it.

## Core Finding

Rubrics improve achievement when they are used **formatively**: students understand the criteria, see what quality looks like, judge their own draft against it, and revise. The effect comes from that cycle, not from the document itself. Of the design features, the strongest support is for **descriptive (not evaluative) level descriptions of criteria that name real learning**.

## Key Studies

### 1. Panadero, Jonsson, Pinedo & Fernández-Castilla (2023) — meta-analysis
*Educational Psychology Review, 35, 113.* The first meta-analysis of rubric effects.
- **Academic performance:** g = 0.45 (21 studies, 54 effect sizes), a moderate positive effect, still positive after correcting for publication bias.
- **Self-regulated learning:** g = 0.23 (5 studies); confidence interval crosses zero.
- **Self-efficacy:** g = 0.18 (3 studies); very wide interval, so the evidence is weak.
- Most moderators (type of rubric, level of schooling, etc.) were not significant.

**Takeaway:** a reliable, moderate effect on the quality of work. The claims that rubrics build self-regulation and confidence rest on too few studies to count on.

### 2. Jonsson, Panadero, Pinedo & Fernández-Castilla (2025) — what the successful rubric studies did
*Assessment in Education, 32(2), 192–211.* Took the 15 highest-quality rubric studies, ranked them by effect, and compared their procedures. The most successful interventions shared two features:
1. Teachers **explained both the content of the rubric and how to use it** for improving work.
2. A **draft → feedback or self-assessment → revision** sequence.

**Takeaway:** build the rubric into the task timeline. A rubric seen only at the end is a grading tool, not a learning tool.

### 3. Brookhart (2018) — "Appropriate Criteria: Key to Effective Rubrics"
*Frontiers in Education, 3, 22.* A review of rubric studies (higher education, 2005–2017) plus design guidance from Brookhart's wider work:
- Criteria must match the learning outcome and be substantive. Criteria about following directions "misdirect student efforts and mis-measure learning."
- Use **descriptive language, not evaluative language** ("excellent", "average"). Descriptions help students "envision where they are in their learning and where they should go next."
- Rubrics that **count** or use rating-scale wording (7 of 51 reviewed) are "more useful for grading than for learning."
- **Analytic** (one criterion at a time) is better for feedback; **holistic** is better for quick grading when students won't use the feedback.
- **General** rubrics (usable across many tasks) can be shared with students and used for learning. **Task-specific** rubrics give away the answer and suit grading only.
- Number of levels should match the decisions to be made. Three, four and five were the most common, and no single number is prescribed.
- From student-use studies: some students read only the top level, and some treat the rubric as "what the teacher wants."

### 4. Jonsson & Svingby (2007) — reliability, validity, consequences
*Educational Research Review, 2(2), 130–144.* A review of 75 studies. As summarized in later reviews (the original was not read directly), rubrics improve scoring consistency when they are **analytic, topic-specific, and supported by exemplars or marker calibration**. The benefit both teachers and students reported most was **clearer expectations**. Note the tension with Brookhart: task-specific rubrics score more consistently, but general rubrics teach better. Pick based on purpose.

### 5. Writing descriptors that work: Popham (1997), Tierney & Simon (2004), Goldberg (2014)
- **Popham** (*Educational Leadership*) named four flaws: task-specific criteria, criteria too general to guide anyone ("a superior response"), **"dysfunctional detail"** (rubrics too long to use), and confusing the test with the skill. His fixes: **3–5 criteria**, each **teachable** and each a key attribute of the skill; a rubric for class use should rarely exceed 1–2 pages. "In rubrics, less is more."
- **Tierney & Simon** (University of Ottawa; *PARE* 9(2)) found most design guides stress clarity but not **consistency**. Across levels, the same attributes should be described. Only the degree changes, and the wording should not switch from positive to negative partway down the scale.
- **Goldberg** (*PARE* 19(8)), revising an engineering design portfolio rubric, gives seven checks:
  1. **Parallelism:** same attributes, same order, same syntax at every level.
  2. **No "orphans"** (a key term at one level only) and **no "widows"** (a term missing from one level but present in all others).
  3. **No redundancy:** the same feature shouldn't appear at two levels unless the levels are explicitly cumulative.
  4. **Consistent qualifiers:** don't switch between frequency ("sometimes"), degree ("partly") and amount ("some") across levels.
  5. **Routes to partial credit:** the middle levels should describe the different ways work can be partly successful.
  6. **Even steps between levels,** like a staircase.
  7. **Consistency across a set of rubrics.**

### 6. Self-assessment, exemplars and co-construction
- **Andrade & Du (2005)** (*PARE* 10(3)): students said rubrics helped them plan, check their work and use feedback, and that they felt less anxious. But most students **did not read the whole rubric**, and some saw it as a teacher's personal demands, not the standards of the subject.
- **Andrade, Du & Wang (2008)** and **Andrade, Wang, Du & Akawi (2009)** (Grades 3–7 writing): having students **derive criteria from a model**, then **self-assess with the rubric**, produced better writing. Self-efficacy rose in all groups, and more for girls in the rubric group. The gender difference shows up across several of Andrade's studies.
- **Panadero, Jonsson & Botella (2017)** (*Educational Research Review* 22): four meta-analyses of self-assessment (19 studies, 2,305 students). Effects on self-regulated learning were 0.23–0.65, depending on the measure; on self-efficacy, 0.73.
- **To, Panadero & Carless (2022)** (*Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education* 47(8)): a review of 40 exemplar studies. The most effective practice **combined exemplars with rubrics and peer or self-assessment**, which helped students understand standards and judge their own work. This fits the CEFL rule of 2–3 deliberately different sample products and the design-fixation finding (Jansson & Smith) in `design-thinking-graphic-web-research.md`.
- **Fraile, Panadero & Pardo (2017)** (*Studies in Educational Evaluation* 53; N = 65): **co-creating** the rubric with students increased self-regulation (seen in think-aloud protocols, not in self-report). Performance improved on 1 of 3 tasks. Promising, but small.

### 7. Single-point rubrics
A single-point rubric lists only the **standard** (outcome met) description for each criterion, with open columns on either side for "not yet: what's missing" and "beyond: evidence of going further" (Fluckiger 2010, crediting Mary Dietz). It forces written feedback and keeps students from fixating on the top or bottom level. **The evidence is thin:** practitioner articles, one validity study showing single-point scores correlate with an established analytic rubric (Popken 2020), and small single-site studies. Use it because it fits the formative cycle above, not because it has been proven better than an analytic rubric.

### 8. The critiques
- **Kohn (2006)**, drawing on **Wilson (2006)** and Mabry (1999) (*English Journal*): rubrics standardize judgment, reward easy-to-agree features (spelling, organization) over substance, and push students toward "how am I doing?" over the content. Kohn would keep rubrics away from students entirely.
- **Sadler**: criteria written in advance can't capture all of quality, and some of it is only recognizable in actual work. He recommends learning standards from **examples of real work** (via Brookhart 2018).
- **Panadero & Jonsson (2020)** (*Educational Research Review* 30) reviewed 27 critical publications. With few exceptions, the critiques rest on anecdote, assume rubrics are only used for summative grading, or use a narrow definition of "rubric." They recommend testing the claimed harms rather than assuming them.

**Takeaway:** treat the critiques as design warnings, not reasons to drop rubrics. Keep criteria few and substantive. Use several different exemplars. Leave room for "beyond" work the rubric didn't foresee. Never let the rubric replace written feedback.

## What It Looks Like in Practice

### Choose the rubric type by purpose

| Purpose | Use | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Feedback during a project, students revise | **Single-point** or **analytic** | One criterion at a time; the space for comments forces specific feedback |
| Summative project or extended response, reported on the 4-point scale | **Analytic, general**, 4 levels | Consistent across tasks; levels match the report card |
| Quick end-of-unit grading with no revision | **Holistic** | Fast; students won't act on detailed feedback anyway |
| Complete / not complete steps (safety, materials, hand-in parts) | **Checklist** (not a rubric) | Compliance belongs on a checklist, never in rubric criteria |
| Marking consistency across a set of papers | **Task-specific** scoring notes, teacher-only, in the answer key | They give away the answer, so keep them away from students |

### Rules for writing criteria
- **3–5 criteria**, each tied to a learning outcome code (Popham; Hard Rule 1 in `CLAUDE.md`).
- Each criterion names **learning**, not task compliance (Brookhart). Neatness, length, format and "followed directions" go on the hand-in checklist.
- Map criteria to Alberta's **K / U / S&P** columns (see `project-based-learning-research.md`): check Knowledge at milestones, Understanding in the student's justification, S&P in the product.
- Use **general** criteria that work across tasks in a strand, so students meet the same language more than once.

### Rules for writing level descriptors
- **Describe the work, don't judge it.** Level labels (Excellent, Proficient…) can be evaluative; the description under each label cannot be.
- **Don't count.** "Uses 3 examples" → "supports the claim with relevant examples."
- **Same attributes, same order, same sentence frame** at every level; only the quality word changes (Tierney & Simon; Goldberg).
- **One type of qualifier per row.** Use degree (thorough / clear / partial / minimal) or frequency (consistently / usually / sometimes / rarely), not both.
- **Say what is there, not only what is missing.** Lower levels describe what the student did ("identifies one factor"), not just the absence ("does not explain"). This also keeps the wording from turning negative partway down the scale.
- **Even steps.** Read the four levels aloud. The gap from Has yet to meet to Meets should feel the same size as the gap from Proficient to Excellent.
- **Write the outcome level first.** On this school's scale that is **Meets**: write it straight from the outcome, then the levels around it. Excellent is depth or transfer, not "more of the same."
- **Keep it to one page,** readable at the students' grade level.

### Line up with the 4-point report card
- Use **4 levels** with the report-card labels: **Excellent / Proficient / Meets / Has yet to meet**.
- **The outcome line sits between levels 1 and 2.** Meets = outcome met at the basic standard; Proficient = met securely; Excellent = depth or transfer. Has yet to meet is the only "not met" level, so its descriptor must name the missing essential part. That is the feedback the student needs.
- This differs from AAC and LearnAlberta usage, where Proficient is the target and Adequate / Limited sit below it. Rubrics borrowed from those sources need their levels re-read before use: an AAC "Adequate" usually maps to Meets, and "Limited" to Has yet to meet.
- Keep **"Insufficient" (no assessable evidence) outside the scale.** It means the work is missing or can't be assessed, not a fifth level of quality. Alberta's PAT scoring and LearnAlberta rubrics handle it the same way.
- **Mismatch to resolve later (not changed here):** the Math 9 inquiry rubric uses 3 levels (12 marks) and the test template uses a 6-level scale. Options: (a) move both to 4 levels; (b) keep them and publish a conversion to the 4-point scale; (c) keep 3 levels for in-project feedback and convert only the final mark. Option (a) is cleanest for students, who would then see one scale everywhere.

### The cycle that produces the effect (Jonsson et al. 2025; To et al. 2022)
1. **Introduce the criteria** at launch with **2–3 deliberately different exemplars**. Students sort or rank them and say which descriptor fits and why. With the Andrade model, they help name the criteria from the exemplars.
2. **Draft.**
3. **Self- or peer-assess against the rubric.** Students highlight the descriptor that fits and point to the evidence in their work. Have them commit to a judgment before seeing any model answer (foresight bias, `answer-key-design-research.md`).
4. **Feedback** on 1–2 criteria at most, at the process or self-regulation level, written to the rubric language.
5. **Revise in class time.** Feedback that isn't acted on does nothing (Wiliam).
6. **Final scoring** on the same rubric the student has been using.

### Practical guards against the critiques
- Add a **"beyond" space** on every rubric (single-point column or Excellent level) worded as open evidence ("goes further by…"), so unexpected quality counts.
- Show **several** exemplars, never one, so students don't copy a single model.
- Ask students to **restate the Meets descriptor in their own words** at launch, and say what would make it Proficient. This catches the "only read the top row" problem.
- Pair every rubric score with at least **one written next step**.

## What It Doesn't Support

- **No evidence that any single rubric format is best.** Brookhart (2018) and the 2023 meta-analysis found no reliable difference by rubric type. Choose by purpose.
- **Self-regulation and confidence claims are under-tested.** The 2023 pooled effects cross zero. Don't sell rubrics to students or parents as building independence on their own.
- **Single-point rubrics have very little outcome evidence.** They are a reasonable formative format, not a proven one.
- **Much of the research is in higher education.** School-level effects tend to be smaller (Frontiers editorial on Brookhart 2018). The Andrade Grade 3–7 writing studies are the main K–12 evidence.
- **Most rubric studies are in writing.** Evidence for rubrics in math problem-solving, physical skills (Phys Ed) and open-ended design is thinner. Goldberg (2014) is a design-quality case study, not an outcome study.
- **Co-construction** helps self-regulation in one small study. It is worth trying, not required.
- **The rubric doesn't make the judgment valid.** Without exemplars or marker calibration, two teachers using the same rubric can still disagree.

## Alberta / Canada Angle

- **Alberta Assessment Consortium (AAC):** *Building Better Rubrics* (2009) includes the **Rubric Wordsmith**, a word bank organized under **Excellent / Proficient / Adequate / Limited**. It is widely used in Alberta divisions. Reuse is restricted to AAC member jurisdictions for professional development, so `templates/rubric-phrase-bank.md` uses original wording on the same four labels instead of copying it.
- **Provincial Achievement Tests (ELA 9 writing)** score with **Excellent / Proficient / Satisfactory / Limited / Poor**, with **Insufficient** as a separate code, and publish **anchor exemplars** for each level. This is the model of an exemplar-anchored scale with "Insufficient" kept outside the levels.
- **LearnAlberta** rubrics use Excellent / Proficient / Adequate / Limited / Insufficient, with a note that Limited or Insufficient work leads to a teacher intervention.
- **Division labels vary** (e.g. Golden Hills: Beginning / Satisfactory / Proficient / Excellent; Parkland: Excellent / Competent / Satisfactory / Insufficient). This school uses Excellent / Proficient / Meets / Has yet to meet; the descriptor rules don't change.
- **Tierney & Simon (2004)** is Canadian (University of Ottawa) research on descriptor consistency.
- **K / U / S&P outcome columns** in the new Alberta curriculum map directly onto rubric rows (see `project-based-learning-research.md`).
