# Teacher Notes Package Design
*Research summary — April 2026*

---

## Why this matters

A student notes package tells us what students should encode. A teacher notes package answers a different question: **what belongs on screen, what belongs in the teacher's mouth, and when do students get a cue to write?** These are not the same problem, and the evidence is clear that confusing them — putting on screen what should be spoken, or failing to signal when students should record — measurably degrades learning.

This file covers the teacher-facing side of direct instruction design: modality split, display density, guide structure, and cueing. It is the evidence base for building teacher notes packages in this workspace.

---

## Core finding

**The screen is for visuals. The voice is for explanation.** When a teacher shows a diagram and explains it in words spoken aloud, students learn significantly more than when the same diagram is accompanied by on-screen text — even when the text says exactly what the teacher would have said. This is the modality principle, and the effect size is large enough (d ≈ 1.02) that it should govern every display decision in a teacher notes package.

## Key studies and findings

### 1. The modality principle and redundancy effect (Mayer)

**What it is:** Mayer's cognitive theory of multimedia learning (2001) identifies separate cognitive channels — auditory and visual. Audio narration uses the auditory channel; visuals use the visual channel. When explanations are delivered as spoken words + visuals, both channels work in parallel. When explanations are delivered as on-screen text + visuals, both text and visuals compete for the same visual channel.

**Evidence:** Mayer & Moreno (2003) meta-analysis across 200+ experiments, including lightning, hydraulic brakes, electric motors, and biology lessons: median effect size **d = 1.02** for spoken words + graphics versus printed text + graphics. This is among the largest effects in instructional design research. Mayer (2001) identified this as one of nine robust multimedia principles.

**The redundancy effect:** When the teacher both displays on-screen text and reads that text aloud, learning is *worse* than either mode alone. Students must now process two streams of verbal information — the spoken words and the written words — which creates redundant load on the cognitive system. The instinct to "put the notes on the screen so students can follow along" is directly contradicted by this finding.

**Design implication for teacher notes packages:**
- What the teacher says aloud should NOT also appear as readable text on the display.
- The display shows the visual anchor (diagram, term, key phrase). The teacher provides the explanation verbally.
- Teacher notes should clearly separate: **[SHOW]** (what goes on screen) from **[SAY]** (what is delivered verbally).

**Sources:** Mayer, R.E. & Moreno, R. (2003). Nine ways to reduce cognitive load in multimedia learning. *Educational Psychologist, 38*(1), 43–52. Mayer, R.E. (2001). *Multimedia Learning.* Cambridge University Press.

### 2. Display text density during direct instruction

**What it is:** Cognitive load theory (Sweller, 1988) predicts that high-density text displays during live teacher talk will fragment attention: students must simultaneously decode on-screen text, listen to the teacher, and attempt to write in their notes — three competing demands on working memory.

**Evidence:** Project Follow Through meta-analysis (Adams & Engelmann, 1996) — the largest randomised K–12 educational intervention study ever conducted — found that lessons with "carefully organised content and removed distracting information" produced an average effect size of d = 0.59 across all measured outcomes. The only program in the study with consistently positive effects across both academic and affective domains. Direct instruction's display discipline (one thing at a time, no excess text) is part of this design.

**The cognitive arithmetic:** A teacher displaying a diagram, a multi-line text explanation, AND speaking while students are expected to write exceeds the capacity of working memory (4 ± 1 chunks; Cowan, 2001). Students presented with all three simultaneously tend to copy text verbatim — the lowest-effort response — rather than thinking and writing their own understanding.

**Design implication:** Each display state in a teacher notes package should show one thing: a diagram, a key term, a question, or a worked example in progress. Text on the display should be labels and key phrases, not sentences. If it takes more than a few seconds to read the on-screen text, there is too much text on screen.

**Sources:** Sweller, J. (1988). Cognitive load during problem solving. *Cognitive Science, 12*(2), 257–285. Adams, G. & Engelmann, S. (1996). *Research on Direct Instruction: 25 Years Beyond DISTAR.* Educational Achievement Systems. Cowan, N. (2001). The magical number 4 in short-term memory. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 24*(1), 87–114.

### 3. Teacher guide structure — reference, not script

**What it is:** Evidence-based teacher guides occupy a specific position between a lesson plan (what to do) and a script (what to say, verbatim). Research on teacher guide design suggests that word-for-word scripts reduce teacher responsiveness and student engagement, while completely unguided lesson plans produce high between-teacher variability and inconsistent coverage of key misconceptions.

**Evidence:** World Bank review of structured pedagogy programmes (Brombacher et al., 2021): guides yielding the largest student gains provided detailed content and pedagogical guidance without prescribing verbatim teacher speech. The structured content ensured coverage; the openness in delivery preserved teacher responsiveness. The review found that scripted lesson components performed best when they were: (a) examples and definitions, and (b) delivered interactively, not as monologue.

**Practical structure from high-performing guides:** Three types of annotation appear in consistently effective teacher guides:
1. **Content anchors** — what the teacher ensures students encode (key idea, diagram, definition). These are non-negotiable.
2. **Pedagogical prompts** — what the teacher does (pose this question, point here, pause and check). These are timed cues, not verbal scripts.
3. **Danger flags** — misconceptions to address before moving on, common errors at this point in the lesson. These are the moments where the guide earns its value: a teacher who hasn't taught this before will hit the same wall without this signal.

**Design implication for this workspace:** Teacher notes packages use three-column annotations:
- **SHOW** — exactly what is on the display (which diagram, which term, which example state)
- **SAY** — phrases and questions, not sentences to read; enough for fluent delivery without a script
- **CUE** — the exact words or gesture that signals students to write; consistent, brief, explicit

**Source:** Brombacher, A. et al. (2021). *Raising the foundation without lowering the ceiling: How to utilize teachers' guides to facilitate high quality instruction.* World Bank Education Blog.

### 4. Signaling and cueing during instruction

**What it is:** Mayer's signaling principle (2001) states that people learn more when cues highlight the structure and essential elements of the lesson. In live instruction, this means explicit verbal, visual, and gestural signals that direct student attention to what matters — and, critically, signals that tell students *when to encode* (when to write in their notes).

**Evidence:** Mayer (2001) Cambridge Handbook signaling chapter: students given cued instruction outperformed uncued students on both retention and transfer measures across multiple experiments. The mechanism: signaling offloads organisational processing from the student — they spend less working memory deciding "is this important?" and more working memory on the idea itself.

**The pause-signal-response sequence:** Research on direct instruction timing (Carnine, Silbert, & Kameenui, 2010) establishes that the most effective cueing sequence in classroom instruction is: teacher delivers new information → pauses (2–3 seconds) → signals for student response. The pause is not filler — it is the moment students hold the idea in working memory before producing a response. Without the pause, students respond before encoding.

**For note-taking specifically:** Students need a clear, consistent signal that tells them "this is the moment to write." Inconsistent or implicit cues ("this is important") are processed differently by different students. Explicit cues ("write this down: [exact phrase]") are equalizers — they ensure all students encode the same critical idea at the same moment.

**Design implication:** Teacher notes packages include standardised cue language. In this workspace:
- **Write cue:** "Write this in your notes: [exact phrase]" — for fill-in blanks
- **Draw cue:** "Draw/label this in your notes" — for diagram work
- **Think cue:** "Don't write yet — think about this first: [question]" — before a worked example
- **Check cue:** "Cover your notes. Without looking — [retrieval check question]" — for retrieval moments

**Sources:** Mayer, R.E. (2001). *Multimedia learning.* Cambridge University Press. Carnine, D., Silbert, J. & Kameenui, E. (2010). *Direct Instruction Reading.* Pearson/Merrill Prentice Hall.

## What this research does not support

- **Putting the student notes on the projector:** The redundancy effect says this degrades learning — students read the screen rather than listening. Show complementary visuals, not the notes text.
- **Reading your teacher notes aloud as a script:** A guide that teachers read from produces monotone delivery and removes responsiveness. Key phrases and prompts, not sentences.
- **Assuming students know when to write:** Without explicit cues, note-taking quality is highly uneven. Students who are uncertain what to write either copy everything (cognitive overload) or copy nothing (disengagement). The cue removes the uncertainty.
- **More visuals is always better:** The coherence principle (Mayer, 2001) applies to teacher displays too. A display with six diagram elements and two text boxes and an annotation competes with itself. One thing per display state.

## What it looks like in practice — the teacher notes format

| Annotation type | What it contains | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **SHOW** | What is on the display right now — diagram title, example state, key term. One thing. | `SHOW: Force diagram — 20N right, 8N left (proportional)` |
| **SAY** | Phrases and questions. Not full sentences. Enough for fluency. | `SAY: "Both arrows push on the box. The 20N arrow is longer — bigger magnitude, same direction question..."` |
| **CUE** | Exact signal language. Brief, consistent, explicit. | `CUE: "Write this in your notes: [force] or [push]"` |
| **FLAG** | Danger point — misconception, common confusion, pacing note. | `FLAG: Students often say "no forces" when the box isn't moving. Address directly before moving on.` |
| **PAUSE** | Timing cue. When to stop and let students think or write. | `PAUSE 5 sec before asking for response` |

## Design implications for teacher notes packages in this workspace

1. **Mirror the student notes section structure** — same sections, same order. Teacher notes travel alongside student notes.
2. **Minimal display — one visual per transition** — each section has a named display state; moving to the next display state is a structural marker in the teacher notes.
3. **Three-column layout** (SHOW / SAY / CUE) — not paragraph prose, not bullet lists.
4. **FLAG annotations for every major misconception** — these are the highest-value annotations. A teacher who hasn't seen the misconception before will walk past it without a flag.
5. **Explicit cue language, consistent across the package** — four cue types (Write / Draw / Think / Check) are enough. Consistency means students learn to respond automatically to the signal.
6. **Not a script** — SAY column contains phrases, questions, and key vocabulary, not sentences to read. Length limit: each SAY entry should be scannable in under 3 seconds.
