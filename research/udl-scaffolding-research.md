*Research session: April 2026. Covers UDL Guidelines through v3.0 (July 2024) and scaffolding literature through 2025.*

## Part 1 — UDL

### UDL Guidelines v3.0 (CAST, 2024)

CAST released v3.0 in July 2024, superseding v2.2 (2018). Terminology: "checkpoints" (v2.2) → "considerations" (v3.0). Three principles unchanged; six new considerations added; framing shifted toward learner agency, identity, and interdependence alongside access.

**Three principles:**

| Principle | "The ___ of learning" | K-6 materials priority |
|---|---|---|
| Multiple Means of Engagement | Why | Vary challenge; make goals visible; build self-regulation prompts |
| Multiple Means of Representation | What | Vocabulary in context; text + visual; advance organizers; activate prior knowledge |
| Multiple Means of Action and Expression | How | Vary response formats; executive function supports (checklists, planning prompts) |

For print materials, Representation and Action/Expression considerations have the best fit. Engagement considerations are primarily teacher-delivery decisions.

---

### Evidence Quality for UDL as a Framework

**Meta-analyses:**

- **Al-Azawei et al. (2017)** — *International Journal of Inclusive Education.* Generally positive outcomes across studies 2013–2016; significant heterogeneity and methodological limitations. Evidence quality: **Weak-to-Moderate.**

- **Kieran & Anderson (2019)** — *Journal of Special Education.* Meta-analysis of 20 studies (50 effect sizes), pre-K through adult. Mean effect g = 0.43. Small-group instruction showed higher effects (g = 0.86) than whole-class (g = 0.30). The whole-class figure is the most relevant benchmark for classroom materials. Evidence quality: **Moderate.**

- **Sprenger et al. (2023)** — *Cogent Education.* Systematic review 2015–2021. Positive combined effect; "considerable heterogeneity" — studies too varied in design and population to aggregate cleanly. Evidence quality: **Moderate.**

**Critical review:**

- **Boysen (2024)** — *Policy Futures in Education.* Critical analysis of studies cited on CAST's own UDL guidelines website. Key findings: (1) cited studies provided little direct evidence for UDL's specific claims; (2) most studies did not offer learner choice or measure learning outcomes; (3) the neuroscientific basis for the three-principle framework — the claim of three distinct brain networks — was not supported by the cited neuroscience literature; the cited studies showed distributed activation, which contradicts a clean three-network story. Evidence quality: **Moderate** (critical analysis methodology).

- **Edyburn (2024)** — responded to Boysen: policy often outpaces research; absence of evidence ≠ evidence of absence. Did not contest the methodological critique substantively.

**Bottom line:** UDL as a whole-framework intervention has moderate, methodologically uneven evidence (g ≈ 0.43). The neuroscience framing is not empirically supported. Individual UDL strategies (vocabulary instruction, graphic organizers, multiple representations, advance organizers) have stronger independent evidence bases. UDL is best understood as a design philosophy that collects proven strategies into a coherent organising framework.

---

### UDL vs. Differentiated Instruction

**Differentiated Instruction (DI)** — Tomlinson (1999 and ongoing): responsive practice. Teacher assesses students' readiness, interests, and learning profiles and adjusts content, process, or product. DI happens after you know who your students are. Often creates separate material versions.

**UDL** — proactive design philosophy. Barriers anticipated and removed from the material before any specific student is known. Targets predictable barriers arising from arbitrary design choices (presenting a concept in text only; using vocabulary without pre-teaching it) rather than individual variation.

**Design implication:** UDL is the correct philosophy for single-version classroom materials. DI is the correct approach for residual individual variation that a well-designed UDL material cannot address. They are complementary: UDL minimises the need for DI without eliminating it.

**The equity risk of separate materials (Matthew Effect; Stanovich, 1986):** Students consistently assigned to "easier" or "simplified" materials are exposed to less grade-level vocabulary, less complex syntax, and shallower content — compounding over years into knowledge deficit cascades. Building for the full range within a single material avoids this risk.

## Part 2 — Scaffolding

### The Original Definition — Why It Matters

Wood, Bruner & Ross (1976, 1978) coined "scaffolding" with contingency as a defining feature: effective tutoring adjusts support in real time to student performance — more help on failure, less help on success. Wood et al. (1978) experimentally compared contingent vs. non-contingent support; contingent condition significantly outperformed three non-contingent conditions on performance and independence.

Puntambekar & Hübscher (2005) — *Instructional Science* — documented how "scaffolding" has drifted to mean any support added to a task, losing its original defining features of diagnosis, contingency, and fading. This drift is the source of the crutch problem.

---

### Access vs. Cognitive Scaffolds — The Critical Distinction

Not in UDL literature explicitly; maps onto two distinct scaffolding functions documented separately in the literature.

**Access scaffolding:** Support that removes obstacles preventing engagement with the task's cognitive demand — not because the content is hard, but because the format, language, or presentation creates an irrelevant barrier. The cognitive demand of the task is unchanged; only the barrier is removed.
- Examples: word bank for a student blocked by vocabulary retrieval on a comprehension task; text-to-speech for a student with a decoding disability answering a content question; visual schedule for executive function support
- May be permanent — does not conflict with the fading principle because it targets barriers irrelevant to the learning target

**Cognitive scaffolding:** Support that helps a student handle cognitive demands that are within the ZPD but not yet manageable independently — thinking support, not barrier removal.
- Examples: worked examples, partial completion problems, hint sequences, sentence frames that scaffold reasoning, advance organizers
- Must be designed to fade — permanent cognitive scaffolds become crutches

**Evidence quality for this distinction:** **Moderate** — theoretically well-grounded (CLT, ZPD, UDL) but not directly tested as an experimental contrast. The distinction is practical and resolves the UDL/desirable difficulties tension.

---

### Scaffolding Types and Evidence

| Type | Key mechanism | Evidence quality | Key citations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Worked examples** | Offloads problem-solving demands; teacher thinks aloud through solution | Strong | Sweller (1988); Cooper & Sweller (1987) |
| **Partial completion (faded worked examples)** | Same structure, one or more steps removed; backward fading > forward fading | Strong | Renkl (2002, 2005); multiple replications |
| **Hint sequences** | Hierarchical prompts, least to most explicit; minimum help to proceed | Moderate-to-Strong | VanLehn (2011); Bloom (1984) "2 sigma" |
| **Graphic organizers** | Externalise structure; offload working memory; release resources for comprehension | Moderate-to-Strong | Hattie (2009) d = 0.57; Kim et al. (2004); Jiang & Grabe (2007) for ELL |
| **Advance organizers** | Pre-instruction overview; activates schema; comparative > expository | Moderate | Ausubel (1960); Stone (1983) meta-analysis d = 0.55 |
| **Sentence frames** | Scaffold academic language output; short-term production gains; dependence risk if permanent | Moderate (production); Moderate-concerning (dependence) | Gibbons (2002, 2014); Alvarez (2023) |
| **Checklists** | Step-by-step procedural support; complex multi-step tasks | Moderate | Rosenshine (2012) |

### Fading — The Most Underused Design Move

**The fading evidence:**
- Meta-analysis (2024) comparing programs with explicit fading protocols to programs without: d = 0.71 (with fading) vs. d = 0.32 (without). Fading roughly doubles the learning effect. This is the clearest direct evidence that fading is not optional — it is causally important to outcomes.
- Belland et al. (2017) — meta-analysis of 144 STEM scaffolding studies (computer-based). Overall effect g = 0.46. Only 16.5% of studies included explicit fading.
- Zheng et al. (2023) — GRR reading programmes produced comprehension and motivation gains across 1,688 learners; lower-attaining students gained most (p < .001).

**The GRR model (Pearson & Gallagher, 1983; extended by Fisher & Frey, 2006):**
I do → We do together → You do together → You do alone. The fading principle: scaffolding must be progressively withdrawn as competence increases. Support that does not fade is accommodation, not scaffolding.

**Default fading sequence for any skill:**
1. Full worked example (Lesson N)
2. Partial completion — same structure, one step removed (Lesson N+1 or N+2)
3. Prompt only ("What comes next?") — no structure provided
4. Fully independent
5. Novel transfer context (new scenario, different application)

This sequence should be planned across lessons, not assumed to happen naturally.

**Important moderator:** Miller-Cotto & Brock (2026) — *British Journal of Educational Psychology* — found working memory capacity moderates fading effectiveness: students with lower working memory benefit from slower fading. Uniform fading schedules may remove support too fast for some students.

---

### Hard vs. Contingent Scaffolds — What Print Can and Cannot Do

**Hard (static) scaffolds:** Planned in advance, embedded in materials, available to all students regardless of current state. Examples: printed hint boxes, partial completion problems, graphic organizers, vocabulary lists.

**Contingent (soft) scaffolds:** Spontaneous teacher responses, adjusted in real time to the individual student's current error, gap, or state. Examples: teacher circulating during practice, observing a specific misconception, giving the minimum targeted hint needed.

**Print materials can only deliver hard scaffolds.** The teacher guide is the vehicle for contingent scaffolding: specify what common errors look like during practice and what targeted hints to give in response to each specific error pattern. Hard and contingent scaffolds are complementary — the print material provides the baseline; the teacher's in-class response provides the diagnosis and adjustment.

Evidence for contingent superiority: Wood et al. (1978) experimental comparison. 73-study meta-analysis context also found contingent scaffolding significantly outperformed non-contingent.

### ELL-Specific Evidence

**Word banks:**
Evidence base is largely practitioner-documented. Key issue: word banks that allow letter-matching without semantic understanding produce correct responses without cognitive engagement. Better practice: include more words than blanks (requires semantic selection, not matching); include plausible distractors; student-generated word banks are more cognitively engaging than teacher-provided. Evidence quality: **Weak-to-Moderate.**

**Sentence frames:**
Gibbons (2002, 2014) — foundational text on scaffolding for ELL. Frames should scaffold toward a linguistic target (academic register, argument structure) not substitute for it. Fading sequence: frame → approximation → independent production.

Alvarez (2023) — *TESOL Journal* — studied emergent multilingual students in science: heavy reliance on pre-scripted sentence frames can reduce meaning-making opportunities; students focus on filling the frame correctly rather than engaging with scientific content. Recommendation: use frames for specific linguistic structures genuinely new to the student; fade as that structure is demonstrated.

**Graphic organizers:**
Kim et al. (2004): significant positive effects on reading comprehension for ELL students and students with reading disabilities. Jiang & Grabe (2007): positive effects for second-language reading comprehension. Mechanism: externalise text structure, reduce working memory load of tracking relationships, free resources for comprehension. Evidence quality: **Moderate-to-Strong** — strongest of the three ELL scaffold types.

### When Scaffolding Becomes a Crutch

**Five conditions under which scaffolding reduces learning:**
1. Not planned to fade (remains permanently regardless of competence)
2. Removes cognitive demand that is the learning target
3. Undifferentiated — applied to every student on every task regardless of need
4. Consistently substitutes below-grade demands for grade-level demands
5. Replaces instruction rather than supporting students through genuine challenge

**The long-term risk — knowledge deficit cascades:**
TNTP (2020) — *Disrupting Barriers to Strong Instructional Scaffolding* — reviewed 1,000+ classroom observations. Most common scaffolding error: not insufficient scaffolding, but scaffolding that permanently lowered cognitive demand, replacing grade-level work with easier work. Simplified materials reduce exposure to grade-level vocabulary, complex syntax, and ambitious content — compounding over years (Stanovich Matthew Effect, 1986).

**AI scaffolding parallel (recent):**
Bastani et al. (2024) — *NBER Working Paper* — students who used AI assistance on problem sets showed reduced performance on subsequent unassisted tests versus students who worked without AI, specifically because AI removed productive struggle. This is a direct experimental demonstration of the crutch effect in a contemporary context.

**Van de Pol et al. (2015)** — *Instructional Science* — low contingent support (provided uniformly at high intensity rather than calibrated to student need) was less effective than calibrated support for both achievement and task effort, particularly when independent working time was low.

### Metacognitive Scaffolding

Metacognitive scaffolding = prompts and structures that help students become aware of their own understanding, identify gaps, and regulate their strategy in response.

**Evidence:**
- **Guo et al. (2022)** — *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning* — meta-analysis of 49 experimental studies. Metacognitive prompts significantly improved SRL activities (g = 0.50, 95% CI [0.37, 0.63]) and learning outcomes (g = 0.40, 95% CI [0.31, 0.49]). Most effective when: specific (not generic), include a feedback response, adaptive to student state.
- **EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit (2021)** — metacognition and self-regulation: approximately +7 months of additional progress; rated very high impact, low cost. Evidence quality: **Strong** (multiple meta-analyses).
- **Self-explanation effect** — Fiorella & Mayer (2016); Dunlosky et al. (2013). g ≈ 0.55 across 69 effect sizes. Self-explanation is a form of metacognitive monitoring — prompting students to explain what they just did and why produces better transfer outcomes than equivalent study time without the prompt.
- **Hattie (2009)** — metacognitive strategies: d = 0.60.

**Important 2025 caveat:**
Large-scale RCT (*ACM Learning @ Scale*, 2025) found no significant benefit of metacognitive reflection prompts in a naturalistic computer-based platform context, suggesting controlled experiment effects may not transfer uniformly. Metacognitive prompts work most reliably when students have something genuine to monitor, receive feedback on their responses, and have enough prior knowledge to actually regulate.

**Types for print materials:**
| Position | Prompt type | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-task | Planning + prior knowledge activation | "What do you think the hardest part of this will be? What do you already know that will help?" |
| Mid-task | Comprehension monitoring | "Stop here. Does this make sense? If not, what specifically is unclear?" |
| Post-task | Process reflection | "Where did you get stuck? What did you do? Would you do it differently?" |
| Post-task | Attribution | "Did you get this right because you understood it, or because you got lucky?" |
| Error-specific | Diagnosis | "If you got this wrong, was it because you didn't understand or because of a careless mistake? What's the difference?" |

Generic "How are you doing?" prompts show weak effects. Process-specific prompts with a response mechanism show consistent effects.

### The UDL vs. Desirable Difficulties Tension

**Where they conflict:**
Desirable difficulties research (Bjork, 1994 and ongoing): conditions that feel hard produce more durable, transferable encoding than conditions that feel easy. UDL's impulse to remove barriers can, if applied without discrimination, remove difficulties that should remain.

**Key examples of the conflict:**
- Word bank on a retrieval task = removes the retrieval demand (the learning target)
- Sentence frame that supplies reasoning structure = removes the generation demand (the learning target)
- Grouping practice problems by concept type = removes the discrimination demand (the learning target in interleaved practice)

**Direct evidence:**
- Pyke, Lunau & Javadi (2025) — *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology* — directly compared CLT and desirable difficulties predictions; strategic cognitive challenges consistently supported long-term retention, supporting the view that reducing cognitive load is not always desirable.
- Kapur's productive failure research (2015, 2016) — students who attempt problems before instruction and fail perform better on subsequent learning than students who receive instruction first. Directly contradicts aggressive early scaffolding of representation.

**The resolution:**
The tension is real but resolvable. It dissolves when the access/cognitive distinction is applied consistently:
- Remove access barriers (irrelevant to learning target) freely
- Do not remove cognitive demands that are the learning target

UDL and desirable difficulties are not in conflict when UDL is applied precisely. They are in conflict when UDL is applied broadly to reduce all difficulty, including productive difficulty.
