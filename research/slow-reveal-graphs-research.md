# Slow Reveal Graphs — Research Summary
*Research session: October 2026. Light review: the routine's originator (Jenna Laib), practitioner accounts, and one practitioner-journal article. No controlled studies of the routine were found.*

---

## How to Read This File

This file supports data-literacy and graph-reading work in math (and science) materials. It positions slow reveal graphs the same way `three-act-math-research.md` positions Three Act Math: a well-designed routine whose format-specific evidence is thin, resting on mechanisms that are already documented in this framework.

The findings that matter most:
1. The routine is a staged information gap: a real graph is stripped to its bare shape and rebuilt one layer at a time
2. The order of the reveal is the main design decision. What you hide is what students reason about
3. Run as discussion only, most students can coast. A written commitment before each reveal is what makes it a thinking task for everyone
4. The evidence for the format itself is practitioner report, not experiment

---

## Topic and Why It Matters

A slow reveal graph is an instructional routine developed by Jenna Laib (math specialist, Massachusetts; curator of slowrevealgraphs.com). The teacher shows a real graph with its numbers, labels, and title removed, then adds the layers back one slide at a time. At each stage students notice, wonder, estimate, and revise.

It matters here because:
- Graphs are dense. Shown all at once, students rush to a conclusion or wait for the teacher's line of questioning
- The routine makes prediction-before-information a habit, which is the same mechanism as Three Act Math's Act 1
- It gives a genuine context for scatter plots, scale, rate, and interpolation/extrapolation: the context generates the question (passes the pseudo-context test)
- It exposes students to graph types and real-world data they will meet outside school

---

## Core Finding

**The learning event is the gap between a student's committed estimate and the next reveal.** Each new layer either confirms or overturns what the student just wrote. Without a committed estimate there is nothing to overturn, and the routine becomes a slideshow.

The format-specific evidence is weak. The underlying mechanisms (information gap, prediction, productive failure, metacognitive reflection) are strong and already in `_meta-context.md`.

## Key Sources

### 1. Laib — slowrevealgraphs.com (routine definition)

The originating description. A typical five-stage sequence:

| Stage | What appears | Prompt |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Bare graph: no numbers, labels, or title | What do you notice? What do you wonder? |
| 2 | Category labels (or one axis) | What new information did we learn? What might this graph be about now? |
| 3 | Title or units, plus one data value | If this one is X, what do you think that one is? |
| 4 | More values | How can we determine the missing value? |
| 5 | Everything | What does it mean now? |

The teacher annotates student observations on the displayed graph. Premade slide decks are organised by graph type and by context; each has a paired text (often a news article).

**Source:** https://slowrevealgraphs.com/introduction/

### 2. Berrigan (2022) — *The Math Practitioner*, 27(3)

Practitioner article (adult education). Three claimed benefits: slowing down, mistake-making as "productive struggle with a safety net", and critical reflection.

- **Reveal order can be engineered to overturn a reading.** In the worked example (US foreign aid), the y-axis first reads "$30", then "$30 billion". A student who read it as dollars was wrong because of what was withheld, not because of a personal failing. Berrigan argues this makes errors feel safe
- **The title goes last**, after students propose their own: "What would you name it? Why?"
- **Standard between-stage prompts:** "What new information did we learn? What changed? How were our estimations? What new estimations can we make?"
- **Reflection sentence starters after the full reveal:** "When I first looked at this graph, I thought ___." "The most important piece of information on this graph is ___." "Next time when working with a graph, I'll make sure to ___."

**Source:** Berrigan, T. (2022). Slow reveal graphs: A powerful instructional routine. *The Math Practitioner, 27*(3), 14–21.

### 3. Kane — classroom account (Grade 7)

Runs the routine daily for a few minutes through the last six weeks of the year (statistics and probability units). Sequence: first reveal and "what do you notice", second reveal and "what can you figure out" plus a prediction, full reveal, then **students write to summarise the graph in their own words**. Reports that quiet students participate more than usual and that conversations run long.

**Source:** Kane, D. *Slow Reveal Graphs Are Awesome* (Five Twelve Thirteen). https://fivetwelvethirteen.substack.com/p/slow-reveal-graphs-are-awesome

### 4. Education Week (2023) and Edutopia — descriptive reporting

Describe classroom use (bare bar chart, then axis numbers, then labels, then title; table talk before whole-group share). Education Week notes that studies find chart lessons can over-focus on the visual without engaging the underlying mathematical meaning, but names no specific study.

**Sources:** https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/graphs-are-hard-for-students-to-digest-can-a-slow-reveal-help/2023/07 · https://www.edutopia.org/article/using-graphs-promote-data-literacy-elementary-school/

### 5. Mechanisms already in this framework

- **Information gap (Loewenstein, 1994):** each stage shows enough to make the missing piece feel closable. See `three-act-math-research.md`
- **Productive failure (Kapur, 2015):** students estimate before they have the scale or units
- **Predictive instruction / predict–observe–explain:** already listed as a direct-instruction and practice variation in `_meta-context.md`
- **Metacognitive reflection (Zimmerman's Phase 3):** the end-of-routine reflection starters

## Effect Sizes or Outcome Data

None for the routine itself. No controlled comparison of slow reveal against showing the full graph was found in this review. Do not cite slow reveal graphs as "evidence-based"; cite the mechanisms.

## What It Looks Like in Practice

**Choosing and staging a graph**
- Pick a graph where one hidden element changes the reading: the scale, the units, a category, or a single surprising point
- Decide what the lesson's math target is, then hide that. Hide the scale for estimation and proportional reasoning; hold back data points for interpolation and extrapolation; hide categories for comparison
- Four to seven reveals. Title last
- Use real data with a named source

**Running it**
- Each stage: silent written response first, then pairs, then whole class (Structure B in `_meta-context.md`). The writing is the accountability
- Standard prompts: "What's new? What do you think now? Estimate ___."
- 5–10 minutes as a warm-up with a reusable recording sheet, or a full period when the graph anchors practice on a specific outcome

**The follow-along worksheet**
- Never print the finished graph. Print the bare version so students annotate it and add labels as they appear
- One written response per reveal, completed before discussion
- Estimate, then actual, then difference: the feedback is acted on immediately
- After the full reveal: outcome-linked math questions, one claim with evidence, one thing the graph cannot tell us
- Close with a reflection starter on how the student's thinking changed

**Display**
- A single HTML page with the graph as a layered SVG, advanced by click or arrow key, lets the reveal order be matched to the lesson target and the worksheet. Premade Google Slides decks from slowrevealgraphs.com need no preparation but have fixed order and prompts and mostly US data

---

## What It Doesn't Support

- **It is not instruction.** Students need the concept (correlation, scale, rate) taught before or after; the routine is engagement and practice, not the Explain phase
- **"Notice and wonder" alone does not guarantee thinking.** In whole-class discussion a few students do the reasoning. Willingham's test applies: can a student get through this without thinking? Without a written commitment, yes
- **No evidence it improves graph-reading on later assessments.** The claims are about engagement and participation
- **Dweck's "every mistake grows a synapse"**, quoted in Berrigan, is not a research finding and should not be repeated in materials here
- **Tangents are a real cost.** Rich contexts pull discussion away from the math. Keep the outcome-linked questions on paper so the math still happens

## Alberta/Canada Angle

Most premade decks use US data and contexts. For Alberta classrooms, build from Statistics Canada, Environment and Climate Change Canada, and Alberta open data so the context is recognisable and the source can be named. Math 9 fit: scatter plots, line of best fit by inspection, interpolation and extrapolation, slope as rate of change.
