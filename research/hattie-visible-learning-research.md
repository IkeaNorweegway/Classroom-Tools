# Hattie's Visible Learning — Research Summary
*Research session: June 2026. Covers Visible Learning (2009), meta-analyses through 2025, and methodological critiques.*

## Topic and Why It Matters

John Hattie's *Visible Learning* (2009) synthesized 800+ meta-analyses covering approximately 250 million students to rank the effect sizes of educational influences on achievement. It is the most widely cited single source in education policy globally, and its terminology — d values, the hinge point, Visible Learning — has entered provincial curriculum policy language, including Alberta's.

It matters for this framework because:
- It provides a ranked filter for strategy selection: strategies consistently below d = 0.40 are likely not worth the instructional cost relative to alternatives
- The Hattie & Timperley (2007) feedback model is the most differentiated, evidence-grounded framework for designing feedback that students act on
- Its critiques are as instructive as its findings — understanding what effect size comparisons can and cannot tell you prevents misapplication

---

## Core Finding

**No single instructional strategy dominates. What matters is whether the strategy exceeds the baseline effect of any schooling (d = 0.40), and whether it is visible — meaning the teacher can see learning from the student's perspective, and the student can see themselves as their own teacher.**

Most common teaching practices "work" in the sense of producing some learning. The useful question is not "does this work?" but "does this work better than the best alternative uses of the same time?" The hinge point is the filter for that question.

## Key Studies

### 1. Hattie (2009) — *Visible Learning*

Meta-meta-analysis. 800+ meta-analyses, ~250 million students, 138 influences on achievement ranked by effect size. The "hinge point" (d = 0.40) represents the average effect of any schooling — strategies below this threshold are producing less than baseline.

**Selected effect sizes relevant to math and to this framework:**

| Influence | d | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Formative evaluation / feedback loops | 0.90 | Assessment used to adjust teaching in real time |
| Teacher clarity | 0.75 | Explicit learning intentions + success criteria |
| Feedback (aggregate) | 0.73 | Highly variable — see Hattie & Timperley (2007) |
| Problem-solving teaching | 0.61 | Teaching students how to approach problems, not just solving them |
| Direct instruction | 0.60 | I do → We do; explicit instruction of new content |
| Metacognitive strategies | 0.60 | Consistent with EEF (+7 months) and Guo et al. (2022) |
| Spaced practice / distributed practice | 0.60 | Later Hattie updates; consistent with Cepeda et al. (2006) |
| Worked examples | 0.57 | Consistent with Sweller (1988) CLT research |
| Graphic organizers | 0.57 | Consistent with Kim et al. (2004) |
| Advance organizers | 0.37 | Below hinge; weak standalone effect — see caveat |
| Discovery-based learning | 0.21 | Well below hinge |
| Problem-based learning | 0.15 | Well below hinge; important caveat for Three Act Math |

**Important caveat on individual numbers:** These figures shift between Hattie's 2009, 2012, and 2015 updates as new meta-analyses are added. Treat them as order-of-magnitude signals, not precise measurements.

**Source:** Hattie, J. (2009). *Visible Learning: A synthesis of over 800 meta-analyses relating to achievement.* Routledge.

### 2. Hattie & Timperley (2007) — The Feedback Model

The most cited paper to emerge from the Visible Learning project. Identified that feedback has an average effect of d = 0.73, but with extreme variance — some feedback studies show d > 1.0, others show negative effects. The variance is explained by the *level* of feedback, not its presence or absence.

**Four levels of feedback:**

| Level | Question it answers | Effect | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Task level** | "Is the work correct?" | Variable | Most common in classrooms; effective for immediate correction but weak for transfer |
| **Process level** | "What strategy should I use?" | High | Most effective for learning — targets the process, not just the product |
| **Self-regulation level** | "How am I monitoring and evaluating my own understanding?" | High | Least commonly given; most powerful for independence |
| **Self level** | "How am I doing as a person?" | Negative to zero | Praise unrelated to task performance ("You're so smart") consistently shows weak or negative effects |

**Design implication:** Most classroom feedback operates at the task level ("this is wrong, here is the right answer"). Feedback at the process and self-regulation levels is what produces transferable learning. Exit ticket responses, returned assessments, and teacher comments should target process and self-regulation, not just task correction.

**Source:** Hattie, J., & Timperley, H. (2007). The power of feedback. *Review of Educational Research, 77*(1), 81–112.

### 3. Teacher Clarity — Hattie (2009, 2012)

One of Hattie's highest-yield, lowest-cost strategies (d = 0.75). Defined as making the learning intention (what students will learn) and success criteria (what it looks like when they've learned it) explicit at the start of each lesson.

**Why this is different from an objective written on the board:**
- A learning intention describes the *thinking* students will be doing, not just the activity ("Today you are going to learn why multiplying by a fraction makes a number smaller" — not "Today we will multiply fractions")
- Success criteria are student-facing benchmarks written at student level ("I can explain why... I can show... I can identify...")
- Together they give students a map of the lesson before it begins — which activates relevant prior knowledge and reduces extraneous cognitive load during instruction

**Source:** Hattie, J. (2012). *Visible Learning for Teachers.* Routledge.

### 4. Simpson (2017) — Methodological Critique

The most rigorous published critique of Hattie's statistical approach.

**Key findings:**
1. Hattie averages effect sizes from meta-analyses that used different outcome measures, different populations, different study designs, and different comparison conditions. These are mathematically incomparable.
2. A meta-analysis of studies comparing intervention to nothing produces a much larger average d than a meta-analysis comparing intervention to a strong alternative. Averaging them conflates "better than nothing" with "better than current practice."
3. Hattie's hinge point (d = 0.40) is calculated from this mixed pool — making it an artifact of the database composition, not a stable threshold.
4. The ranked list is unstable: the same influence changes substantially in rank across Hattie's own updates.

**The honest position:** The ranked list should be read as a rough priority signal, not a precise ordering. Strategies consistently at the top of Hattie's list across updates (feedback, formative evaluation, metacognition) are likely genuinely high-yield. Strategies near the boundary (d = 0.35–0.45) cannot be reliably distinguished from each other. Strategies well below the hinge (d < 0.20) across consistent updates are a reliable signal to deprioritize.

**Source:** Simpson, A. (2017). The misdirection of public policy: Comparing and combining standardised effect sizes. *Journal of Educational Policy, 32*(4), 450–466.

---

### 5. Cheung & Slavin (2016) — Publication Bias and Study Size

Systematic analysis showing that the same educational interventions produce smaller effect sizes in larger, more rigorously controlled studies. Hattie's database draws heavily on smaller studies with weaker controls — which systematically inflates effect sizes across the board.

**Implication:** The absolute values of Hattie's d estimates are likely too high. The *relative* ordering — strategies that rank consistently higher vs. lower — is more trustworthy than the specific numbers.

**Source:** Cheung, A.C.K., & Slavin, R.E. (2016). How methodological features affect effect sizes in education. *Educational Researcher, 45*(5), 283–292.

## What It Looks Like in Practice

**The hinge point as a strategy filter:**
When selecting between two instructional approaches, prefer the one with stronger independent evidence. Use d = 0.40 as a rough threshold: strategies without at least this level of evidence in independent meta-analyses should not displace strategies with strong evidence from the materials design.

**Teacher clarity in materials:**
- Every lesson opens with a learning intention written in student language
- Success criteria appear alongside the learning intention — what a complete, correct response looks like
- Exit tickets reference the success criteria directly: "Did you meet the success criteria for today? Where are you uncertain?"

**The Hattie & Timperley feedback model in materials design:**
The existing feedback architecture in `_meta-context.md` specifies what happens after exit tickets, quizzes, and returned assessments. Apply the four-level model to the *type* of feedback built into teacher guides and returned work:

- **Task level** — mark answers correct/incorrect (minimal learning value alone)
- **Process level** — "The error here is in the second step — what was the process you used? What would a different process give you?" (higher learning value)
- **Self-regulation level** — "Before you looked at the key, did you have any sense something was wrong? What would you check next time?" (highest learning value)
- Avoid **self level** feedback ("you're not a math person", "great job!") — positive or negative, it has weak to negative learning effects

**Formative evaluation as a design principle:**
The highest-yield influence in Hattie's synthesis is formative evaluation — assessment that changes what happens next in instruction. Every exit ticket, every quiz, and every retrieval warm-up should be designed with the teacher's *next move* in mind. Collecting data without adjusting instruction produces no benefit.

## What It Doesn't Support

- **Precise rankings.** The difference between d = 0.55 and d = 0.60 is not meaningful given the methodological issues Simpson identified. Don't make fine-grained distinctions on the basis of effect size alone.
- **Discovery learning.** Problem-based learning (d = 0.15) and pure discovery learning are consistently in the lower range of Hattie's synthesis. This is consistent with Sweller's cognitive load research and Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006). "Student-centred" is not a proxy for evidence-based.
- **Praise as feedback.** Self-level feedback — generic praise about the person rather than the work — consistently shows weak or negative effects. "Great effort!" without a process referent is not formative feedback.
- **The neuroscience framing.** Hattie's "three brain networks" framing (similar to UDL) is not supported by his own cited neuroscience. The classroom implications stand independently of the neuroscience claims.
- **Application without context.** Effect sizes are averages across populations. A strategy with d = 0.60 overall may not work for a specific population, grade level, or subject without adaptation.

## Alberta / Canada Angle

The Alberta Assessment Consortium (AAC) and many Alberta school divisions have adopted Visible Learning language directly — "learning intentions," "success criteria," and "assessment-capable learners" appear in Alberta Education's curriculum documentation. This means teachers in Alberta will encounter Visible Learning as institutional vocabulary regardless of their familiarity with the research behind it.

The honest position for Alberta educators: the concepts are sound (learning intentions and success criteria are evidence-supported), but the specific d rankings used to justify policy decisions deserve the Simpson critique applied before treating them as authoritative.

The Canadian Education Association (CEA) and the Alberta Teachers' Association (ATA) Research Department have both published materials drawing on Visible Learning. The ATA's *What Research Says About...* series references Hattie's synthesis with appropriate caveats.
