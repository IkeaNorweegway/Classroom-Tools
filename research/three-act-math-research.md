## Topic and Why It Matters

Three Act Math is a classroom instructional format developed by Dan Meyer (2010, 2011, blog and TED talk) and refined through his Stanford dissertation (2015). A teacher presents a perplexing real-world scenario — typically a short video or photograph — with no numbers or questions provided. Students generate the question themselves, identify what information they need, work through the math, and then see the resolution compared to their earlier estimates.

It matters for this framework because:
- It is the most structurally coherent Engage phase design for math currently in wide use
- It is grounded in curiosity research (Loewenstein, 1994) and productive failure (Kapur, 2015) — both of which have strong independent evidence bases
- Its critique of textbook "pseudo-context" has direct implications for how math problems should and should not frame real-world situations in any materials built here
- It models mathematical modeling as a process, not just a procedure — which aligns with Alberta Math curriculum competencies

---

## Core Finding

**The most important move in Three Act Math is Act 1: creating a genuine information gap before providing any numbers or questions.** This is what activates the curiosity mechanism (Loewenstein, 1994) and separates the approach from pseudo-context. Without a genuinely perplexing Act 1, the structure reduces to a decorated version of a standard word problem.

The format-specific evidence is weak. The underlying research on curiosity and productive failure is strong. Use Three Act Math for its mechanisms, not its brand.

## Key Studies and Frameworks

### 1. Loewenstein (1994) — Information Gap Theory of Curiosity

**The mechanism behind Act 1.**

Loewenstein proposed that curiosity is not a fixed trait — it is a situational response triggered when a person becomes aware of a gap between what they currently know and what they want to know. Critically: the gap must be perceived *before* it can motivate information-seeking. You cannot be curious about something you don't know you don't know.

**Three conditions for curiosity to be triggered:**
1. A person must have enough prior knowledge to recognize that something is missing
2. The missing information must feel significant or interesting, not trivial
3. The person must believe the gap is closable — infinite uncertainty does not produce curiosity, it produces anxiety

**Application to Three Act Math:** Act 1 is engineered to create this gap. The video or image gives enough context to trigger recognition ("I can see what's happening here") while withholding the number or outcome that resolves the scenario. Students must have enough schema to perceive what's missing — which is why Three Act Math works better after initial domain knowledge is established, not as a zero-knowledge entry point.

**Design implication:** An Act 1 scenario fails if students don't have enough prior context to feel the gap, or if the scenario is so alien that the missing information feels irrelevant. Effective Act 1 scenarios are perplexing, not mysterious.

**Source:** Loewenstein, G. (1994). The psychology of curiosity: A review and reinterpretation. *Psychological Bulletin, 116*(1), 75–100.

### 2. Berlyne (1960) — Epistemic Curiosity

**The theoretical precursor to Loewenstein.**

Berlyne distinguished two types of curiosity: perceptual curiosity (triggered by novel stimuli) and epistemic curiosity (triggered by gaps in knowledge). Epistemic curiosity is more durable and more closely tied to learning behavior. It is activated by conditions of moderate uncertainty — too little uncertainty produces boredom, too much produces anxiety.

**Relevance:** The Berlyne curve (inverted U between uncertainty and curiosity) maps directly onto difficulty calibration. Act 1 should sit at moderate uncertainty — students are sure something interesting is happening, but don't know the answer. This is the same zone as Vygotsky's ZPD, applied to motivation rather than capability.

**Source:** Berlyne, D.E. (1960). *Conflict, Arousal, and Curiosity.* McGraw-Hill.

### 3. Kapur (2015, 2016) — Productive Failure

**The mechanism behind Act 2.**

Already documented in `_meta-context.md`. Key application here: Act 2 of Three Act Math is a structured productive failure event. Students attempt to solve the problem before they have all the information or tools to solve it optimally. The initial struggle — including identifying what they're missing — primes the schema that subsequent instruction fills.

**Why this matters for Act 2 specifically:** Students who identify the missing information themselves (rather than being given it) generate a more active encoding of why that information matters. The information request in Act 2 is not an administrative step — it is a learning event. The teacher should not volunteer information; students should name what they need.

**Evidence:** Kapur (2016) showed that students who generated multiple solution approaches before instruction — including failed approaches — outperformed students who received worked examples first on transfer tasks (d ≈ 0.50). Effect was strongest for transfer, weakest for near-identical procedural recall.

**Source:** Kapur, M. (2016). Examining productive failure, productive success, unproductive failure, and unproductive success in learning. *Educational Psychologist, 51*(2), 289–299.

### 4. Meyer (2010–2015) — The Pseudo-Context Critique

**The most practically useful output of Meyer's work.**

Meyer's critique: most textbook math problems claim to be real-world but are pseudo-context. A pseudo-context problem wraps a math procedure in a scenario that students immediately discard — they identify the operation without engaging with the situation. The math and the context are separable: removing the context does not change the problem.

**Test for pseudo-context:** If you can strip the scenario down to the numbers and operation without losing any mathematical content, it's pseudo-context. A genuine context is one where the mathematical question is *generated by* the situation — removing the context makes the problem incoherent.

**Examples:**
- Pseudo-context: "Sarah has 3 bags of apples with 7 apples in each bag. How many apples does she have?" — strip it and you have 3 × 7.
- Genuine context: A video of water filling a tank — students wonder how long it will take to fill, must measure the rate, estimate the volume, model the situation. The scenario generates the question.

**Why this matters beyond Three Act Math:** Any math problem or task created in this workspace should pass the pseudo-context test. If the context is cosmetic, remove it and present the math directly — a naked math problem is more honest than a thin scenario that students ignore anyway.

**Source:** Meyer, D. (2013). *The Fake World and the Real World of Mathematics.* Blog and conference presentations; academic development in Meyer (2015) dissertation, Stanford University.

### 5. Mathematical Modeling — NCTM and CCSS/Alberta Curriculum

Three Act Math is one operationalization of the mathematical modeling process. The modeling cycle (from NCTM and embedded in Alberta Mathematics K–6 curriculum):

1. **Identify** a question worth solving in the real world
2. **Make assumptions** to simplify the situation
3. **Identify variables** and required information
4. **Build** a mathematical representation
5. **Solve** the representation
6. **Interpret** the solution in terms of the original situation
7. **Validate** — does the answer make sense? Does it match reality?
8. **Report** the result

Act 1 handles steps 1–2. Act 2 handles steps 3–4. Act 3 handles steps 6–7 (compare to the real answer). Most math instruction covers steps 4–5 only (build and solve) — Three Act Math is the rare structure that exercises the full cycle.

**Alberta Math curriculum connection:** Mathematical reasoning, communication, and problem-solving are explicit process competencies in Alberta Mathematics K–6 (2022). Three Act Math directly develops all three. The "wonder" phase (Act 1) develops mathematical reasoning and communication. The modeling phase (Act 2) develops problem-solving. The validation phase (Act 3) develops both.

## Evidence Quality

| Claim | Evidence quality | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Information gap triggers curiosity (Loewenstein) | Moderate-to-Strong | Laboratory and naturalistic studies across multiple domains |
| Epistemic curiosity supports information-seeking (Berlyne) | Moderate | Foundational; less experimental direct evidence |
| Productive failure produces transfer advantage (Kapur) | Moderate | Multiple replications; effect strongest for transfer, weaker for near-transfer |
| Three Act Math as a format increases engagement | Weak-to-Moderate | Meyer (2015) dissertation; practitioner studies; no large RCTs |
| Three Act Math improves standardized math outcomes | No evidence | Not studied at scale; wrong comparison to make for an Engage-phase tool |
| Pseudo-context reduces meaningful engagement | Moderate (indirect) | Supported by situational interest research; not directly tested as a Three Act comparison |

**The honest summary:** The theoretical mechanisms are well-supported. The format is not. Three Act Math should be evaluated as a delivery of those mechanisms, not as a program with its own evidence base. The format-specific question to ask is: *does this Act 1 create a genuine information gap?* If yes, the curiosity and productive failure mechanisms activate. If no, it's a decorated worksheet.

## What It Looks Like in Practice

### Full structure (50–80 minutes)

**Act 1 (~10–15 min)**
- Show the video or image. No numbers. No question from the teacher yet.
- "What do you notice?" — collect observations on the board
- "What do you wonder?" — collect questions on the board
- Identify the most interesting/common mathematical question together
- "What's your best guess?" — collect guesses publicly, including upper and lower bounds
- Record estimates where everyone can see them throughout Act 2 and 3

**Act 2 (~25–40 min)**
- "What information would you need to solve this?" — students name it; teacher provides it on request only
- Students work in pairs or small groups
- Teacher circulates; does not reveal solution strategy; asks Socratic questions ("Why did you decide to use that approach?")
- If students are stuck, use hint sequences (least to most explicit), not direct instruction

**Act 3 (~10–15 min)**
- Reveal the real answer (second video, photograph, measurement)
- Compare to estimates recorded in Act 1
- "Who was closest? What made the difference?" 
- Optional: "What would you do differently? What would change your answer?"
- Brief consolidation: teacher names the math explicitly — "What we just did is called..."

### Practical constraints

- **Time:** A full Three Act task requires a full class period. This is the biggest barrier to daily use. Use it as a unit entry point (Engage phase) or at the start of a new concept cluster — not as a daily warm-up.
- **Designing Act 1:** The hardest part. A weak Act 1 (not perplexing enough, or the question is obvious) collapses the whole structure. Vet before using. Existing databases: 101qs.com (Meyer's original repository), Desmos activity builder, Gfletchy.com (Graham Fletcher's 3 Act library, K–5 focused).
- **Grade appropriateness:** Three Act Math works across K–12. At K–3, the "wonder" vocabulary needs scaffolding. At Grade 5–6 (this workspace's context), students can run the Act 1 discussion with minimal teacher structuring after one or two practice runs.

### Fits in the existing framework as:

| Framework element | Three Act Math role |

| 5E Engage phase | Primary vehicle — the best single Engage move for math |

## What It Doesn't Support

- **Daily instruction.** Three Act Math is an Engage and Elaborate tool. It is not a vehicle for direct instruction of new procedures. Using it daily would crowd out retrieval warm-ups and direct instruction — both of which have stronger evidence for skill development.
- **Replacing worked examples for novices.** Students without sufficient schema cannot generate the right question in Act 1 or make useful estimates. The productive failure mechanism requires enough prior knowledge to recognize what's missing. For brand-new content, direct instruction comes first.
- **Standardized test preparation.** Three Act Math targets modeling, reasoning, and transfer. It is not designed to improve performance on procedural recall items. The outcomes it produces are real but measured poorly by most standardized assessments.
- **Low-perplexity scenarios.** If the Act 1 scenario doesn't trigger genuine curiosity, the entire structure collapses. A mediocre Three Act task is worse than a well-designed worked example followed by interleaved practice — it wastes time and misleads students about what math is.
- **Discovery learning by another name.** Three Act Math is teacher-facilitated. The teacher manages Act 1 convergence, curates Act 2 hint sequences, and names the math in Act 3. It is not unguided discovery — the teacher's role is high throughout.

## Alberta / Canada Angle

Alberta Mathematics K–6 (2022 curriculum) explicitly identifies mathematical reasoning, problem-solving, communication, and connections as process competencies. Three Act Math is one of the strongest single formats for developing all four simultaneously in a single class period.

The Alberta curriculum's emphasis on real-world contexts and Indigenous perspectives creates a natural extension: locally relevant Act 1 scenarios (land use, community data, natural phenomena) can make the approach culturally responsive in ways that generic textbook pseudo-context cannot. A genuinely perplexing local scenario activates the information gap mechanism while grounding the math in students' actual world.

Graham Fletcher's 3 Act Math library (gfletchy.com) has a significant K–5 collection with Canadian-accessible contexts. Many Meyer tasks assume US unit conventions (feet, miles) — verify units before using with Canadian students.
