# Math Notebook and Workbook Design — Research Summary
*Research session: June 2026. Covers multiple representations research, generation and self-explanation effects, low floor/high ceiling tasks, and writing-to-learn in mathematics.*

## How to Read This File

This file extends the existing `print-material-design-research.md` for math specifically. That file covers the general evidence for guided notes format, generative processing, dual coding, and answer space signaling — all of which apply to math. This file covers what's unique to math: the Rule of Four (multiple representations), the recording vs. processing distinction, writing-to-learn in math, and low floor/high ceiling task design as a math-specific resolution to the UDL/desirable difficulties tension.

The key findings that matter most for design decisions:
1. The Rule of Four — every major concept should appear in at least two representations; moving between them is itself the mathematical skill
2. Recording vs. Processing — the biggest design error in math notebooks is treating them as transcription tools
3. Low floor/high ceiling — the math-specific way to resolve the UDL/desirable difficulties tension in a single task
4. Writing-to-learn in math — reasoning quality over achievement on recall measures

## Topic and Why It Matters

Math materials have design requirements that generic print material research does not address. The central challenge is this: math is procedural and conceptual simultaneously. A student can execute a procedure perfectly without understanding the concept behind it, and can understand the concept without being able to execute the procedure fluently. Materials that only test one leave the other invisible.

The research in this file addresses the design question: *what does a math workbook or notebook need to do differently than a generic fill-in notes package to develop both?*

---

## Core Finding

**The biggest design error in math materials is building notebooks and workbooks as recording tools rather than processing tools.** A recording notebook is one where students copy procedures and examples. A processing notebook is one where students produce mathematical thinking — generate, explain, translate between representations, reflect. The generation effect (Slamecka & Graf, 1978) and self-explanation research (Fiorella & Mayer, 2016) both predict that the difference in learning outcomes between these two designs is large and durable.

The shift from recording to processing does not require more pages or more time — it requires that the blank on the page demand production rather than transcription.

## Key Studies

### 1. Lesh, Post & Behr (1987) — The Rule of Four (Multiple Representations)

**The foundational framework for math-specific UDL.**

Lesh, Post and Behr proposed that mathematical understanding requires fluency across multiple representation modes. The "Rule of Four" (formalized further by NCTM and extended by Leinhardt & Smith, 1985; Ainsworth, 2006) identifies four core modes:

| Representation | What it looks like | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **Algebraic / symbolic** | Equations, expressions, formulas, notation | Compact; generalizes; requires decoding |
| **Numerical / tabular** | Tables of values, patterns, specific cases | Concrete; reveals patterns; computationally accessible |
| **Graphical / visual** | Graphs, diagrams, number lines, geometry | Spatial reasoning; shows relationships and trends |
| **Verbal** | Words, stories, explanations in natural language | Connects to prior knowledge; reveals conceptual understanding |

**The core claim:** A student who can only work in one representation has learned a procedure, not a concept. Understanding is demonstrated by the ability to move *between* representations — to see that the equation, the table, the graph, and the description are all saying the same thing.

**Design implication:** Every major concept in a math notebook or workbook should appear in at least two representations. The translation tasks — "draw the graph that matches this table," "write the equation that describes this situation," "explain in words what this formula means" — are themselves mathematical learning events, not just comprehension checks.

**Evidence quality:** The Rule of Four is widely adopted in mathematics education (NCTM Principles to Actions, 2014; Alberta Mathematics K–6, 2022) and supported by multiple representation research (Ainsworth, 2006; Mayer's multimedia principle for math, 2009). Evidence quality: **Moderate-to-Strong** for the general principle; strongest for graphical + symbolic combinations.

**Source:** Lesh, R., Post, T., & Behr, M. (1987). Representations and translations among representations in mathematics learning and problem solving. In C. Janvier (Ed.), *Problems of Representation in the Teaching and Learning of Mathematics* (pp. 33–40). Lawrence Erlbaum.

### 2. Ainsworth (2006) — The DeFT Framework: Multiple Representations Are Not All Equal

**Important caveat on the Rule of Four.**

Ainsworth's DeFT (Design, Functions, Tasks) framework showed that not all combinations of multiple representations are equally effective. Adding representations can *increase* cognitive load if students don't understand how the representations relate to each other, or if they're presented simultaneously without support for translation.

**Three functions of multiple representations:**
1. **Complementary** — each representation conveys different aspects of the concept (e.g., graph shows trend; equation shows exact relationship)
2. **Constraining** — a familiar representation helps students interpret an unfamiliar one (e.g., a table scaffolds reading a graph)
3. **Constructing deeper understanding** — combining representations in a task requires students to build the connection actively (the highest-yield function)

**Design implication:** Don't present multiple representations simultaneously and hope students see the connection. Design tasks that require students to *make* the connection — translate from one to another, identify what's the same and what's different, explain why both show the same answer.

**Evidence quality:** Moderate — well-theorized framework with supporting experimental studies, but heterogeneous literature.

**Source:** Ainsworth, S. (2006). DeFT: A conceptual framework for considering learning with multiple representations. *Learning and Instruction, 16*(3), 183–198.

### 3. Slamecka & Graf (1978) — The Generation Effect

**The mechanism behind the recording vs. processing distinction.**

Slamecka and Graf demonstrated that information students generate themselves is retained significantly better than information they read or copy. Across 10 experiments, generated items were consistently recalled better than read items, even when the generated responses required only minor transformations (completing a word pair, generating the antonym, filling in a missing letter).

**Effect size:** d = 0.40–0.50 across meta-analyses of the generation effect in classroom contexts.

**Application to math notebooks:** Every blank in a math notebook is either a generation blank (student must produce the answer from reasoning) or a transcription blank (student copies what the teacher writes). Only generation blanks produce the generation effect. A math notebook filled with transcription blanks provides organizational structure without learning benefit.

**Common transcription blanks masquerading as generation:** Copying the learning objective; writing the title and date; copying a definition that was just read aloud; writing the steps of a procedure while watching the teacher do it.

**What genuine generation looks like in math:**
- Completing a partially-worked example (without looking at the full worked example)
- Writing the definition in your own words after studying it
- Generating a non-example of a concept
- Producing the graph from the equation (not labeling a pre-drawn graph)
- Writing the equation that matches a situation

**Source:** Slamecka, N.J., & Graf, P. (1978). The generation effect: Delineation of a phenomenon. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Learning and Memory, 4*(6), 592–604.

### 4. Fiorella & Mayer (2016) — Self-Explanation Effect in Mathematics

**The strongest individual learning move for math workbooks.**

Self-explanation — prompting students to explain what they just did and why, step by step, in their own words — produces significantly better transfer than equivalent study time without the prompt.

**Evidence:** g ≈ 0.55 across 69 effect sizes (Fiorella & Mayer, 2016 meta-analysis). Effect is strongest for transfer to novel problems — exactly what math assessment demands. Effect is weaker for near-identical procedural recall.

**Why it works:** Self-explanation forces students to identify gaps in their understanding. A student who can execute a procedure cannot always explain why each step was taken. The explanation attempt reveals the gap; the gap produces a sense of incompleteness; the incompleteness drives repair. This is the same mechanism as the information gap (Loewenstein) applied to self-monitoring.

**Application to math workbooks:**

Worked examples in math workbooks should be followed by a self-explanation prompt, not just a practice problem. The sequence:
1. Worked example (full or partial)
2. "Explain what happened at Step 2 and why that step was necessary"
3. Practice problem (same type, new numbers)

This is structurally different from the current standard (worked example → practice). The self-explanation step between them significantly improves transfer (Renkl, Atkinson & Grosse, 2004).

**Important moderator:** Self-explanation is most effective when students have enough prior knowledge to generate a meaningful explanation. For brand-new content, self-explanation prompts after the first example can produce confusion rather than monitoring. Use after the second or third exposure to new content, not the first.

**Source:** Fiorella, L., & Mayer, R.E. (2016). Eight ways to promote generative learning. *Educational Psychology Review, 28*(4), 717–741.

### 5. Pugalee (2004) — Writing-to-Learn in Mathematics

Writing while solving mathematics problems — not writing up a solution, but writing through the reasoning process — produces higher quality mathematical reasoning than silent solving.

**Key finding:** Students who were asked to write their thinking while solving nonroutine problems demonstrated significantly higher quality reasoning (more logical connections, more self-monitoring, more error-detection) than students who solved silently. Effect was strongest for nonroutine problems, not standard procedural tasks.

**Mechanism:** Writing slows the pace of thinking enough for monitoring to operate. Errors that are invisible in the internal monologue of rapid calculation become visible when written out. This is metacognitive monitoring made external.

**Application:** Math journals and reflection sections in workbooks are not decorative. "Show your thinking" is a metacognitive prompt, not just a marking instruction. The design question is whether the student is writing *while* they think (processing) or transcribing *after* they've already solved it (recording).

**What this does NOT support:** Writing as a replacement for mathematical fluency. Writing-to-learn effects are strongest for reasoning and transfer; they do not improve procedural speed or accuracy on standard computational tasks. Writing belongs in the process column, not in place of procedural practice.

**Evidence quality:** Moderate — largely Pugalee's own research program; consistent with broader writing-to-learn literature but limited large-scale replication in math specifically.

**Source:** Pugalee, D.K. (2004). A comparison of verbal and written descriptions of students' problem solving processes. *Educational Studies in Mathematics, 55*(1–3), 27–47.

### 6. Boaler (2016) — Low Floor, High Ceiling Tasks

**The math-specific resolution to the UDL/desirable difficulties tension.**

Jo Boaler's *Mathematical Mindsets* (2016) introduced the "low floor, high ceiling" task design principle: a single task that all students can enter (low floor = UDL access) while extending to genuine mathematical complexity (high ceiling = preserves productive difficulty). The task is not tiered into easy and hard versions — it is designed so that deeper engagement reveals more mathematics naturally.

**Why this matters for the UDL/desirable difficulties tension:** The existing framework resolves this tension as "remove access barriers freely; do not remove learning targets." Low floor/high ceiling operationalizes this for math: the floor removes the access barrier (all students can begin); the ceiling preserves the cognitive demand (no student hits an artificial stopping point). One task, one design, full range.

**Design principle:** Start with a specific case, then generalize. The specific case is the floor (accessible); the generalization is the ceiling (demanding).
- "What is 5 × 4? What is 10 × 4? What is 100 × 4? What pattern do you see? Will it always work? Prove it."
- "How many squares are in a 3 × 3 grid? A 4 × 4 grid? An n × n grid? Can you write a formula?"

**Evidence quality:** Moderate, with important caveats. Boaler draws on Dweck's growth mindset research (strong but contested in replication at scale), her own longitudinal studies (Phoenix Park study, Stanford studies), and PISA data. Her Phoenix Park study (Boaler, 1998) is frequently cited but has been criticized for methodological issues (Milgram & Bishop, 2011 — disputed; Boaler's formal response, 2012). The low floor/high ceiling principle is sound; the evidence base for Boaler's broader Mathematical Mindsets program is moderate.

**Distinction from "open" tasks:** Not all open tasks are low floor/high ceiling. Many "open" math tasks are low floor/low ceiling — they're accessible but don't extend. True low floor/high ceiling tasks require deliberate design: a specific-to-general structure, or a task that reveals new mathematical structure as students go deeper.

**Source:** Boaler, J. (2016). *Mathematical Mindsets: Unleashing Students' Potential Through Creative Math, Inspiring Messages, and Innovative Teaching.* Jossey-Bass.

## Recording vs. Processing — The Design Framework

The most important application of this research to materials design. Every element of a math notebook or workbook should be categorized and evaluated against this distinction:

| Recording (avoid or minimize) | Processing (build toward) |
|---|---|
| Copy the worked example from the board | Reproduce the worked example from a partial prompt |
| Write the definition as given | Write the definition in own words; generate a non-example |
| Record the formula | Derive the formula from a specific case; explain what each part means |
| Copy the steps | At Step 2, explain why this step is necessary before continuing |
| Label a pre-drawn graph | Produce the graph from the equation or table |
| Fill in a matching exercise | Write the matching connection and explain why it matches |
| Answer the practice problem | Identify what type of problem this is, then solve it |

**The notebook as a thinking record, not a storage device.** A student who uses a processing notebook can explain their reasoning. A student who uses a recording notebook can locate information but cannot reproduce it without the notebook. Long-term, the processing notebook is the notebook students no longer need — because the thinking has transferred to memory.

## Cornell Notes for Mathematics

The Cornell note format adapts well to math with specific modifications:

| Zone | Math adaptation | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Cue column** (left, ~1/3) | "What type of problem is this?" / "What is the key term or formula?" / "What step do I always forget?" | Forces categorization — the cue column is where students build the discriminative knowledge that interleaving practice requires |
| **Notes column** (right, ~2/3) | Worked example with student self-explanation at each step | Narrated worked example, not just steps |
| **Summary row** (bottom) | "Explain the main concept of this lesson in 2–3 sentences without looking" | The highest-yield zone — self-generated summary is both a retrieval event and an advance organizer for the next encounter |

**The summary row as a retrieval practice move:** The summary row, completed at the end of a lesson, requires students to retrieve and synthesize what they just learned — not transcribe it. This is a generation event (Slamecka & Graf) embedded in the note-taking structure. It also creates an accessible review prompt for subsequent retrieval warm-ups.

**What makes Cornell notes fail in math:** Students fill the summary row in immediately after notes, while the content is still in working memory, without closing their notes. This produces a transcription, not a retrieval. The summary row should be completed after notes are closed, ideally at the end of the lesson during the exit ticket phase.

## What It Looks Like in Practice

**Rule of Four in workbook design:**
- Every major concept unit contains questions in at least two representation modes
- At least one task per unit explicitly asks students to *translate* between representations: "Draw the graph that matches this equation," "Write the equation that describes this pattern," "Describe in words what the graph is showing"
- Translation tasks appear before procedural practice — not after, where they would be purely confirmatory

**Processing notebook page design (per lesson):**
1. Learning intention (student-facing language: "Today I'm learning to...")
2. Worked example — teacher models, student follows with narration in cue column ("This step is... because...")
3. Self-explanation prompt — "Explain what happened at [specific step] in your own words" — before the practice problem
4. 1–2 practice problems with "What type of problem is this?" cue before each
5. Summary row — completed with notes closed

**Low floor/high ceiling sequence in workbook:**
- Problem set opens with the specific case (floor — all students can begin)
- Mid-set questions move to generalization ("What pattern do you notice?")
- Extension question reveals the ceiling ("Will this always be true? How could you show that?")
- No student is told to stop; the task extends naturally

**Writing-to-learn prompts in reflection sections:**
- "Show your thinking" means: write the reasoning alongside the calculation, not just the answer
- Post-problem reflection: "Where did you get stuck? What did you do? Does your answer make sense?"
- These belong in the workbook alongside practice problems, not only at the end of a unit

## What It Doesn't Support

- **Multiple representations for every problem.** The Rule of Four applies at the concept level, not the individual problem level. Not every practice problem needs four representations. The evidence supports exposing the concept in multiple modes and requiring translation — not adding representations to routine procedural practice.
- **Writing as a replacement for procedural fluency.** Writing-to-learn improves reasoning quality; it does not build arithmetic fluency or procedural automaticity. Fluency requires repetition and retrieval, not reflection. Writing belongs in process tasks; fluency practice is a separate and necessary component.
- **Low floor as permission to avoid rigor.** A low entry point is only valuable if the ceiling is genuinely high. If the task doesn't extend beyond accessible content, "low floor" just means "easy." Low floor/high ceiling requires deliberate design of the ceiling, not just accessibility at the start.
- **Cornell notes without the summary row.** Cornell notes without the summary row are a cue column + notes column — an organizational system, not a learning system. The summary row is the highest-yield element.
- **Boaler's broader program claims.** The low floor/high ceiling principle and the mathematical mindsets framing are distinct. The principle is well-grounded. The broader mindset intervention claims (that changing students' beliefs about fixed vs. growth intelligence substantially improves math outcomes at scale) have had mixed replication results. Design materials around the principle; do not rely on the broader program evidence.

## Alberta / Canada Angle

Alberta Mathematics K–6 (2022 curriculum) explicitly identifies multiple representations as a mathematical process competency and emphasizes communication of mathematical thinking as a required demonstration of understanding. This curriculum alignment means that materials built with the Rule of Four and writing-to-learn approaches are not only evidence-grounded but directly assessed — students are expected to explain and represent, not just compute.

The curriculum's emphasis on mathematical reasoning and connections (connecting concepts within math and to real life) maps directly onto the translation tasks in the Rule of Four and the low floor/high ceiling design principle.

WNCP (Western and Northern Canadian Protocol) resources, including many provincially-adopted math materials, use multiple representation approaches consistently. This framework's design principles are consistent with those materials and extend them with the explicit evidence base that curriculum documents reference but rarely unpack.
