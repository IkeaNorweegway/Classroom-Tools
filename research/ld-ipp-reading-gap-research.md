# Teaching Grade 7–9 Students with LD/IPP and Grade 2 Reading Levels — Research Summary
*Research session: September 2026.*

---

## How to Read This File

This file supports design decisions for materials used with junior-high students who have an Individualized Program Plan (IPP) for a learning disability (LD) and a reading level several grades below their chronological grade — the specific case of a Grade 7–9 student reading at roughly a Grade 2 level. It covers two things together, because they answer different questions: (1) what the reading-intervention research says about closing the gap itself, and (2) what the content-access research says about teaching grade-level science/PE/etc. *while* the gap is being closed. Read this before building any worksheet, notes package, or assessment for a student profile like this.

---

## 1. Topic and Why It Matters

A student in Grade 7–9 reading at a Grade 2 level is not simply "behind" — the gap (roughly 5 grade-equivalent levels) is large enough that grade-level print text is not a viable access route for content instruction, and generic differentiation ("give simpler words") is not the same thing as evidence-based intervention or evidence-based access support. Two separate problems have to be solved:

- **The literacy gap itself** — does the student get structured, explicit reading intervention, and how much can realistically be closed at this age.
- **Content access in the meantime** — how the student learns Grade 7–9 science/PE/humanities content without being gated by decoding they don't yet have.

Materials built for this workspace need to serve both: they should not silently assume grade-level reading, and they should not become "the reading intervention" by accident — that's a different, specialist-delivered track.

---

## 2. Core Finding

The evidence converges on a clear division of labour:

1. **Reading intervention** for older struggling readers works, but effects are modest (mean ES ≈ 0.10–0.40 depending on outcome and study quality) and get smaller the older the student is and the more severe the gap. Intervention must be explicit, systematic, and intensive (small group or 1:1) — general classroom differentiation does not substitute for it.
2. **Content access** for grade-level curriculum is a *separate, higher-leverage* problem with stronger, more classroom-actionable evidence: text-to-speech/read-aloud accommodations (ES ≈ 0.35–0.56), graphic organizers (ES ≈ 0.59–0.63 for learner-generated use), and explicit vocabulary/text-structure instruction reliably unlock comprehension of content a student cannot yet decode independently.

**Implication for this workspace:** don't try to write the reading intervention into a science workbook. Write the science workbook so it doesn't require Grade 7–9 decoding to access Grade 7–9 thinking — via read-aloud/audio support, graphic organizers, controlled vocabulary load, and short chunked text — while the student's IPP team runs structured literacy intervention separately.

## 3. Key Studies

- **Wanzek, Vaughn, Scammacca, Metz, Murray, Roberts, & Danielson (2013)**, *Review of Educational Research* — "Extensive Reading Interventions for Students With Reading Difficulties After Grade 3." For Grades 4–12, mean effect sizes for reading interventions ranged **0.10–0.16** across comprehension, word reading, fluency, and spelling. This is the most direct evidence for the "how much can intervention alone close the gap by junior high" question — effects are real but small, meaning intervention needs to be sustained over years, not weeks, and content access can't wait for it.

- **Gersten, Fuchs, Williams, & Baker (2001)**, *Review of Educational Research* — synthesis of reading comprehension strategy instruction for students with LD. Establishes that explicit, purposeful strategy instruction (not incidental exposure) is what moves comprehension — the rationale for structured literacy over exposure-based approaches.

- **Dexter & Hughes (2011)**, *Learning Disability Quarterly* — meta-analysis of graphic organizers for students with LD, Grades 4–12, 16 studies/808 participants. Learner-generated graphic organizers: **g = 0.59** (memory), **g = 0.63** (comprehension). Directly actionable for content-area worksheets — organizer use should be learner-generated (student builds it), not just teacher-provided-and-filled.

- **Dexter, Park, & Hughes (2011)**, *Learning Disabilities Research & Practice* — graphic organizers specifically in science instruction for adolescents with LD. Confirms the graphic-organizer effect transfers to science content specifically, which is directly relevant to this workspace's Grade 6 science line and any junior-high science extension.

- **Wood, Moxley, Tighe, & Wagner (2018)**, *Journal of Learning Disabilities* — meta-analysis of text-to-speech/read-aloud tools for reading comprehension, 22 studies/2,942 participants, Grade 3 through postsecondary. Weighted **ES = 0.35** (95% CI 0.14–0.56). A separate meta-analysis of the read-aloud *test accommodation* specifically found scores rose **0.56 SD**. This is the single strongest, most directly applicable finding for a Grade 2-reading-level student in Grade 7–9 content: audio/read-aloud access to grade-level text materially improves comprehension independent of decoding skill.

## 4. Effect Sizes / Outcome Data (Summary Table)

| Strategy | Effect size | Source |
|---|---|---|
| General reading intervention, Grades 4–12 | ES = 0.10–0.16 | Wanzek et al. 2013 |
| Reading intervention, broader/mixed-age meta-analyses | ES = 0.23–0.39 | Neitzel et al. 2022; Gersten et al. 2020; Wanzek et al. 2018 |
| Text-to-speech / read-aloud tools, comprehension | ES = 0.35 | Wood, Moxley, Tighe & Wagner 2018 |
| Read-aloud test accommodation | ES = 0.56 SD | ASHA-indexed meta-analysis |
| Graphic organizers, learner-generated (memory) | g = 0.59 | Dexter & Hughes 2011 |
| Graphic organizers, learner-generated (comprehension) | g = 0.63 | Dexter & Hughes 2011 |

Reference frame (What Works Clearinghouse): small effect ≈ 0.2–0.4; medium ≈ 0.5–0.8. Read this table as: **closing the literacy gap itself is a small-effect, slow-moving process; giving content access around the gap is a medium-effect, immediately actionable one.** Prioritize materials design effort accordingly.

## 5. What It Looks Like in Practice

**For content-area materials (worksheets, notes, quizzes) built in this workspace:**
- Never gate a content concept behind independent silent reading of grade-level text. Pair any dense text block with an audio option (read-aloud, TTS-compatible digital format) or a teacher/EA read-aloud note.
- Build graphic organizers as something the student constructs (fills in from a word bank, sorts, sequences) rather than a pre-filled reference — the effect is strongest for learner-generated use.
- Control vocabulary load deliberately: pre-teach or glossary any Tier 2/3 word before it appears in a task, don't assume in-context inference.
- Chunk text into short blocks with a visual or concrete anchor between them — long unbroken paragraphs are the single biggest access barrier at this reading level, independent of content difficulty.
- Keep response format flexible (oral, circle/sort, short structured frames) so the assessment measures science/PE/content understanding, not reading/writing stamina — this matters directly for the "every quiz item maps to an LO" rule already in this workspace's Build Protocol: if the LO is about content knowledge, the item shouldn't silently also be testing Grade 7 reading.

**For the IPP itself (separate from materials, but shapes what accommodations to assume are in place):**
- IPP goals for a student this far behind should be SMART, and for literacy specifically should target the actual skill deficit (decoding vs. fluency vs. comprehension vs. vocabulary) rather than a generic "will improve reading level" goal — Alberta Education's *Individualized Program Planning* guide (2006) frames this as one of its core chapters ("establishing meaningful goals").
- Structured literacy intervention (explicit, systematic, sequential — e.g., Orton-Gillingham-aligned approaches) should be happening in a separate, intensive small-group/1:1 block, not folded into content-area class time.
- Assistive technology (text-to-speech, speech-to-text) is named explicitly in Alberta's IPP guidance as a distinct chapter — it is a legitimate, evidence-supported accommodation, not a workaround.

## 6. What It Doesn't Support

- **It doesn't support the idea that a large gap (5 grade levels) closes quickly at this age.** Effect sizes for reading intervention in Grades 4–12 are small (0.10–0.39). Materials and teacher expectations should plan for gradual, multi-year gains, not a same-year catch-up.
- **The dyslexia/structured-literacy evidence base is thin specifically for Grades 7–9.** Most of the strongest structured-literacy RCT evidence is from elementary grades; the extrapolation to junior high is reasonable (mechanisms don't change) but not as directly evidenced.
- **It doesn't support assuming one universal profile.** "LD and grade 2 reading level" could mean a decoding-specific disability (dyslexia-type), a broader language/comprehension disability, or an unaddressed compounding factor (vision, attendance, prior instruction gaps). The specific skill deficit named in the IPP should drive which strategy is prioritized — the research above supports the general toolkit, not a single fixed recipe.
- **Accommodations research (TTS, read-aloud) measures comprehension of accessed content, not literacy growth.** These are the right lever for content access; they are not a substitute for intervention and shouldn't be framed as one.

---

## 7. Alberta/Canada Angle

- Alberta Education's **Individualized Program Planning: ECS to Grade 12 (2006)** is the governing provincial resource — it does not mandate a specific IPP format but requires the components listed in the *Standards for Special Education (Amended June 2004)*. It explicitly includes a chapter on **promising practices for junior and senior high school** and a chapter on **assistive technology**, both directly relevant to this student profile.
- IPP goals must be reviewed regularly (not static), collaboratively developed with parents/students where possible, and should reflect current levels of performance realistically — for a Grade 2 reading level in Grade 7–9, that means goals framed around specific skill deficits and accommodation use, not a blanket "grade level" target.
- This workspace's existing Build Protocol two-hard-rules (every quiz item maps to an LO; every thinking prompt requires reasoning) already align well with this research: they keep assessment focused on content/thinking outcomes rather than accidentally measuring reading stamina — worth stating explicitly as a design constraint whenever building for this student profile.
