*Research session: October 2026. Built for Grade 5 English Language Arts and Literature (Alberta ELAL K–6, implemented September 2023). Covers how comprehension works, background knowledge, comprehension strategies, text level (leveled vs. grade-level text), fluency, vocabulary and morphology, spelling and word study, writing instruction, reading–writing links, grammar, discussion, assessment and feedback, motivation and reading volume, and students reading well below grade level. ## How to Read This File

This file is the evidence base for `_courses/grade-5-ela/_context.md` and any LA material built in this workspace. It sits beside the general files (`lesson-structure-research.md`, `metacognition-lasting-knowledge-research.md`, `udl-scaffolding-research.md`) and does not repeat them. Section 15 turns the findings into design rules.

**About the numbers.** Effect sizes (ES, d or g) are from the original paper or report where it was read, and from the abstract or a reliable secondary summary otherwise. Each one is marked:
- **[checked]** — read in the primary source or its abstract during this session.
- **[secondary]** — confirmed in a secondary summary only.
- **[memory]** — well known in the field but not re-checked this session. Check before quoting it in anything public.

Two cautions apply to every number here:
1. **Researcher-designed tests give much bigger effects than standardized tests.** In *Writing to Read*, the same practices scored 0.32 and 0.36 on standardized tests and 0.88 and 0.86 on researcher tests [checked]. Compare like with like.
2. **A bigger number is not automatically a better practice.** Effects depend on the comparison group, the length of the study and the students.

**The findings that matter most for Grade 5:**
1. **Comprehension runs on knowledge and language, not on skills alone.** A Grade 5 student who knows a topic understands texts about it far better. Genre units still need a topic.
2. **Comprehension strategies work and are learned quickly.** Teach them explicitly and briefly, then put the time into reading and discussing good texts.
3. **Writing instruction has some of the strongest evidence in education.** Explicit strategy teaching (SRSD), summary writing, sentence combining, peer work with structure, clear product goals and feedback that leads to revision.
4. **Writing about reading improves reading.** Summaries and extended written responses beat rereading and discussion alone.
5. **Isolated grammar drills don't improve writing.** Grammar taught inside sentence work and the student's own drafts does.
6. **Spelling instruction works and transfers to writing.** Words Their Way's own evidence base is thin, so use its sorts inside explicit, planned spelling teaching.
7. **Text level is a real debate.** Leveled text helps fluency and access, but only reading at "instructional level" keeps weaker readers away from grade-level language and ideas. Use both on purpose.
8. **Students far below grade level need real intervention, not just easier pages.** Effects for older struggling readers are smaller and slower than for young children. Classroom materials give access; intervention closes the gap.

Related files: `ld-ipp-reading-gap-research.md` (severe reading gaps, IPP), `significant-learning-gaps-research.md` (skill gap vs. cognitive disability), `udl-scaffolding-research.md` (access vs. cognitive scaffolds, fading), `graphic-organizers-research.md` (organizers for writing and text structure), `rubric-design-research.md` and `templates/rubric-phrase-bank.md` (writing rubrics), `metacognition-lasting-knowledge-research.md` (self-regulation, SRSD's roots), `project-based-learning-research.md` (critique cycles).

## Topic and Why It Matters

Every other course in this workspace is a content course: there is a body of knowledge, and the materials help students build it. Language arts is different. Most of the Grade 5 program is **skill practice inside long texts**: reading, writing, speaking, listening. The knowledge parts (genres, text structures, figurative language, conventions) are small next to the time students need to spend reading and writing.

That changes what good material looks like. A notes package can cover "what is a tall tale" in a page. It cannot teach a student to write one. The research below is about what actually moves reading and writing for 10- and 11-year-olds, and which of the workspace's existing rules carry over.

**The Grade 5 ELAL program** (Alberta, implemented September 2023) has six organizing ideas at Grade 5: Text Forms and Structures, Oral Language, Vocabulary, Comprehension, Writing, and Conventions. Phonological awareness and phonics end in earlier grades, and fluency is not a separate organizing idea at Grade 5. A Grade 5 class still includes students who need decoding and fluency work, which is why Section 14 exists.

---

## Core Finding

Grade 5 readers and writers improve most from **explicit teaching of a small number of strategies and craft moves, applied right away to rich texts on topics students are learning about, with a lot of writing about what they read, and feedback that leads to revision.** Knowledge and vocabulary grow alongside. Students who cannot yet read grade-level text accurately need targeted intervention on top of this, plus access to grade-level ideas through read-aloud and discussion so they don't fall further behind in knowledge.

## 1. How Reading Comprehension Works

### The Simple View of Reading (Gough & Tunmer 1986; Hoover & Gough 1990) [memory]
Reading comprehension = **decoding × language comprehension**. It is a product, not a sum: if either is near zero, comprehension is near zero. A student who decodes well but has weak vocabulary and background knowledge will struggle, and so will a student with rich language who cannot decode.

By Grade 5, for most students, language comprehension (vocabulary, background knowledge, syntax) explains more of the differences in reading comprehension than decoding does. For students still struggling to decode, decoding remains the bottleneck.

### Scarborough's Reading Rope (2001) [memory]
Splits the two strands into threads. **Language comprehension:** background knowledge, vocabulary, language structures (syntax, semantics), verbal reasoning (inference, metaphor), literacy knowledge (print concepts, genres). **Word recognition:** phonological awareness, decoding, sight recognition. Skilled reading is the threads becoming more automatic and more tightly woven.

Useful for teachers as a diagnostic picture: which thread is weak for this student?

### Construction-Integration model (Kintsch 1988, 1998) [memory]
Readers build two things: a **textbase** (what the text says) and a **situation model** (what it means, built by combining the text with what the reader knows). Inference happens when knowledge fills gaps the text leaves. A reader without the knowledge can recall sentences but cannot build the situation model.

### What it means for our materials
- **Diagnose before supporting.** A student who struggles with a text may have a decoding problem, a vocabulary problem, a knowledge problem, or a syntax problem. Each needs a different support (Section 14).
- **Comprehension questions should test the situation model** (why, what would happen if, how does this connect), not only the textbase (what did it say).
- **Syntax matters.** Long, complex sentences are a real barrier. Sentence-level work (Section 8) helps reading as well as writing.

## 2. Background Knowledge

### Recht & Leslie (1988) — "the baseball study" [checked]
*Journal of Educational Psychology, 80(1), 16–20.* 64 middle school students in four groups: good or poor readers × high or low baseball knowledge. They read a passage about half an inning of a game, then re-enacted it with figures. **Poor readers who knew baseball did better than good readers who didn't.** Knowledge mattered more than measured reading ability.
**Limits:** small sample, one topic, one task. It shows how much knowledge matters to comprehension, not that knowledge teaching beats everything else.

### Grissmer et al. (2023) — Core Knowledge charter school lottery study [checked, with caveats]
*Annenberg EdWorkingPaper 23-755.* Fourteen kindergarten lotteries at nine Core Knowledge charter schools in Colorado; 2,310 applicants. Students who won a lottery and attended a knowledge-rich curriculum from kindergarten scored about **16 percentile points higher** in reading by Grades 3–6 than students who lost. The effect was larger in the school serving more low-income students.
**Limits:** charter schools, one state, one curriculum, a working paper rather than a peer-reviewed article at the time of the search. The size of the effect is debated, but the direction matches a lot of other evidence.

### Willingham on knowledge and strategies [memory]
Daniel Willingham (*Why Don't Students Like School?*, 2009; *The Reading Mind*, 2017) argues that reading comprehension is not a general skill that transfers across topics. "Teaching content is teaching reading." This is already Principle 5 in `_meta-context.md`.

### CORI — Concept-Oriented Reading Instruction (Guthrie et al. 2004) [memory]
Grade 3 classes taught reading strategies inside a science topic, with hands-on activities and choice, did better on comprehension and motivation than strategy instruction alone. One of the few studies that combines knowledge, strategy and motivation.

### What it means for our materials
Units are by genre (your decision), and that works for Grade 5 ELAL because the outcomes are organized around text forms. But **a genre unit with unrelated texts on 20 topics builds no knowledge.** Each genre unit should have one **anchor topic** for its mentor texts and shared reading:
- Information report: a topic students can research in depth (e.g. a Grade 5 science or social topic).
- Opinion/editorial: one local issue, read from several sides.
- Tall tales: one tradition (e.g. North American tall tales), read in several versions.
- Lyric poetry: stories of place and land (an ELAL outcome).

Independent reading stays open (Section 13). Only the texts the whole class studies together share a topic.

## 3. Comprehension Strategy Instruction

### National Reading Panel (2000) [memory]
Found evidence for teaching comprehension monitoring, cooperative learning, graphic organizers, question answering, question generation, story structure, summarization, and **multiple strategies taught together** (e.g. reciprocal teaching).

### Rosenshine & Meister (1994) — reciprocal teaching [checked via *Writing to Read*]
Reciprocal teaching (predict, question, clarify, summarize, in a structured group) had an effect of about **0.32** on standardized tests.

### Willingham & Lovette (2014) — strategies are learned quickly [secondary]
*Teachers College Record.* Looked at eight reviews of reading comprehension strategy instruction. All found that strategy instruction helped. **None found that more sessions gave more benefit:** programs of about 6 sessions did as well as programs of 50. The authors' reading: strategies are a bag of tricks that students learn fast, not a skill that keeps growing with practice.
**The other side:** critics (e.g. Grant Wiggins's response) argue that the reviews weren't designed to test dose, and that applying strategies flexibly to harder texts does take practice. Fair point: the bag of tricks is learned quickly, but using it well on hard texts is part of reading itself.

### Text structure instruction — Hebert, Bohaty, Nelson & Brown (2016) [checked, partial]
*Journal of Educational Psychology, 108(5), 609–629.* 45 studies, Grades 1–12. Teaching students to recognize expository text structures (description, sequence, compare/contrast, cause/effect, problem/solution) improved comprehension of informational text, with a **large effect (ES = 0.96) for students with or at risk for disabilities.** The overall figure for all students was not confirmed in this session.
Grade 5 ELAL names "larger topics and subtopics" and "cause and effect" as non-fiction structures. These are worth teaching explicitly.

### Practice guides [memory]
- **IES: Improving Reading Comprehension in K–3** (Shanahan et al. 2010): teach strategies, teach text structure, guide focused discussion, choose texts purposely, build engagement.
- **IES: Providing Reading Interventions for Students in Grades 4–9** (Vaughn et al. 2022): build multisyllabic word reading, build vocabulary and morphology, build fluency with repeated reading, routinely use comprehension strategies (with monitoring and summarizing).

### What it means for our materials
- Teach each strategy **explicitly and briefly** (a mini-lesson or two with think-aloud), then fold it into ordinary reading. Don't run a "strategy of the week" all year.
- **Summarizing, questioning, monitoring and text structure** have the best support. "Making connections" (text-to-self) has the weakest and can pull readers away from the text.
- Strategies are tools for understanding *this* text. Questions should be about the text, not about the strategy ("What did you visualize?" is weaker than "What does the author want you to picture here, and which words do it?").

## 4. Text Level: Leveled vs. Grade-Level Text

This is the most contested area in this file, and the one that touches Fountas & Pinnell most directly.

### The idea behind guided reading levels
Fountas & Pinnell's guided reading (1996 onward) places students in small groups by **instructional level**: text the student reads at roughly 90–94% word accuracy with some comprehension support. The theory goes back to Betts (1946): text too hard frustrates, text too easy doesn't teach. F&P's level gradient (A–Z) is widely used in Canadian schools.

### The critique [checked / memory]
- **Shanahan** (many articles and his blog, *Shanahan on Literacy*; e.g. "Rejecting Instructional Level Theory") argues that the original "instructional level" criteria were never properly validated, and that keeping weaker readers in easier text denies them the vocabulary, syntax and ideas of grade-level text, so the gap grows. He argues for **grade-level text with heavy teacher support** for most whole-class work. [memory]
- **Amendum, Conradi & Hiebert (2018)**, *Educational Psychology Review, 30(1), 121–151* [checked]: reviewed 26 studies on text difficulty and elementary readers. Harder text generally went with lower fluency and lower comprehension, with exceptions. So text difficulty does matter, and the question is about how much support goes with it, not whether difficulty matters.
- **Burns et al. (2015)** [secondary]: matching texts to students' F&P instructional level did not lead to better reading outcomes than using other texts.
- **Three-cueing (MSV: meaning, structure, visual):** earlier F&P and Units of Study materials taught children to figure out unknown words from pictures, context and sentence sense as well as letters. Reading researchers say this encourages guessing instead of decoding. Lucy Calkins's 2022 revision of *Units of Study* dropped cueing; Heinemann said F&P materials were being updated with more decodable text, without naming what would be removed [checked, Education Week]. For Grade 5 this matters mostly for students who are still struggling to decode: they need to be taught to decode the word through its letters and parts, not to guess.

### Where the evidence supports levels
- Easier text helps **fluency practice** (accuracy needs to be high for repeated reading to work) and **independent reading volume** (Section 13).
- Leveled text is an access tool for students who cannot yet read grade-level text accurately.

### Verdict for this workspace
Use **both, on purpose**:

| Purpose | Text | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Whole-class mini-lesson, shared reading, mentor text study | **Grade-level or above**, read aloud or with support | Every student meets grade-level language, ideas and craft |
| Guided reading groups (F&P) | **Instructional level**, but not only: some sessions use grade-level text with more support | Builds accuracy and confidence without capping exposure |
| Fluency practice | **High-accuracy text** (instructional or slightly easier) | Repeated reading needs accurate reading |
| Independent reading | **Student choice, mostly independent level** | Volume and motivation |
| Assessment (cold reads) | **Grade-level** | Shows how the student does on Grade 5 text, which is what the outcome asks |

F&P levels decide **access**: which version of a text a student gets for practice, and how much support. They do not decide **what ideas or outcomes** a student works on. That matches the access-vs-cognitive-scaffold rule in `_meta-context.md`.

## 5. Fluency at Grade 5

### Why it still matters
Fluency (accurate, automatic, expressive reading) frees attention for comprehension. By Grade 5 most students are accurate; the differences are in speed and **prosody** (phrasing and expression). Prosody is a better predictor of comprehension than speed alone [memory].

### Repeated reading — Therrien (2004) [checked]
*Remedial and Special Education, 25(4), 252–261.* 18 studies, ages 5–18. Repeated reading improved fluency (**ES = 0.75**) and comprehension (**0.73**) on the practised passages. Effects on new passages were smaller and less certain. Best practice: an adult or trained peer, reading to a performance goal, with corrective feedback.

### Wide reading vs. repeated reading — Kuhn et al. (2006) [memory]
Second graders reading many different texts with support (wide reading) made fluency and comprehension gains similar to repeated reading. Both beat the usual practice. Reading *more text with support* matters more than the exact method.

### Reader's theatre [checked, mixed quality]
Students rehearse a script to perform it. Rehearsal is repeated reading with a real reason. Studies (e.g. Young & Rasinski 2009 with Grade 2) report gains in fluency and prosody; a 2023 meta-analysis (Mastrothanasis et al., 11 studies) found large effects on reading skills. Many studies are small, and not all are positive.
It fits Grade 5 ELAL directly: **scripted dramatic works** and **verbal, non-verbal and paraverbal language** are outcomes.

### What it means for our materials
- The drama unit carries fluency for the whole class through reader's theatre.
- Students still working on fluency get short, regular repeated reading with feedback (2–3 times a week, about 10 minutes, inside work time).
- **"Fluent = fast" is a misconception.** Fluency practice and checks should include phrasing and expression, not just words per minute.
- Reciting a poem from memory (an ELAL outcome) is fluency and oral language practice too.

## 6. Vocabulary and Morphology

### Vocabulary instruction
- **Stahl & Fairbanks (1986)** [memory]: vocabulary instruction improved comprehension of passages containing the taught words. Best results came from teaching both definitions and context, with several exposures and active processing.
- **Elleman, Lindo, Morphy & Compton (2009)**, *Journal of Research on Educational Effectiveness, 2(1), 1–44* [secondary]: vocabulary instruction improved comprehension on researcher tests (about 0.50) but **barely on standardized tests (about 0.10)**. Students with reading difficulties benefited about three times as much. Teaching words helps students understand texts with those words; it doesn't quickly raise general comprehension.
- **Beck, McKeown & Kucan, *Bringing Words to Life* (2002, 2013)** [memory]: **three tiers** of words. Tier 1 everyday (dog, happy); **Tier 2 general academic words** that appear across many texts (reluctant, consequence, analyze); Tier 3 subject-specific (photosynthesis, stanza). Tier 2 words give the most return for teaching time. "Robust" instruction: student-friendly definition, examples and non-examples, use in several contexts, students produce the word, many encounters over time.

### Morphology
- **Goodwin & Ahn (2010)** [secondary]: morphology instruction improved literacy for children with literacy difficulties (**d = 0.33**). Morphology as part of broader literacy teaching worked (0.36); morphology in isolation showed no significant effect.
- **Goodwin & Ahn (2013)**, *Scientific Studies of Reading, 17(4)*, and **Bowers, Kirby & Deacon (2010)**, *Review of Educational Research* [memory]: similar findings for school-age children generally: small to moderate effects, larger for weaker readers and younger students, larger when integrated with other literacy instruction.

Grade 5 is the right age: students meet many multisyllabic words, and prefixes, suffixes and roots (Latin and Greek) unlock meaning and spelling at once. The ELAL Vocabulary organizing idea covers word meaning and **structures**.

### Figurative language
Grade 5 ELAL asks students to examine figurative language and use a variety of words. Figurative language is a vocabulary and inference skill: understanding *why* "the classroom was a zoo" works requires knowing both things and seeing the link. Teach it with examples from the mentor texts, and have students explain the comparison, not just name the device.

### What it means for our materials
- Each unit names **6–10 Tier 2 words** from its mentor texts, taught robustly. This follows the workspace rule "vocabulary is the knowledge": definition, example, non-example, application. Never copying definitions.
- **Morphology** is taught in short weekly routines and linked to Words Their Way's syllables-and-affixes and derivational stages (Section 7).
- Retrieval warm-ups include vocabulary and morphology (generate a word with *-ful*, explain what *reluctant* means in this sentence).
- Device-spotting ("find a simile") is the weakest task. "What does this comparison make you picture, and why did the author choose it?" asks for reasoning.

## 7. Spelling and Word Study

### Spelling instruction works — Graham & Santangelo (2014) [checked, abstract]
*Reading and Writing, 27, 1703–1743.* 53 studies, 6,037 students, K–12.
- Formal spelling instruction vs. none: **ES = 0.54**. Vs. informal or incidental approaches ("they'll pick it up from reading and writing"): **0.43**.
- More formal instruction: **0.70**.
- Gains lasted (**0.53**) and **transferred to spelling in students' own writing (0.94)**.
- Spelling instruction also improved phonological awareness (0.51) and reading (0.44).

### Developmental spelling stages (Henderson; Templeton; Bear et al.) [memory]
The theory behind Words Their Way: children's spelling moves through stages: emergent, letter name–alphabetic, **within word pattern**, **syllables and affixes**, **derivational relations**. Most Grade 5 students are in the last three. Teaching is matched to the stage the student is in, found with a spelling inventory.

### Words Their Way — what the evidence says [checked]
- The **What Works Clearinghouse** (2013 review) found **no studies of Words Their Way that met its standards**, so it could not say whether the program works.
- A randomized study with Grade 2–3 students (Eddy et al. 2011) found a **very small effect (0.05)**.
- Most other studies are small theses and action research, with positive results.

So: the *approach* (systematic, explicit spelling instruction organized around patterns and morphology) has good support from the meta-analysis above. The *program* itself has not been well tested. The sorts are a reasonable vehicle as long as they are used inside **explicit teaching**: the teacher names and explains the pattern, students sort and then **produce** spellings (write words from memory, use them in sentences), and the pattern shows up in their writing.

### Weak spots to manage with WTW
- **Sorting is recognition, not retrieval.** Students can sort by matching visual patterns without being able to spell the words later. Add production: blind sorts, writing sorts (teacher says the word, student writes it in the right column), and spelling the pattern in new words.
- **Transfer to writing doesn't happen by itself.** Editing checklists should name the student's current patterns, and conferences should point to them.
- **Grouping by stage can turn into tracking.** Keep it to the word-study block. Stage decides the word list, not the rest of the LA program.

### What it means for our materials
- WTW stays the spelling program. Our materials don't replace the sorts.
- The **interleaved Word Work & Conventions practice** adds what sorts lack: retrieval (spell from memory), mixed review of earlier patterns, morphology for meaning, and use in sentences.
- Each unit's editing checklist includes "my spelling patterns this week."

## 8. Writing Instruction

Writing has one of the strongest evidence bases in literacy, mostly from Steve Graham, Karen Harris and colleagues.

### Writing Next — Graham & Perin (2007) [secondary]
*Alliance for Excellent Education / Carnegie.* Meta-analysis for Grades 4–12. Eleven recommended practices, in order of effect:
| Practice | ES |
|---|---|
| Writing strategies (planning, revising, editing) | 0.82 |
| Summarization | 0.82 |
| Collaborative writing (structured peer work) | 0.75 |
| Specific product goals | 0.70 |
| Word processing | 0.55 |
| Sentence combining | 0.50 |
| Prewriting | 0.32 |
| Inquiry activities | 0.32 |
| Process writing approach | 0.32 |
| Study of models | 0.25 |
| Writing for content learning | 0.23 |
| *Explicit grammar instruction (not recommended)* | *−0.32* |
These figures come from secondary summaries; strategy instruction, summarization, sentence combining and grammar were confirmed in two or more.

### Elementary meta-analysis — Graham, McKeown, Kiuhara & Harris (2012) [checked, abstract]
*Journal of Educational Psychology, 104(4), 879–896.* 115 studies, Grades 1–6.
| Practice | ES |
|---|---|
| Strategy instruction | 1.02 |
| **SRSD (Self-Regulated Strategy Development)** | **1.17** |
| Peer assistance | 0.89 |
| Product goals | 0.76 |
| Text structure instruction | 0.59 |
| Transcription (handwriting, spelling, typing) | 0.55 |
| Prewriting activities | 0.54 |
| Adding self-regulation to strategy instruction | 0.50 |
| Comprehensive writing programs | 0.42 |
| Process approach | 0.40 |
| Extra writing time | 0.30 |
| Grammar instruction | not significant |

### SRSD — Self-Regulated Strategy Development (Harris & Graham) [memory + above]
The highest-effect writing approach. Six stages, taught to mastery, not by the calendar:
1. **Develop background knowledge** (what makes a good opinion piece, vocabulary).
2. **Discuss it** (the strategy, why it helps, the student's current writing).
3. **Model it** (teacher thinks aloud while planning and writing, including self-talk).
4. **Memorize it** (a mnemonic, e.g. POW + TREE for opinion: Topic sentence, Reasons, Explain, Ending).
5. **Support it** (shared and guided practice, supports fading).
6. **Independent performance.**
Self-regulation is taught alongside: goal setting, self-monitoring, self-instruction, self-reinforcement. This matches `_meta-context.md` Principles 7 and 9 almost exactly.

### WWC Practice Guide: Teaching Elementary School Students to Be Effective Writers (Graham et al. 2012) [memory]
1. Provide daily time for students to write (the guide suggests about an hour a day from Grade 1, including writing across subjects).
2. Teach students to use the writing process for a variety of purposes (strategies, genres, modelling).
3. Teach students to become fluent with handwriting, spelling, sentence construction, typing and word processing.
4. Create an engaged community of writers.

### Sentence-level work
- **Sentence combining** (Saddler & Graham 2005 [memory]; Writing Next 0.50): students combine short "kernel" sentences into longer ones. Builds syntax for writing and reading.
- **The Writing Revolution** (Hochman & Wexler 2017) [memory]: sentence activities such as *because / but / so*, appositives, sentence expansion, and single-paragraph outlines, done with content students are learning. It is built on the sentence-combining and writing-about-text research but **has no large controlled studies of its own**. Use the activities; don't cite the program as proven.

### Mentor texts and models
"Study of models" alone has a small effect (0.25). Models work when the teacher points out the craft move, explains why it works, and students try it in their own writing right away, which is how SRSD's modelling stage works. A mentor text is the writing equivalent of a worked example; the self-explanation prompt is "What did the author do here, and why?"

### Writing time
Grade 5 students typically write far less than the guide recommends [memory: US surveys found about 15 minutes a day in elementary]. With a single 60-minute block, writing time has to come from work time on most days, and from writing in other subjects.

### What it means for our materials
- Each writing unit is built as **SRSD**: background → discuss → model (think-aloud, in the teacher guide) → memorize (a mnemonic) → supported practice with fading → independent.
- **Specific product goals** for every piece ("include a counter-argument and answer it"), not "write a good opinion piece."
- **Structured peer work**: roles, a checklist, a specific question. Free peer editing spreads errors (Principle 8).
- **Sentence work** in warm-ups and the Writer's Notebook: combining, expanding, *because/but/so*, using the unit's mentor text topic.
- **Transcription fluency** for students who need it: typing or handwriting practice is a real intervention, not busywork (0.55).

## 9. Reading–Writing Links

### Writing to Read — Graham & Hebert (2010) [checked]
*Carnegie Corporation report; Harvard Educational Review 2011.* Meta-analysis of writing practices' effects on reading (Grades 1–12, mostly middle and high school).
**Core finding 1 — Write about what you read.**
- Writing about text improved comprehension: **0.40** on standardized tests. 57 of 61 outcomes were positive.
- Writing about text **beat** just reading, rereading, studying, discussing, or getting reading instruction (0.35 standardized, 0.49 researcher tests).
- **Extended writing** (personal response, analysis, interpretation): all 9 comparisons positive.
- **Summary writing: 0.79 for elementary students** (4 studies), 0.33 for middle/high (11 studies).
- **Note-taking**: 21 of 23 comparisons positive. Answering or generating written questions: small but consistently positive.
- **Lower-achieving students: 0.63**, but **only when they were explicitly taught how** to do the writing activity. Without instruction, the effect was not greater than zero.

**Core finding 2 — Teach students to write.** Writing instruction (process, text structure, sentence and paragraph construction) improved reading comprehension a little (0.23 standardized, 0.30 researcher tests). Teaching spelling and sentence construction improved reading fluency and word reading.

**Core finding 3 — Write more.** Increasing how much students write improved reading comprehension (**0.30** on standardized tests).

### Reading for Writing — Graham et al. (2018) [checked, abstract]
The reverse direction: reading instruction improved writing (overall 0.57, quality 0.63, spelling 0.56), and the effect lasted (0.37).

### What it means for our materials
- **Every reading lesson ends in writing**: a summary, a written response with evidence, notes. This is the strongest single argument for the Reader's/Writer's Notebook.
- **Teach the writing-about-reading move before asking for it**, especially for weaker readers (the 0.63 effect needed instruction).
- Summary writing is worth teaching formally at Grade 5 (ELAL: "respond to texts by summarizing").
- The mentor text a student reads is the model for the piece they write.

## 10. Grammar and Conventions

### Isolated grammar doesn't transfer [secondary]
Teaching grammar as a separate subject (labelling parts of speech, diagramming, worksheets) had a **negative** effect on writing quality in *Writing Next* (−0.32) and no significant effect in the elementary meta-analysis. This finding is old and repeated (Braddock 1963; Hillocks 1986; Andrews et al. 2006 [memory]).

### What does work
- **Sentence combining** (0.50).
- **Grammar taught in context**: a convention introduced through a mentor sentence, practised in sentences students build, then used and checked in their own drafts. The evidence for "in context" grammar is weaker than the negative finding for drills, but it is consistent with the sentence-combining and transfer evidence [memory: Myhill et al. 2012, UK RCT on contextualized grammar, positive for more able writers].
- **Editing for a short list of target conventions**, not "fix everything."

### Grade 5 ELAL Conventions
Capitalization, abbreviations, punctuation including parentheses, and experimenting with capitalization and punctuation "to achieve a desired effect", plus spelling patterns. The "desired effect" wording invites the mentor-sentence approach: why did the author use parentheses here?

### What it means for our materials
- Conventions live in **mentor sentences** ("notice and imitate"), sentence-building practice, and the editing checklist. No standalone grammar worksheets.
- Interleaved Word Work & Conventions practice asks students to **produce** (punctuate, combine, fix and explain), not to label.

## 11. Oral Language and Discussion

### Discussion improves comprehension — Murphy et al. (2009) [memory]
*Journal of Educational Psychology.* Meta-analysis of classroom discussion approaches. Many approaches reduced teacher talk and increased student talk; several improved literal and inferential comprehension. Increasing student talk alone did not reliably improve comprehension: the structure of the discussion mattered.

### Dialogic teaching — EEF trial (Jay et al. 2017) [checked]
Robin Alexander's Dialogic Teaching, 20 weeks with **Year 5** classes in England, 76 schools, about 5,000 pupils. **About two months' extra progress in English** and science, one in maths. Low cost.
**Limits:** heavy data loss in the treatment group (over 21%), which critics say could bias the result; EEF rated it moderate security.

### Accountable talk (Michaels, O'Connor & Resnick) [memory]
Talk that is accountable to the community (builds on others), to reasoning, and to knowledge (evidence from the text). Talk moves: "Say more", "Who can add to that?", "What in the text makes you think so?", "Do you agree or disagree, and why?"

### What it means for our materials
- Peer talk is always **structured**: a specific question, a talk move or role, a product (Principle 8).
- **Text-based discussion** before writing gives students ideas and language to write with.
- Grade 5 ELAL oral language outcomes (collaborative dialogue; verbal, non-verbal and paraverbal communication) are assessed through planned talk tasks, not just participation marks.

## 12. Feedback and Assessment in Literacy

### Formative assessment in writing — Graham, Hebert & Harris (2015) [memory]
*Elementary School Journal.* Meta-analysis, Grades 1–8. Feedback improved writing: **from adults (about 0.87), self-assessment (about 0.62), peers (about 0.58)**. Teaching students to self-assess against clear criteria worked well. Machine feedback was smaller.

### Rubrics and exemplars
See `rubric-design-research.md`. Rubrics help (g = 0.45) when students use them in a draft → feedback/self-assessment → revise cycle. Descriptive levels, 3–5 criteria, several different exemplars.

### Writing conferences [memory]
One-to-one or small-group talks during writing time. Strong practitioner tradition (Graves, Calkins, Anderson); the research base is mostly descriptive, but conferences are where feedback-with-a-response happens naturally. Best when brief, focused on one teaching point, and the student leaves with something to do now.

### Comprehension assessment
- **Different reading tests measure different things.** Comprehension test results depend on the passage topic, the question format and whether decoding or knowledge carries more weight [memory: Keenan, Betjemann & Olson 2008]. One cold read is a weak signal; several over the unit are better.
- **Cold reads** (an unseen grade-level passage, short-answer questions) show whether students can apply skills to new text. Passages need to be on reasonably familiar topics or have a short knowledge primer, or they measure knowledge more than skill.
- **F&P Benchmark Assessment System**: useful to find a starting level for guided reading. As a screener, a 2015 study (Parker et al.) found it identified at-risk Grade 2–3 readers correctly about **54%** of the time, compared with **80%** for a one-minute oral reading fluency measure [secondary]. Use a fluency measure (e.g. DIBELS or a school ORF tool) to screen, and the BAS for placement and observation.
- **Running records** show what a reader does with words (errors, self-corrections). Score error patterns for decoding (did the student use the letters?), not for "used meaning cues."

### What it means for our materials
- Writing: **rubric + exemplars + self-assessment + revision** every piece. Teacher feedback names one or two moves to make in the next draft.
- Comprehension: **cold-read checks** mapped to LOs, several per unit, short, grade-level, with any needed background given.
- Exit tickets still work: one question about today's text, one about today's craft move.
- Every assessment item maps to an LO (workspace hard rule).

## 13. Motivation, Choice and Reading Volume

### Reading volume [memory / checked]
- Print exposure is correlated with vocabulary, comprehension and knowledge across ages (Mol & Bus 2011, *Psychological Bulletin*, meta-analysis of correlations) [memory]. Strong readers read more and get stronger ("Matthew effects", Stanovich 1986).
- But the **National Reading Panel (2000) found too little evidence that sustained silent reading (SSR) time improves reading** [checked]. Reasons offered: students may not actually read, may choose texts too hard or too easy, and get no feedback.
- Hiebert and colleagues found that students, especially weaker readers, often don't read much during independent reading time unless it is structured [checked, abstract].

### What makes independent reading work [memory]
Book choice with guidance, texts students can read, accountability that isn't a burden (a short response, a conference, a reading log that asks one real question), and teacher conferring. "Scaffolded" independent reading does better than plain SSR.

### Choice [memory]
Patall, Cooper & Robinson (2008) meta-analysis: choice raises intrinsic motivation and effort, more so when choices are meaningful and few. CORI (Section 2) found motivation and knowledge reinforced each other.

### Reading stamina
Grade 5 ELAL: "Develop reading stamina by engaging with text that is personally enjoyable." Stamina grows with interest and success. Students reading well below grade level need texts that are both readable and age-appropriate in content (high-interest, lower-level books).

### What it means for our materials
- Independent reading is **structured**: choice within a range, a goal, a short weekly response or conference.
- Genre units give choice through **genre-matched independent reading** (read a tall tale of your choice) while the whole class shares the anchor-topic texts.
- Reading logs ask a thinking question, not just minutes.

## 14. Students Reading Well Below Grade Level

This section feeds the two extra versions of each unit (Scaffolded and Below Reading Level) in the course plan. Read it with `ld-ipp-reading-gap-research.md` and `significant-learning-gaps-research.md`.

### Older struggling readers improve more slowly [checked]
- **Wanzek et al. (2013)**, *Review of Educational Research, 83(2), 163–195*: extensive interventions (75+ sessions) for students after Grade 3. Mean effects **0.10–0.16** across comprehension, word reading, fluency and spelling. Group size, hours and grade did not change results.
- **Scammacca et al. (2015)**, Grades 4–12 interventions 1980–2011: mean effect **0.49**, much smaller than the 0.95 found in their 2007 review, and smaller on standardized tests [secondary]. Effects have shrunk as control classrooms improved and studies got stricter.

**What this means:** a Grade 5 student reading at a Grade 2 level will not catch up from classroom adaptations. They need targeted intervention (phonics and multisyllabic decoding for decoding gaps; vocabulary and language for language gaps) on top of class, ideally daily, plus access to grade-level ideas so knowledge and vocabulary don't fall further behind.

### Finding the gap
Use the Simple View (Section 1):
| Profile | Signs | Main need |
|---|---|---|
| **Decoding gap** | Inaccurate oral reading, guesses at long words, comprehension fine when text is read aloud | Decoding/multisyllabic word intervention; listening access to grade-level text |
| **Language/knowledge gap** | Reads accurately, but comprehension is weak even when read aloud | Vocabulary, background knowledge, syntax, discussion |
| **Both** | Weak in both | Both, with intervention as the priority |
| **Fluency only** | Accurate but slow, flat | Repeated reading, reader's theatre |
Listening comprehension of grade-level text (read it aloud, ask questions) is the quickest screen to separate the first two.

### F&P Leveled Literacy Intervention [checked]
The WWC (2017) found **positive effects on general reading achievement** for LLI, based on two studies of **beginning readers in Grades K–2**. Evidence for Grade 5 is weaker. Shanahan and others note LLI has used cueing practices (Section 4). If the school uses LLI for Grade 5 students, pair it with explicit multisyllabic decoding.

### Read-aloud and text-to-speech: access vs. cognitive scaffold
- **Wood et al. (2018)**, *Journal of Learning Disabilities, 51(1), 73–84* [checked]: text-to-speech and read-aloud improved reading comprehension for students with reading disabilities, **ES = 0.35**, Grade 3 to college.
- A 2023 meta-analysis of reading while listening for students in general found a smaller benefit (g = 0.18) [checked].

The rule from `udl-scaffolding-research.md` applied to LA:
| Outcome being taught or assessed | Read-aloud / TTS is... |
|---|---|
| Comprehension of ideas, theme, character, text structure | **Access scaffold**: may stay |
| Writing about a text | **Access scaffold**: may stay |
| Vocabulary in context | Usually access |
| Decoding, word reading | **Removes the skill**: not on these tasks |
| Fluency | **Removes the skill**: not on these tasks |
Every LA support in our materials is labelled access or cognitive in the teacher notes.

### Writing supports and how to fade them
- **SRSD** was developed for and works especially well with struggling writers and students with LD (the Graham meta-analyses).
- **Graphic organizers** for planning help (`graphic-organizers-research.md`), learner-completed better than pre-filled.
- **Sentence frames** ("One reason is ___ because ___") are a cognitive scaffold for organization. Plan the fade: full frame → sentence starter → word list → none.
- **Dictation / speech-to-text** is an access scaffold when the outcome is composing ideas and organization; it removes the skill when the outcome is spelling or handwriting.
- **Typing** may help students whose handwriting slows them, once they type reasonably fast.

### English language learners [memory]
WWC practice guide *Teaching Academic Content and Literacy to English Learners* (Baker et al. 2014): teach academic vocabulary intensively over several days, integrate oral and written language into content teaching, give regular structured writing opportunities, and give small-group support for students who need it. Visuals, word banks for access (not on retrieval tasks), sentence frames for academic talk, and home-language connections where possible.

### What it means for our materials
- **Same unit, same topic, same mentor text, same writing piece** for all three versions, so students are in the same lesson.
- The **Scaffolded** version keeps the Grade 5 outcomes and adds frames, part-filled organizers, a worked model beside each task, and chunked steps, each with a planned fade.
- The **Below Reading Level** version (Easy Read spec) gives grade-level text by read-aloud or audio for comprehension and writing tasks, a separate passage at the student's level for decoding and fluency, and draw/label/dictate options. It keeps the same outcomes unless an IPP team has set modified outcomes (Track B).
- Teacher notes give the gap profile table above so the teacher can choose the right version per student and per task.

## 16. Stronger Options Than F&P or Words Their Way Alone

*Added 2026-10-23.* Neither F&P guided reading nor Words Their Way has a strong independent evidence base, so "better efficacy" here means practices with stronger evidence that can run **inside** the same block. No study compares these head to head with F&P or Words Their Way; the effect sizes come from different studies and are a rough ranking only.

### Reading and writing

| Practice | Effect | Source | Grade 5 shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| SRSD strategy writing | 1.17 | Graham et al. 2012 [checked] | Strategy + mnemonic, think-aloud model, memorize, supported practice, self-regulation |
| Text structure instruction | 0.96 (students with or at risk for disabilities) | Hebert et al. 2016 [checked] | Topic/subtopic, cause and effect, signal words, matching organizer |
| Summary writing | 0.79 (elementary, 4 studies) | Graham & Hebert 2010 [checked] | Short written summaries, taught step by step |
| Writing about reading | 0.40 standardized | Graham & Hebert 2010 [checked] | Every reading lesson ends in writing; teach the move first |
| Explicit spelling instruction | 0.54; transfer to writing 0.94 | Graham & Santangelo 2014 [checked] | Name the pattern, practise, spell from memory, apply when editing |
| Sentence combining | 0.50 | Graham & Perin 2007 [secondary] | Combine kernel sentences from the unit's texts |
| Morphology | 0.33 (larger when integrated and for weaker readers) | Goodwin & Ahn 2010 [secondary] | Weekly prefix/suffix/root routines tied to reading and spelling |
| Structured discussion | ~2 months' progress (Year 5) | EEF Dialogic Teaching [checked] | Talk moves, one question, a product |
| Topic-linked texts | ~16 percentile points (Gr 3–6) | Grissmer et al. 2023 [checked] | One anchor topic per genre unit for shared texts |

### Fluency

| Practice | Effect | Source | Grade 5 shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| Repeated reading with feedback | 0.75 fluency, 0.73 comprehension (practised passages) | Therrien 2004 [checked] | 3–4 reads to a goal, adult or trained partner, error correction |
| HELPS (10-minute fluency routine) | 0.74 (Grade 2, Begeny et al. 2011); 1.21 in a small ELL RCT | NSSA / WWC study listings [secondary] | Model, repeated reading, phrase drill, goal graph, praise. Listed in the WWC K–3 foundational guide; reported to be free from its developer (confirm) |
| Multisyllabic word reading (REWARDS-style) | ~0.38–0.47 on word-reading fluency (Gr 3–4 RCT, Toste et al. 2017) | NSSA listing [secondary] | Circle prefixes and suffixes, chunk the rest, say the parts, say the word |
| Reader's theatre | Large in small studies (Mastrothanasis et al. 2023, 11 studies) | [checked, mixed quality] | Rehearse a script over a week, perform |
| PALS partner reading | "Potentially positive" on comprehension for Gr 2–6 struggling readers (WWC; report not confirmed) | [secondary] | Partner reading with error correction, retell, paragraph shrinking, prediction relay |
| Wide reading with support | Similar to repeated reading (Kuhn et al. 2006) | [memory] | Several different texts a week with support |

### How F&P and Words Their Way change
- **F&P guided reading:** group by need (decoding, fluency, comprehension), not only by level; some sessions on grade-level text with support; use leveled books for fluency practice and independent reading. Screen with an oral reading fluency measure (about 80% correct classification vs. 54% for the BAS, §12).
- **Words Their Way:** keep the sorts, but teach each pattern explicitly first; add production (blind sorts, writing sorts, spelling from memory); add morphology for meaning; put each student's current patterns on their editing checklist. No direct study compares sorts with other explicit spelling methods [checked: none found].

Sources for this section, beyond those listed below: [HELPS, NSSA listing](https://nssa.stanford.edu/studies/effects-helps-reading-fluency-program-when-implemented-classroom-teachers-low-performing); [WWC HELPS study](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Study/94731); [multisyllabic word reading RCT](https://nssa.stanford.edu/studies/multisyllabic-word-reading-instruction-and-without-motivational-beliefs-training-struggling); [PALS, WWC summary](https://youthtoday.org/2012/05/elementary-education-studies-show-mixed-results-for-peer-assisted-learning-strategies/).

## What the Evidence Doesn't Support

- **Comprehension strategies as the main content of reading lessons all year.** Gains level off quickly (§3).
- **Decontextualized skills on random short passages.** Builds no knowledge (§2).
- **Keeping weaker readers only in easy text.** Limits exposure to grade-level language and ideas (§4).
- **Three-cueing (guessing words from pictures and context).** Teach decoding through letters and word parts (§4).
- **Isolated grammar worksheets.** No transfer, possibly harmful to writing quality (§10).
- **Unstructured silent reading** as a guaranteed gain (§13).
- **Copying vocabulary definitions** (§6; workspace rule).
- **Peer editing without structure** (§8, §11).
- **"Learning styles" or matching reading to a preferred modality** [memory: Pashler et al. 2008].
- **Claims that a specific program is proven** when only its approach is: Words Their Way, The Writing Revolution (§7, §8).
