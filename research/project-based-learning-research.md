# Project-Based Learning — Research Summary
*Research session: October 2026. Covers the PBL design literature (Krajcik & Blumenfeld 1991 → PBLWorks Gold Standard 2015/2019), the Lucas Education Research randomized trials (2016–2022), meta-analyses through 2026, the minimal-guidance critique, and emerging AI-supported PBL.*

---

## How to Read This File

This file supports the **Elaborate + Explore** phase of the unit architecture in `_meta-context.md` (the inquiry project brief) and reconciles project-based learning with the Hattie numbers already documented in `research/hattie-visible-learning-research.md`.

The key findings that matter most:
1. **Structured PBL works; unstructured PBL doesn't.** Every positive randomized trial used a heavily scaffolded curriculum with explicit instruction built inside the project.
2. **Project-based ≠ problem-based.** Hattie's d = 0.15 is for *problem-based* learning, mostly from medical schools. K–12 *project-based* meta-analyses land at d ≈ 0.44–0.71, and the best trials show real achievement gains.
3. **Implementation quality explains most of the variance.** Teacher support, project length, and group size all moderate the effect strongly.
4. **The North American caveat is real.** In the largest recent meta-analysis, studies from North America showed almost no effect (0.06). Most of the large pooled effects come from Asian quasi-experiments. Trust the US randomized trials over pooled averages.

---

## Topic and Why It Matters

Project-based learning (PBL) organizes learning around a sustained investigation of a driving question that ends in a product or presentation for a real audience. The "project" is the vehicle for learning content, not a reward after it.

It matters for this framework because:
- Every unit in the materials suite includes an **inquiry project brief**, and this file is the evidence base for designing it
- PBL is widely promoted as "student-centred," and `hattie-visible-learning-research.md` already warns that *student-centred is not a proxy for evidence-based*. This file says where that warning applies and where it doesn't
- The randomized trials show PBL can **narrow gaps** for low-income students when it is done well

---

## Core Finding

**PBL improves achievement and engagement when the project is the main vehicle for learning, *and* the teacher builds explicit instruction, scaffolds, and critique-and-revision cycles into it.** When those structures are missing, PBL drifts toward minimally guided discovery, which the cognitive-load evidence predicts will fail (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006). Both claims are true at the same time. The design is what decides which one you get.

## Key Studies

### 1. Saavedra et al. (2022) — Knowledge in Action (KIA), AP courses (RCT)

**The strongest high-school evidence.**

Schools in five large urban US districts were randomly assigned to teach AP U.S. Government or AP Environmental Science with the KIA project-based curriculum plus sustained teacher PD, or to teach as usual. Most students were Black or Hispanic, and a large share came from low-income households.

- **Year 1:** KIA raised the share of students scoring 3+ (college credit) by about **8 percentage points, from ~37% to ~45%**. Reported effect size **0.46** (p = .002, n = 3,645).
- **Low-income students:** 0.39. **Higher-income students:** 0.50. Both groups benefited.
- **Year 2 (teachers' second year with KIA):** **+10 percentage points**, effect size **0.53** (n = 2,946). The approach gets better as teachers gain experience with it.
- Students said the projects felt authentic and that they felt prepared for the AP exam. Over 90% of teachers recommended the approach, even though the shift was hard.

**Design principles of KIA:** projects form the *spine* of the course. Key content spirals across projects. Each project creates a "need to know." Instructional practices are chosen deliberately to drive engagement.

**Source:** Saavedra, A. R., et al. (2022). The impact of project-based learning on AP exam performance. *Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis, 44*(4), 638–666. Summary brief: Lucas Education Research / College Board.

*Note: the KIA effect sizes are reported on the probability of a qualifying score, so they are not directly comparable to Cohen's d from achievement-test meta-analyses.*

---

### 2. Krajcik et al. (2021) — ML-PBL, 3rd-grade science (RCT)

- 2018–19 school year. 2,371 third graders across Michigan and Wisconsin, 91 teachers, four science units.
- ML-PBL students scored **about 8% higher** on the state science assessment than the control group.
- Gains held across **reading levels, socioeconomic status, race/ethnicity, and special-education status**.
- ML-PBL students also showed measurable gains in collaboration and reflection.

**Source:** Krajcik, J., Schneider, B., et al. (2021/2023). Assessing the effect of project-based learning on science learning in elementary schools. *American Educational Research Journal*. Funded by Lucas Education Research.

---

### 3. Duke, Halvorsen et al. (2021) — Project PLACE, 2nd grade (RCT)

- 2nd graders in 20 high-poverty, low-achieving Michigan schools were randomly assigned to project-based social studies or to the school's usual instruction.
- PBL students gained **about 5–6 extra months of learning in social studies** and **about 2 extra months in informational reading**.
- An earlier pilot (Halvorsen, Duke et al., 2012) found **no SES achievement gap** after project-based instruction on the standards-based assessments.

**Source:** Duke, N. K., Halvorsen, A.-L., et al. (2021). Putting PjBL to the test: The impact of project-based learning on second graders' social studies and literacy learning and motivation in low-SES school settings. *American Educational Research Journal, 58*(1).

### 4. Meta-analyses

| Study | Scope | Pooled effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Chen & Yang (2019)**, *Educational Research Review* | 30 studies, 12,585 students, 9 countries (1998–2017) | **d = 0.71** on academic achievement vs. traditional instruction |
| **Zhang & Ma (2023)**, *Frontiers in Psychology* | 66 studies, 190 effects (20 years) | **Overall 0.44**. Achievement **0.65**. Affective attitudes 0.39. Thinking skills 0.39 |
| **Stojadinović et al. (2021)** | 18 studies | **g = 0.39** on academic achievement |
| **GenAI-supported problem/project-based learning (2026)**, three-level meta-analysis | 22 controlled studies, 66 effects (2023–2026) | **g = 0.82** across achievement, higher-order thinking, motivation, and collaboration |
| **Hattie, *Visible Learning*** (for comparison) | *Problem*-based learning | **d = 0.15**. Inquiry-based teaching 0.31. Discovery 0.21 |

**Moderators from Zhang & Ma (2023)** that matter for design:

| Moderator | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Group size** | Groups of **4–5: 0.91**. 6–7: 0.44. 8+: 0.51 |
| **Duration** | **9–18 weeks: 0.67**. 1–8 weeks: 0.50. One-off: 0.36. 18+ weeks: ~0.30 |
| **Grade band** | High school 0.72. Primary 0.53. Junior high 0.52. University 0.12 |
| **Subject** | Engineering/technology 0.62. Natural science 0.48. Humanities/social 0.28 |
| **Class size** | Small 0.48. Medium 0.47. Large 0.11 (n.s.) |
| **Region** | SE Asia 0.68. East Asia 0.55. **Western Europe 0.08. North America 0.06** |

Several moderator cells contain very few studies, and the paper's own figures are not fully consistent between its tables and text. Treat these as directional, not precise.

### 5. The critique and the rebuttal

**Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006):** minimally guided instruction (discovery, inquiry, problem-based, project-based) overloads working memory, especially for novices, and decades of advocacy have not produced strong evidence for it.

**Hmelo-Silver, Duncan & Chinn (2007):** well-designed problem-based and inquiry learning is *not* minimally guided. It scaffolds heavily, which reduces cognitive load and lets students learn in complex domains.

**Condliffe et al. / MDRC (2017):** PBL is "promising but not yet proven." The weak points were few experimental studies, inconsistent implementation fidelity, and weak outcome measures. The Lucas-funded RCTs (Studies 1–3) were largely the field's response.

**Reconciliation for this framework:** the critique is about *guidance*, not about *projects*. A project with explicit mini-lessons, worked examples, and planned scaffold fading is consistent with Principle 5 (knowledge first) and the cognitive-load research. A project where students "go research it" is not.

| Stojadinović 2021 | Meta-analysis | g = 0.39 |
| Hattie hinge point | Benchmark | 0.40 |
| Hattie: problem-based learning | Meta-synthesis | 0.15 |
| KIA AP, year 1 / year 2 | RCT | +8 / +10 points pass rate (ES 0.46 / 0.53) |
| ML-PBL grade 3 science | RCT | ~8% higher state test scores |
| Project PLACE grade 2 | RCT | +5–6 months social studies, +2 months informational reading |

**The honest read:** pooled averages sit at or above the 0.40 hinge, but the spread is wide. The US RCTs show moderate, real gains *with strong curriculum and PD*. The North American meta-analytic subgroup shows that typical, unsupported implementations can produce close to nothing.

## What It Looks Like in Practice

### PBLWorks Gold Standard — 7 Essential Project Design Elements
1. **Challenging problem or question**: a driving question at the right level of challenge
2. **Sustained inquiry**: asking questions, finding resources, applying information, over time
3. **Authenticity**: real-world context, tasks, tools, or personal relevance
4. **Student voice and choice**: decisions about process and product
5. **Reflection**: on learning, on the inquiry, on obstacles
6. **Critique and revision**: feedback cycles that change the work
7. **Public product**: shared with people beyond the classroom

### Gold Standard — 7 Project Based Teaching Practices
Design & plan · Align to standards · Build the culture · Manage activities · **Scaffold student learning** · **Assess student learning** · Engage & coach

### What the successful RCT curricula had in common
1. **Projects are the main course, not dessert.** Content is taught *through* the project and spirals across projects.
2. **Driving question first, which creates a "need to know."** Direct instruction lands when students want it.
3. **Explicit instruction inside the project.** Mini-lessons, worked examples, and skill workshops are scheduled into the project calendar.
4. **Critique and revision cycles.** Multiple drafts, gallery walks, and structured peer-critique protocols (Berger).
5. **A real audience.** Exhibitions, community partners, and products someone actually uses.
6. **Formative checkpoints.** Milestones assessed along the way, not just the final product.
7. **Structured teams of 4–5 with roles.** This matches Principle 8: peer time is structured or skipped.
8. **Sustained teacher support.** Effects grew in teachers' second year (KIA +8 → +10 points).

### The project cycle (as used in the RCT curricula)
```
Entry event + driving question
        ↓
"Need to know" list (students generate)
        ↓
Explicit mini-lessons + worked examples  ←─┐
        ↓                                  │
Inquiry / build (teams of 4–5, roles)      │  repeat per milestone
        ↓                                  │
Checkpoint: critique → revise  ────────────┘
        ↓
Public product to a real audience
        ↓
Reflection (process + content)
```

### Fits in the existing framework as:
The current unit architecture (Engage → Explain → Elaborate/Explore → Evaluate) puts the inquiry project in the last ~40% of the unit, after the knowledge base is built. The research supports keeping that order, with **two upgrades drawn from the RCT curricula**:

1. **Launch the driving question in the Engage phase (Lesson 1),** not at the start of the project. The direct-instruction lessons in Explain then answer questions students already have. This is the "need to know" mechanism, and it uses the same information-gap logic as Three Act Math (Loewenstein).
2. **Keep explicit mini-lessons running inside the project phase.** The Elaborate phase is not "turn them loose." Plan 1–2 short skill workshops and one critique/revision cycle per project milestone.

Working this way keeps the binding rules intact (Explain before Explore, structured peer time, feedback with a response, reflection built in) while getting most of the PBL benefit.

### Emerging (2025–26): AI inside projects
- Uses: research assistant, draft feedback partner, simulated expert or stakeholder interviews.
- **Design beats tools:** requiring students to build something that works *and justify their decisions* makes it harder to hand the thinking off to AI (software-engineering PBL study, 2026).
- Watch-outs: unclear group roles and phone distractions still erode focus. Most AI-PBL studies are small, short, and self-reported.

## What It Doesn't Support

- **Unstructured "go research it" projects.** Minimal guidance underperforms, especially for novices and students with significant learning gaps (see `significant-learning-gaps-research.md`).
- **Replacing knowledge-building with projects.** Every effective model teaches content explicitly *within* or *before* the project.
- **"Dessert projects"** tacked on after the unit test, with no content learning attached. They may be engaging, but they aren't PBL as tested.
- **Assuming authenticity.** Faculty and students disagree sharply on whether projects are real-world (2026 Time for Class survey: 61% of faculty say they assign real-world projects, 26% of students say they did one). Ask students.
- **Very long projects.** Effects drop past ~18 weeks. 9–18 weeks is the sweet spot in the meta-analytic data. Within a unit, that means a project that spans the unit, not one that spans the year.
- **Big groups.** Effects fall off above 5 students per group.
- **Trusting headline meta-analysis numbers.** High heterogeneity, small samples, and the near-zero North American subgroup all argue for leaning on the RCTs.

## Alberta / Canada Angle

- **Inquiry is embedded in Alberta programs of study.** Inquiry has a central place in Alberta's science and social studies programs. Friesen & Scott (2013) wrote a literature review for Alberta Education on inquiry approaches to inform the *Inspiring Education* vision.
- **Galileo Educational Network, *Focus on Inquiry* (2014/15):** a University of Calgary and Galileo study with 140 participants from 36 Alberta schools. It found engagement deepens when schools build a *culture* of inquiry among students, staff, and leaders. It also found significant misunderstandings among educators about what inquiry-based learning actually is. That matches the implementation-fidelity problem in the US literature.
- **Alberta classroom research:** Dinel & Vigfusson (2017) described moving three Alberta charter-school classes from individual research projects toward collaborative, problem-based PBL. MacMath, Sivia & Britton (2017, *Alberta Journal of Educational Research*) studied teacher perceptions of PBL in a Western Canadian secondary school.
- **No Alberta RCT exists** on PBL achievement effects. The causal evidence is American.
- **Design fit:** Alberta's three-column outcome structure (Knowledge / Understanding / Skills & Procedures) maps cleanly onto a project rubric. Knowledge is checked at milestones, Understanding in the justification, and Skills & Procedures in the product.
