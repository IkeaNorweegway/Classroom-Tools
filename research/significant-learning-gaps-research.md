# Significant Learning Gaps, Low Literacy, and Cognitive Disability — Research Summary
*Research session: September 2026.*

---

## How to Read This File

This file supports building materials for students working substantially below grade level — including students multiple grades behind in literacy and/or numeracy, students with diagnosed learning disabilities (LD), and students with identified intellectual/significant cognitive disabilities (ID/SCD). It extends `research/udl-scaffolding-research.md` (access vs. cognitive scaffold distinction, fading, UDL) and connects directly to `_meta-context.md`'s Scaffolding Architecture and equity principles. Read the meta-context for the general design rules; read this file for the evidence and design logic specific to large grade-level gaps.

**The single most important move in this file:** separate "how far behind is this student" into two different questions — *a skill gap* (the student can learn grade-level concepts but lacks a specific prerequisite skill, usually decoding/fluency or number sense) versus *a cognitive gap* (the student has a diagnosed intellectual or significant cognitive disability that changes what outcome is appropriate, not just how it's taught). These require different instructional responses. Conflating them is the single most common design error — and undertreats capable students or overwhelms students who need alternate outcomes.

---

## Part 1 — Terminology and the Two-Track Framework

"Low cognitive" is not a clinical or pedagogical category — it conflates two genuinely distinct populations that need different responses. This file uses precise terms throughout:

| Term | What it means | Typical instructional response |
|---|---|---|
| **Below grade level / significant skill gap** | Achievement is multiple grades behind expected level, but general cognitive ability is in the typical range. Usually caused by an unaddressed specific learning disability (e.g., dyslexia), inconsistent prior schooling, language background, or unremediated early gaps compounding over years (Matthew Effect). | Grade-level concept, intensified/explicit instruction in the deficit skill, access scaffolds to reach the grade-level content, no permanent change to outcomes |
| **Learning disability (LD)** | A specific, diagnosed processing disorder (e.g., dyslexia, dyscalculia) that affects one or more academic domains despite typical cognitive ability | Structured/explicit instruction targeted at the specific deficit (decoding, number sense), access scaffolds elsewhere, grade-level outcomes with accommodations |
| **Intellectual / significant cognitive disability (ID/SCD)** | A diagnosed disability affecting general cognitive functioning and adaptive behaviour, identified through psychoeducational assessment | Systematic instruction (task analysis, explicit prompting), alternate/modified outcomes documented in an IPP, content kept age-appropriate even when skill demand is lowered |

A student who is five grades behind could be any of these three — or a combination. The gap size alone doesn't tell you which track applies; that requires the school's assessment process (see Part 5). What this file provides is the instructional science for each track, and a practical framework for building materials once the track is known — or while it's still being determined.

## Part 2 — Core Finding

**For skill gaps and LD:** the strongest, most consistent finding across five decades of intervention research is that these students need *more explicit, more structured, and more intensively practiced* instruction in the specific deficit skill — not a permanently reduced curriculum. Simplifying the grade-level concept is the wrong lever; intensifying the explicitness and practice volume of instruction in the missing prerequisite skill is the right one. This is the direct instructional analogue of the access-vs-cognitive scaffold distinction already established in `research/udl-scaffolding-research.md`.

**For ID/SCD:** the strongest finding is that systematic instruction — explicit task analysis, response prompting, and immediate feedback — reliably teaches new skills to this population, and that skipping straight to "modified worksheet, lower expectations" without this instructional structure under-serves students who can learn a great deal given the right method.

**The shared principle across both tracks:** the amount of explicitness, repetition, and feedback frequency should scale up sharply as the gap grows. What changes between the two tracks is whether the *learning outcome itself* also changes (ID/SCD: yes, documented in an IPP) or stays at grade level with the *route* to it changed (skill gap/LD: outcome stays, access and instructional intensity change).

## Part 3 — Key Studies and Effect Sizes

### Skill gaps and learning disabilities generally

- **Swanson & Hoskyn (1998)** — *Review of Educational Research.* Meta-analysis of 180 intervention studies with students with LD. Overall mean effect size **M = 0.79** (large). Models combining direct instruction *and* strategy instruction outperformed either alone. Significant predictors of effect size: controlling task difficulty during instruction, small interactive groups, and directed questioning/response opportunities. Evidence quality: **Strong.**

- **Stockard, Wood, Coughlin & Khoury (2018)** — *Review of Educational Research.* Meta-analysis of 328 studies (~4,000 effects) on Direct Instruction (Engelmann-model) curricula, spanning reading, math, language, and spelling. All estimated effects positive and statistically significant, with comparable results for general-education and special-education populations. Evidence quality: **Strong** — largest and most methodologically scrutinized synthesis in this area.

- **Gersten, Chard, Jayanthi, Baker, Morphy & Flojo (2009)** — *Review of Educational Research.* Meta-analysis of 42 math intervention studies for students with LD. Effect sizes ranged **0.21–1.56**; the two components producing the largest, most consistent gains were **explicit instruction** and **teaching students to use heuristics** for problem-solving. Peer-assisted learning within a class did not reach significance. Evidence quality: **Strong.**

### Reading-specific

- **Ehri, Nunes, Stahl & Willows (2001)** — National Reading Panel meta-analysis, *Review of Educational Research.* Systematic phonics instruction vs. unsystematic/no phonics: **d = 0.41** overall; larger when begun early (**d = 0.55**) vs. after Grade 1 (**d = 0.27**). Benefited low- and middle-SES readers, at-risk younger readers, and older students with reading disabilities. **Critical finding for this file: phonics instruction did not significantly help low-achieving readers whose profile included cognitive limitations** — i.e., it is a highly effective LD/skill-gap intervention but not, on its own, an intervention for ID/SCD-driven reading difficulty. Evidence quality: **Strong**, and directly supports the two-track framework in Part 1.

- **IES/What Works Clearinghouse Practice Guide — "Assisting Students Struggling with Reading: RTI in the Primary Grades"** (Gersten et al., 2009, NCEE 2009-4045). Five recommendations: universal screening twice yearly; differentiated Tier 1 instruction matched to assessed skill; daily, intensive small-group Tier 3 instruction for students showing minimal Tier 2 progress; explicit, systematic instruction in the specific deficient component; ongoing progress monitoring. Evidence quality: **Strong** — synthesizes the RTI/MTSS evidence base into actionable tiers.

- **IES/WWC Practice Guide — "Providing Reading Interventions for Students in Grades 4–9"** (2022). Four recommendations for older struggling readers: build word-reading skills where they remain a barrier (decoding does not "age out" as a need); provide explicit vocabulary and academic-language instruction; teach comprehension monitoring/text-structure routines; give students meaningful, supported practice with **challenging, not simplified, text.** Evidence quality: **Strong.**

### Grade-level content access

- **Shanahan (research synthesis, ongoing) and related studies** (e.g., a study finding struggling Grade 3 readers who worked with teachers on texts two to four grade levels above their independent reading level outscored peers who worked only in easier, "matched" texts). Core finding: below-level texts do little to build comprehension; grade-level texts *with scaffolding* (oral re-reading, teacher support, vocabulary pre-teaching) produce stronger comprehension growth. Oral re-reading specifically can functionally raise a student's ability to handle a text by roughly 2–3 grade levels. Evidence quality: **Moderate-to-Strong** — consistent across multiple studies but with fewer large-scale RCTs than the phonics/DI literature.
- **Important boundary condition:** this evidence applies to students who have basic decoding in place. It is not evidence for placing a non-decoding reader in front of grade-level text without parallel foundational-skill instruction — see "What It Doesn't Support" below.

### Math-specific

- **Witzel, Mercer & Miller (2003) and the Concrete-Representational-Abstract (CRA) sequence.** Students move from physical manipulatives → visual/pictorial representations → abstract symbols, ensuring understanding at each stage before advancing. Evidence quality: **Strong**, with a large evidence base specific to students with LD in math across grade levels and topics.
- **Ebner, MacDonald, Grekov & Aspiranti (2025)** — *Learning Disabilities Research & Practice* meta-analysis of 30 single-case-design CRA studies. Overall effect very large (Tau-BC ≈ 0.997). Evidence quality: **Moderate-to-Strong** — single-case design effect sizes are not directly comparable to group-design Cohen's *d*, but the consistency across 30 studies is a strong practical signal.

### Intellectual / significant cognitive disabilities

- **Systematic instruction literature** (reviewed in Drasgow, Wolery, Chezan, Halle & Hajiaghamohseni, and in CEEDAR Center's evidence-based practices synthesis for students with severe disabilities). Documents a strong single-subject-design evidence base for: **task analysis** (breaking a skill into an explicit, ordered sequence of steps), **systematic prompting** (least-to-most prompts, time delay, simultaneous prompting), and **chained-task instruction with a consistent response system**. Evidence quality: **Strong** within single-case-design methodology, which is the dominant and appropriate design for this low-incidence population.
- **High-Leverage Practices for special education** (CEEDAR Center; AFT, 2020–2021 synthesis): systematically designing instruction toward a specific goal, adapting curriculum tasks and materials, teaching cognitive/metacognitive strategies, scaffolded supports, explicit instruction, and explicit instruction in maintaining/generalizing skills across settings. Evidence quality: **Strong** as a synthesis of converging single-case and quasi-experimental literature.

### Intensification framework (applies across tracks)

- **Fuchs, McMaster, Fuchs & Al Otaiba (2014); National Center on Intensive Intervention (NCII).** Data-Based Individualization (DBI): a systematic process of frequent progress monitoring, and when a student is not responding, deliberately intensifying one dimension of instruction at a time (dosage, group size, explicitness, or skill target) rather than guessing. This is the standard framework for what Tier 3/intensive intervention should look like, and it applies whether the student's ultimate track is LD or ID/SCD. Evidence quality: **Strong** as a process framework; effectiveness depends on fidelity of implementation, which is harder to guarantee than the underlying component evidence.

## Part 4 — What It Looks Like in Practice: Building Assignments for a Student Several Grades Behind

This is the operational framework for the specific problem: a student is markedly behind grade placement (illustratively, ~5 grades), and the task is to build an Alberta-curriculum-aligned assignment they can genuinely access and grow from.

### Step 1 — Anchor the topic, separate the load

Keep the **grade-level Alberta Program of Studies topic and concept** as the anchor whenever the student is on Track A/B (skill gap or LD) rather than a documented Modified program. Do not substitute an easier grade's *topic*. Instead, separate two things that are usually bundled together in a standard assignment:
- **The concept being learned** (stays at grade level — e.g., a Grade 8 student studying cell structure studies actual Grade 8 cell structure content)
- **The reading/writing load required to access and demonstrate it** (adjusts heavily — shorter sentences, read-aloud/audio access, response by drawing/labelling/oral explanation instead of paragraph writing, visuals carrying more of the explanatory weight)

This is the access-vs-cognitive scaffold distinction from `research/udl-scaffolding-research.md` applied at the whole-assignment level, and it is what keeps the student exposed to grade-level ideas and vocabulary rather than falling further behind through the Matthew Effect (Stanovich, 1986 — already established in `_meta-context.md`).

### Step 2 — Run a genuine foundational-skills track in parallel, don't fold it into content assignments

A student who is not yet decoding, or lacks basic number sense, needs **explicit, systematic, daily instruction in that specific skill** — this is a separate instructional stream from the content-area assignment, not something a single worksheet can accomplish. For literacy: structured/systematic phonics instruction, controlled-vocabulary decodable text, explicit letter-sound and morphology instruction. For math: explicit instruction plus the CRA sequence (concrete manipulatives → visual representation → abstract symbol), moving to the next stage only once the current one is secure.

**Do not wait for this track to finish before giving content-area access.** Run both simultaneously: foundational skills work builds the underlying capacity; content-area assignments (built per Step 1) keep the student engaged with grade-level ideas using access scaffolds. Neither substitutes for the other.

### Step 3 — Calibrate explicitness and practice volume to the size of the gap

The evidence (Swanson & Hoskyn's predictors of effect size; Gersten et al.'s explicit-instruction finding; the DBI intensification model) converges on the same levers, scaling with gap size:
- More worked examples before independent practice, more steps shown explicitly, less left implicit
- Smaller chunks per task (task analysis — break a multi-step assignment into an explicit, ordered sequence rather than one open-ended prompt)
- More response opportunities with immediate, specific feedback per unit of time (not more time spent passively reading/listening)
- Higher repetition/practice volume on the specific deficit skill than a typical student would need
- More frequent, shorter progress checks (weekly or twice-weekly skill probes) rather than one end-of-unit assessment — this is what makes it possible to intensify the right dimension when something isn't working, per the DBI framework

### Step 4 — For ID/SCD: use systematic instruction and keep content age-appropriate

When the track is a documented intellectual/significant cognitive disability (not just a skill gap), the outcome itself is different — the IPP specifies alternate/modified outcomes. Within that:
- Use **task analysis**: break the target skill into its explicit component steps, in order, and teach each with a consistent prompting hierarchy (model → guided practice with prompts → independent, fading prompts as the student succeeds — the same fading logic as `research/udl-scaffolding-research.md`, applied to a prompt hierarchy instead of a worked-example hierarchy)
- Use **systematic prompting** (least-to-most, or consistent time delay) rather than inconsistent, ad hoc help — the evidence favours a planned, predictable prompting system over improvised assistance
- **Keep materials age-appropriate even when skill demand is much lower.** A Grade 8 student on a modified program should not receive early-elementary-themed materials; use grade-appropriate topics, images, and contexts with the skill demand adjusted (e.g., a Grade 8 modified-program science task can use the same ecosystems theme the class is studying, with a matching task that asks the student to sort images into categories rather than write an explanatory paragraph)

### Step 5 — Build the assignment, don't retrofit one

Concretely, when building a workbook, notes package, or quiz item for this student:
1. Start from the grade-level LO/topic (per `worksheets/workbooks-context.md` or the relevant artifact context)
2. Decide the track (skill gap/LD vs. ID/SCD) — this determines whether the outcome itself changes
3. Reduce reading/writing load via access scaffolds (Step 1), not by reducing the concept
4. Add explicit structure: task analysis for multi-step tasks, worked examples with self-explanation prompts (already the workbook default per `_meta-context.md`), CRA sequence for math
5. Build in short, frequent response/feedback opportunities rather than one long independent block
6. Note in the teacher guide what the specific deficit skill is and what the parallel foundational-skills instruction should target — the content assignment is not expected to fix the underlying skill gap on its own

## Part 5 — What This Doesn't Support

- **Phonics/structured literacy is not a universal fix.** Ehri et al. (2001) found it did not significantly help low-achieving readers whose profile included cognitive limitations. Before assuming "more phonics" is the answer for a student far behind in reading, the track (Part 1) needs to be established — otherwise time may be spent on an intervention mismatched to the actual cause.
- **"Grade-level text with scaffolding" is not a license to skip foundational skills.** The Shanahan-synthesis evidence for grade-level text applies to students with basic decoding already in place. A student who cannot decode needs an explicit, systematic decoding track running in parallel — grade-level text scaffolding is not a substitute for that.
- **Direct Instruction's evidence is strong at the program level, not guaranteed at the worksheet level.** Stockard et al.'s effects come from full DI curricula delivered with high fidelity by trained instructors — often scripted, sequenced, and delivered in small groups. A single explicit-instruction-flavoured assignment borrows the principle, not the guaranteed effect size.
- **CRA's strongest evidence is single-case design**, which shows a skill was reliably learned by the specific students studied, not the same thing as a large-sample average effect size. Treat the Tau-BC figure as strong practical support, not as directly comparable to a Cohen's *d* from a randomized trial.
- **None of this evidence identifies where the line is between Adapted and Modified programming for a specific student.** That is a clinical determination requiring psychoeducational assessment, not something a materials-design framework can or should decide unilaterally (see Part 6).
- **This is not a substitute for RTI/MTSS infrastructure.** Tier 3 intensive intervention requires dedicated time, small group size, and trained delivery that a single differentiated assignment embedded in a regular class cannot replicate. Materials built from this file support what happens in the regular classroom and content-area assignments; they don't replace a resourced intervention block for a student who needs one.

## Part 6 — Alberta / Canada Angle

**Individualized Program Plans (IPPs):** Alberta Education's *Standards for Special Education* (amended 2004) and the *Individualized Program Planning* guide (2006) govern how schools document programming for students with identified needs. A student working substantially below grade level should have (or should trigger a referral toward) an IPP developed by the school team, not a unilateral classroom decision.

**Accommodated vs. Modified programming:** Alberta distinguishes between students who work toward the **same grade-level Program of Studies outcomes** with accommodations/strategies (the Track A/LD case in Part 1 — supports the design in Part 4, Steps 1–3), and students on a **Modified program** working toward alternate outcomes not based on the grade-level Program of Studies (the ID/SCD case — Part 4, Step 4). Building grade-level-anchored materials with heavy access scaffolding is the correct approach for the first group; building toward IPP-specified alternate outcomes is correct for the second. Materials should not default to "make it easier" without first knowing which of these applies.

**Template A threshold:** Alberta's IPP guidance defines Template A as designed in part for students working two to three years below grade-level expectations in Language Arts. A student five grades behind is well past this threshold and very likely warrants formal IPP programming (if not already in place) rather than ad hoc classroom differentiation alone — this is a signal to loop in the school's Learning Support/Resource team, not just intensify materials independently.

**Inclusive education context:** Alberta's inclusive education policy expects students to be educated in general-education settings to the greatest extent appropriate. This is consistent with the framework here — grade-level topic access with heavy scaffolding, rather than routine removal to separate, permanently simplified material — but it also means intensive Tier 3 instruction (which genuinely needs small-group, high-dosage delivery) has to be resourced deliberately within an inclusive model, not assumed to happen inside whole-class differentiation alone.

**Practical implication for this workspace:** when a build request specifies a student significantly below grade level, the clarifying questions (per the Build Protocol) should establish which track applies and whether an IPP already specifies modified outcomes — this changes whether the material anchors to the grade-level LO (Track A/LD) or to IPP-specified alternate outcomes (Track ID/SCD) before any content is written.
