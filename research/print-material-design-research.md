# Print Material Design for Learning
*Research summary — April 2026*

---

## Why this matters

The existing research base in this workspace covers *what* to put in workbooks and notes packages — interleaving, retrieval, scaffolding, metacognition. This file covers a distinct question: *how the physical and visual design of the page affects whether students engage deeply or superficially*. Four evidence areas have direct design implications: guided notes format, generative processing, dual coding, and answer space signaling.

---

## Core finding

**The design of a page is an instructional decision, not a formatting one.** Where a label appears relative to a diagram, how much space is provided for a response, and whether a blank requires generation or recognition — each of these changes what cognitive work the student does. Materials that look identical in terms of content can produce significantly different learning outcomes depending on these design choices.

---

## Key studies and findings

### 1. Guided notes — fill-in format vs. full notes vs. no structure

**What it is:** Guided (or skeleton) notes provide the structural framework of a lesson with blanks at key knowledge points. Students complete them during instruction rather than constructing notes from scratch or copying verbatim.

**Evidence:** A systematic review (ERIC, EJ1264517) across K–12 and postsecondary settings finds guided notes substantially increase achievement compared to unguided note-taking. Effects are strongest for students with learning disabilities and ELL students, but extend to the general population. A meta-synthesis from Ohio State finds students retain 3–8 more words of information per minute of instruction with guided notes compared to conventional notes.

**Mechanism:** Guided notes reduce extraneous cognitive load (students are not managing structure and content simultaneously) while preserving generative processing (blanks require active production). Full verbatim notes overload working memory; no structure leaves novice learners without a framework to sort incoming information.

**Limitation:** Effects are weaker with postsecondary students who have developed their own note-taking strategies. At K–12 level, the effect is consistently positive. Evidence in online/asynchronous contexts is thin.

**Design implication:** The fill-in blank format in notes packages is directly supported. The key constraint — that blanks should require genuine retrieval, not letter-matching — is what preserves the generative advantage over passive copying.

---

### 2. Generative processing — why blanks have to require thought

**What it is:** Generative learning theory (Wittrock, 1974, 1990) holds that students learn by constructing meaning — making connections, paraphrasing, drawing inferences — not by receiving it. Passive re-reading and verbatim copying produce shallow encoding. Active generation produces durable encoding.

**Evidence:** Fiorella & Mayer (2015) meta-analysis of generative learning strategies finds consistent advantages for summarising, self-explaining, drawing, and mapping over passive study across hundreds of studies. The 2021 meta-analysis of note-taking methods (Luo et al., *Contemporary Educational Psychology*) finds students who elaborate in notes outperform verbatim copiers specifically on transfer questions — the gap is largest for questions requiring application, not just recall. A 2025 Frontiers in Psychology study replicates this with the additional finding that motivation mediates the effect: students who find blanks purposeful generate more, and learn more.

**Important caveat:** Generative processing has a capacity cost. During fast-paced instruction, requiring too much generation at once can overwhelm working memory and cause students to fall behind. This is why the fill-in format — pre-written transitions, blanks only at key points — outperforms pure generation tasks during instruction. The blank must be cognitively demanding but not working-memory overloading.

**Design implication:** The notes-package rule — "never blank everything, a page of blanks produces completion behaviour not thinking behaviour" — is directly supported by this research. Blank density should be high enough to demand engagement, low enough to prevent overload. Concrete target: no more than 30–40% of the running text should be blanked. Supporting text and transitions should be pre-written.

### 3. Dual coding and the multimedia principle

**What it is:** Paivio's dual coding theory (1971) proposes that the verbal and visual cognitive systems are separate channels, each with independent storage. Information encoded in both channels is more robustly retained than information encoded in one. Mayer's cognitive theory of multimedia learning (2001) operationalises this for instructional design: words + pictures > words alone.

**Evidence:** Mayer & Moreno (1998) randomised experiment: students receiving integrated text-diagram instruction significantly outperformed text-only students on both retention and transfer measures. Sadoski & Paivio (2001) review across dozens of studies: dual-coded information is recalled more reliably and forgotten more slowly. Effect is most reliable when images are *explanatory* — showing relationships, processes, before/after states — rather than decorative.

**The contiguity principle** (Mayer, 2001): Text should be physically placed *near* the image it describes. When an explanation is separated from its diagram — in a caption below, in a legend on the side — the learner must hold both in working memory simultaneously to integrate them, increasing extraneous load. Placing labels directly on the diagram eliminates this cost.

**The coherence principle** (Mayer, 2001): Irrelevant visuals *reduce* learning. Decorative images, thematic graphics, and stock illustrations that have no explanatory function compete for attention and consume channel capacity without contributing to encoding. The "seductive details" effect: an interesting but irrelevant image near a key diagram draws attention away from the learning target.

**Design implications for workbooks and notes:**
- Every visual element must be explanatory, not decorative. If a diagram does not ask students to do something with it (label, complete, compare) or show a relationship central to the content, it should not be there.
- Labels and annotations belong on the diagram, not in a separate legend or caption.
- White space around diagrams is not wasted space — it is visual breathing room that reduces attention competition.

### 4. Split-attention effect

**What it is:** Chandler & Sweller (1992) identified that when two sources of information must be mentally integrated to be understood, but are physically separated on the page, learners must split attention between them. This creates extraneous cognitive load — working memory is consumed by the physical search-and-match task rather than the conceptual one.

**Evidence:** Tarmizi & Sweller (1988) original geometry study: students given worked examples where the diagram and algebraic steps were integrated performed significantly better than students given the same content in separated format — despite identical information. Chandler & Sweller (1992) replicated and extended this across science and technical materials. Effect size for integration interventions is consistently positive (d = 0.5–0.9 in controlled studies).

**The fix:** Physical integration. Labels placed directly on the diagram. Steps numbered and arrowed to the part of the diagram they describe. Explanatory text embedded within or immediately adjacent to the visual. This is a design constraint, not just a preference.

**Boundary condition:** The split-attention effect disappears or reverses when the two sources *can* be understood independently (redundancy effect). If a diagram is fully self-explanatory, adding integrated text creates load rather than reducing it. The design rule: integrate when integration is necessary for understanding; omit text when the diagram speaks for itself.

**Design implication for diagrams in workbooks:** Particle arrangement diagrams, phase change sequences, food web networks, and force diagrams should all have labels integrated directly onto the image. A legend in the margin requires the student to look back and forth — exactly the split-attention condition. This applies whether the diagram is in a notes package (completed during instruction) or a workbook (used during retrieval).

### 5. Answer space size as a signal

**What it is:** The physical size of the response space in a workbook or assessment communicates the expected depth of response. Students use space size as a cue for how much to write.

**Evidence:** Israel (2010) study across 2,200 survey responses: larger answer spaces produce longer responses with more themes elaborated — space size had no effect on *whether* a student answered, but significantly affected the *depth* of the answer. Christian & Dillman (2004) and multiple replication studies confirm the same pattern in educational contexts: students given more lines write more, with greater elaboration and fewer single-word answers.

**Mechanism:** The space is a social signal — it tells the student what answer depth is acceptable and expected. A 2-line space after a question signals that a short answer is adequate. A 6-line space signals that explanation is expected. Students calibrate accordingly, often without consciously noticing.

**Design implication:** Response space size in workbooks should be calibrated to the question type:
- Recall questions (Core, single concept): 2–3 lines
- Explanation/justification questions (Core with reasoning): 4–5 lines
- Extended questions: 6–8 lines minimum
- Error analysis and multi-concept questions: 8+ lines or a full box

The space IS part of the difficulty signal. Extended (★★) questions should look harder than Core (★) questions before a student reads a single word — the visual weight of the response space is the first cue. Shrinking the space to fit more questions on a page undermines this signal and degrades response quality.

## What this research does not support

- **Decorative formatting as engagement:** Colourful borders, themed graphics, and elaborate visual design have no evidence of improving learning and may reduce it (coherence principle). Visual design in educational materials should be functional, not aesthetic.
- **More space is always better:** Extremely large spaces produce longer responses but not necessarily better ones. The signal is "how much is expected," not "how much space is available." Match space size to cognitive demand, not to page layout preferences.
- **Format effects transfer across all learners:** Effects are most consistent for novice learners and students with lower prior knowledge. Students with strong domain knowledge are less affected by format — their schema does the organisational work that the format provides for novices.

---

## What it looks like in practice

| Design decision | What the research supports |
|---|---|
| Fill-in blank format | Preserves generative processing while reducing extraneous load; outperforms full generation during instruction |
| Blank density | 30–40% of running text; never blank transitions or supporting text |
| Diagram labels | Integrated directly onto the diagram, not in a separate legend |
| Decorative images | Remove — coherence principle; only explanatory visuals |
| Response space for Core questions | 2–4 lines (signals brief, direct answer expected) |
| Response space for Extended questions | 6–8+ lines (signals explanation and justification expected) |
| Text near diagrams | Adjacent to the part of the diagram it describes (contiguity) |
| White space | Preserve around diagrams; not wasted — reduces attention competition |
