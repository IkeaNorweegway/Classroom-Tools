# When to Teach New Vocabulary: Experience First or Words First? — Research Summary
*Research session: October 2026. Question: should students meet a unit's words in context and learn them explicitly afterwards, or be taught them explicitly before they have a working knowledge of the topic? Sources: meta-analyses of incidental word learning, context-clue instruction, vocabulary-and-comprehension, problem-solving-before-instruction and multimedia pre-training; classroom trials in Grade 5 science and kindergarten read-alouds. Tags: [checked] = figure confirmed against the abstract or a review of the paper in this session; [secondary] = from a later review or summary; [memory] = from prior knowledge, not re-checked this session.*

Builds on §6 of `research/literacy-reading-writing-research.md` (Stahl & Fairbanks, Elleman et al., Beck's tiers). This file is about **timing and sequence**, which that section does not cover.

---

## Topic and Why It Matters

Every notes package, slide deck and workbook in this workspace has to decide where the key terms first appear: in a vocab box at the top, or after the students have done something. The workspace rule (`_pedagogy-reference.md`, "Vocabulary as Knowledge") already says "introduce vocabulary in the context of its use, not in isolation before instruction begins." This file checks that rule against the evidence and sharpens it.

The question is usually framed as a choice between two options. The evidence rejects both pure forms:
- **Context only** (students work out meanings from use, nobody teaches them): too slow and unreliable, and weakest for the students who most need the words.
- **Definitions first, cold** (a list of terms and definitions before students know what the ideas are): students memorise labels with nothing attached, and the definition doesn't stick.

What works is a sequence, and the right sequence depends on **what kind of word it is.**

---

## Core Finding

**For a word that names a new idea, give students the experience of the idea first (in everyday language), then name it and teach it explicitly in the same lesson, then make them use it many times.** The naming has to be explicit and has to come soon. "Context first" only works when the context is a designed experience followed by teaching, not a hope that students will infer the meaning.

Two exceptions mean teaching some words up front:
1. **Names of parts students need to follow the explanation** (hypotenuse, stamen, numerator). Teach these briefly before the main explanation, so working memory isn't spent on new labels during it.
2. **New words for ideas students already have** (Tier 2: reluctant, justify, consequence). There is no concept to build first, so teach them directly at, or just before, the point of reading.

## Key Sources

### 1. Context alone is slow: Swanborn & de Glopper (1999)
*Review of Educational Research, 69(3), 261–285.* [checked]
Meta-analysis of 20 experiments on incidental word learning during normal reading. Students learned **about 15% of the unknown words** they met. The chance went up with grade level and reading ability. So context does teach words, but one encounter gives roughly a 1-in-7 chance, and weaker readers get less.

A later L2 meta-analysis (Webb, Uchihara & Yanagisawa) found similar rates: about 15–17% from reading, 13–15% from listening. [secondary]

### 2. Natural contexts are often unhelpful: Beck, McKeown & McCaslin (1983)
*"Vocabulary development: All contexts are not created equal," Elementary School Journal, 83, 177–181.* [checked: citation and main argument]
Most contexts in ordinary text don't narrow a word's meaning much, and some point students to the wrong meaning. Contexts used to **teach** a word should be written to be highly constraining. Implication: a reading passage is a poor place to *meet* a key term for the first time unless the passage was written to explain it.

### 3. Concept first, then the scientific term: Brown & Ryoo (2008)
*"Teaching science as a language: A 'content-first' approach to science teaching," Journal of Research in Science Teaching, 45(5), 529–553.* [checked, via WWC quick review]
49 Grade 5 students, randomly assigned. Both groups had web-based lessons on photosynthesis. The **content-first** group learned the ideas in everyday language and then moved to the scientific terms. The control group started with definitions of the scientific terms and then did similar activities using only scientific language. On a post-test **in scientific language**, the content-first group scored higher, both on concepts and on using the terms. Small sample and one topic, but the closest direct test of this question at this grade level.

### 4. Experience first prepares students to learn from telling: Schwartz & Bransford (1998)
*"A time for telling," Cognition and Instruction, 16(4), 475–522.* [checked: abstract]
University students who first analysed **contrasting cases** (sets of examples that differ in one key feature) learned more from a later lecture than students who read about the same ideas first. The experience doesn't replace the explanation. It makes students notice the distinctions the explanation names. The "time for telling" is after students have seen the differences that the words describe.

### 5. Problem-solving before instruction: Sinha & Kapur (2021)
*"When problem solving followed by instruction works: Evidence for productive failure," Review of Educational Research, 91(5), 761–798.* [checked: abstract figures]
53 studies, 166 comparisons. Problem-solving first, then instruction, beat instruction first, then problem-solving on conceptual knowledge: **g = 0.36** overall, **0.37–0.58** when the problem-solving phase was well designed. Important moderator: in this meta-analysis, **younger students (Grades 2–5) tended to do better with instruction first** [secondary, from abstract summary; check the paper before leaning on it]. For Grades 5–6, keep the exploration short and structured.

### 6. Pre-teach the names of parts: Mayer's pre-training principle
*Mayer, Mathias & Wetzell (2002), Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 8, 147–154; summarised in Mayer, Multimedia Learning, "Pre-training principle" chapter.* [checked: principle; median effect size d ≈ 0.85 across pre-training studies as reported in that chapter]
People learn more deeply from an explanation when they **already know the names and characteristics of its main parts.** Pre-training moves some of the work of learning new labels to before the explanation, so the explanation itself can be about how the parts work together. The benefit is mainly for students with low prior knowledge [memory]. Note what is pre-taught: **names and what each part looks like or does**, not the full concept.

### 7. Extended explicit teaching beats a quick mention: Coyne et al. (2007, 2009)
*Coyne, McCoach & Kapp (2007), Learning Disability Quarterly, 30, 74–88; Coyne, McCoach, Loftus, Zipoli & Kapp (2009), Elementary School Journal.* [checked: 2009 abstract]
Kindergarten read-alouds. **Embedded** instruction (a quick definition in passing, about a minute per word) gave partial knowledge. **Extended** instruction (definitions, examples, discussion, use in new contexts) gave fuller knowledge. Both beat exposure alone. A replication with Grade 2 English learners found the same order. [secondary]

### 8. Explaining words during repeated reading: Biemiller & Boote (2006)
*Journal of Educational Psychology, 98(1), 44–62.* [checked: abstract figures]
Repeated reading of a story alone: about **12%** gain in word meanings. Adding short word explanations: **22%**. Adding review and practice in new sentences (Study 2): **41%**. In a school with about 50% English learners. Context helps; explicit explanation doubles it; review and reuse nearly doubles it again.

### 9. Definitions alone don't work
- **Stahl & Fairbanks (1986)** [memory]: methods giving **both** definitional and contextual information beat definition-only methods.
- **Miller & Gildea (1987)**, *Scientific American, 257(3)* [memory for the specific examples]: students given dictionary definitions wrote sentences like "Me and my parents *correlate*, because without them I wouldn't be here." They grab one part of the definition and attach it to the wrong idea.
- **Wright & Cervetti (2017)**, *Reading Research Quarterly, 52(2), 203–226* [checked]: almost any teaching of specific words beats none, but the gain is mostly for the text containing those words. **Active processing** of words beats definition or dictionary methods. No evidence that teaching one or two word-learning strategies raises general comprehension.

### 10. Integrated science and literacy: Seeds of Science / Roots of Reading
*Cervetti, Barber, Dorph, Pearson & Goldschmidt (2012), Journal of Research in Science Teaching; Barber & Pearson (2014) review.* [secondary]
Grades 2–5 units built on a "do–talk–read–write" cycle: students investigate, talk about it, read about it, write about it, with key terms used across all four. Compared with matched units, students gained on science understanding and on **science vocabulary (effects about 0.34–0.41)**. The words were met in an experience and taught explicitly, not one or the other.

### Supporting
- **Fukkink & de Glopper (1998)**, *Review of Educational Research, 68(4), 450–469* [checked]: teaching students to work out word meanings from context has a medium effect (**0.43**), with clue instruction best. This is a strategy for words students meet on their own, not a way to teach a unit's key terms.
- **McKeown, Beck, Omanson & Pople (1985)** [memory]: rich instruction with many encounters (around 12) was needed for words to improve comprehension; a few encounters gave definitional knowledge only.
- **Pretesting / errorful generation** (Richland, Kornell & Kao 2009; Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009) [memory]: trying to answer before being taught, even wrongly, improves later learning when correct answers follow. Supports a "what do you think this is?" moment before the explanation.

## What It Looks Like in Practice

### Sort the unit's words into four kinds first

| Kind of word | Examples | When and how |
|---|---|---|
| **A. New idea, new word** (most Tier 3 terms) | photosynthesis, slope, ecosystem, refraction, inertia | **Experience → name → explain → use.** A short investigation, demo, contrasting cases or sim, described in everyday words. Then the term, with a student-friendly definition, an example and a non-example, **in the same lesson.** |
| **B. Part names needed to follow the explanation** | hypotenuse, numerator, stamen, fulcrum | **Brief pre-teach** just before the explanation: labelled picture + one line each. Not the full concept. Then use them in the explanation. |
| **C. New word, known idea** (Tier 2) | reluctant, justify, consequence, evidence, predict | **Teach explicitly** at or just before the reading or task where they appear: friendly definition, examples, non-example, students produce the word. Context won't do it fast enough. |
| **D. Everyday words with a technical meaning** | force, work, energy, matter, average, similar, theory | The riskiest: students think they already know them. **Bring out the everyday meaning first, then contrast it explicitly with the technical one** (refutation). Teaching the term cold lets students map it onto the everyday meaning. |

### A lesson sequence for kind A words
1. **Prompt before the word** (1–2 min): "What do you think plants need to make food?" Students commit to an answer.
2. **Experience** (5–15 min for Grades 5–6; keep it structured): an investigation, a demo, two or three contrasting cases, a sim run. Students describe what they see **in their own words**.
3. **Name it** (the "time for telling"): "What you just described has a name: *photosynthesis*." Link the term to the students' own words on the board.
4. **Explain explicitly**: student-friendly definition, example, non-example, how it connects to the experience.
5. **Use it right away**: students explain the experience again using the term (talk, then write).
6. **Spaced use**: the term returns in retrieval warm-ups, readings and tasks over following weeks. Aim for many encounters, not one.

### What this means for our materials
- **Notes packages and slides:** don't open a section with a vocab box of kind A terms. Put the experience or hook first, then the term box. A **small labelled diagram of part names (kind B)** before the explanation is fine and supported.
- **Workbooks:** vocabulary questions about kind A terms belong after the lesson that names them, then interleaved later. Not as a front-loaded matching exercise.
- **Readings (ELA and content):** pre-teach the few Tier 2 (kind C) words students need just before reading, as Grade 5 ELA already does. Don't rely on the passage to teach them.
- **Kind D words** get a refutation box ("In everyday life, *work* means... In science, *work* means...") and a flag in the teacher guide.
- **Never** a definitions-first list copied at the start of a unit. This is the control condition that lost in Brown & Ryoo.

### Proposed refinement of the workspace rule
Current: *"Introduce vocabulary in the context of its use, not in isolation before instruction begins."*
Suggested: *"Name a new idea after students have experienced it, and teach the term explicitly in the same lesson. Exceptions: pre-teach part names needed to follow the explanation, and teach Tier 2 words directly before the reading that uses them. Everyday words with a technical meaning get an explicit everyday-vs-technical contrast."*

## What It Doesn't Support

- **Not "let them figure it out."** Every positive "context first" result includes explicit teaching afterwards. Exploration without the naming and explaining step is the minimal-guidance approach that loses to explicit teaching (see `research/simulations-research.md`, Alfieri et al.).
- **Not long open-ended exploration for younger students.** The Sinha & Kapur moderator suggests Grades 2–5 may do better with instruction first. For Grades 5–6, the experience should be short and focused on the one distinction the word names.
- **Brown & Ryoo is one small study** (n = 49, one topic, immediate post-test). It fits the wider pattern but isn't strong evidence alone.
- **Pre-training evidence comes mostly from lab studies** with university students and short multimedia lessons. The idea transfers well, but the d ≈ 0.85 shouldn't be expected in a classroom.
- **Teaching words doesn't quickly raise general reading comprehension** (Elleman et al., Wright & Cervetti). It helps with the texts and topics where the words are used.
- **No study settles the best gap** between experience and naming. The safe reading of the evidence is "same lesson."

## Alberta / Canada Angle

- **Alberta science programs (Science 7, new K–6 Science) are inquiry-based** in their skills outcomes. The experience-then-name sequence fits those outcomes without giving up explicit teaching of terms.
- **English learners** are a large share of many Alberta classes. Biemiller & Boote's 41% result was in a school with about half English learners. The WWC practice guide for English learners (Baker et al. 2014; see `research/literacy-reading-writing-research.md`) recommends intensive academic vocabulary teaching over several days. For EAL students, a picture plus home-language link at the naming step is an access scaffold, not a lowering of demand.
- **Alberta ELAL K–6 (2023)** has Vocabulary as an organizing idea. Kind C (Tier 2) teaching before reading fits it directly.
