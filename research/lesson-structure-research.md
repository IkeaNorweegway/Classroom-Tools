# Lesson Structure for Lasting Learning and Engagement
*Research summary — April 2026*

---

## Why this matters

A teacher who knows the right content but structures it poorly will produce worse outcomes than a teacher who knows less but designs the class hour deliberately. The research on lesson structure is among the most applied in cognitive science — it maps directly onto what happens in the first and last five minutes, how practice is sequenced, and how the three-class-per-week schedule is used or wasted.

This file addresses the specific context of **1-hour classes, 3 times per week.**

---

## Core finding

The single most evidence-supported structural principle is this: **retrieval beats re-exposure.** Memory is not strengthened by seeing material again — it is strengthened by being required to produce material from memory under mild difficulty. Every structural decision should flow from this.

---

## Key studies and findings

### Roediger & Butler (2011) — The testing effect in classrooms
Roediger and colleagues demonstrated that students who received low-stakes retrieval quizzes at the start of class outperformed unquizzed students on both immediate and delayed assessments, on the same curriculum. Effect size: g ≈ 0.50 across classroom studies.

**Implication:** The retrieval warm-up at the start of class is not review — it is a learning event. Opening with questions about prior material is more effective than opening with re-teaching it.

**Source:** Roediger, H.L., Agarwal, P.K., McDaniel, M.A. & McDermott, K.B. (2011). Test-enhanced learning in the classroom. *Journal of Educational Psychology, 103*(2), 399–414.

---

### Sweller (1988) — Cognitive load theory and worked examples
Sweller's cognitive load theory established that working memory is severely limited (4 ± 1 chunks, 10–20 seconds without rehearsal). For novice learners, problem-solving consumes working memory that should be going toward building schemas. Worked examples dramatically outperform problem-solving as an introduction to new material.

**The fading effect:** As competence grows, worked examples should be progressively withdrawn. Fully guided examples → partially completed examples → independent problems. Giving experts worked examples actually slows them down (the "expertise reversal effect").

**Implication:** Direct instruction with 2–3 fully worked examples should precede any independent practice. The I do → We do → You do sequence is not tradition — it is cognitive architecture.

**Source:** Sweller, J. (1988). Cognitive load during problem solving. *Cognitive Science, 12*(2), 257–285.

---

### Rohrer & Taylor (2007) — Interleaving versus blocked practice
Students who practiced mixed problem types (interleaved) across sessions outperformed students who completed all of one type before moving to the next (blocked), even though interleaved students scored lower during practice. On delayed tests, the advantage was large and consistent: effect sizes around d = 0.40.

**The paradox:** Blocked practice produces better in-session performance. Interleaved practice produces better retention and transfer. Students consistently prefer blocked practice and rate interleaved practice as harder. The difficulty is the mechanism.

**Implication:** Practice sets should mix current content with problems from previous weeks. Do not assign all fraction problems together, then all ratio problems. Mix them.

**Source:** Rohrer, D. & Taylor, K. (2007). The shuffling of mathematics problems improves learning. *Instructional Science, 35*(6), 481–498.

---

### Bjork (1994) — Desirable difficulties and spacing
Bjork's framework establishes that learning conditions that feel productive (smooth, fast, high-performance) often produce shallow encoding. Conditions that feel slow and difficult often produce durable, transferable learning.

**Spacing** is the most robustly supported desirable difficulty: distributing practice across sessions outperforms equivalent time massed in a single session. The 3x/week schedule already creates this structure — but only if the same concepts appear in multiple sessions, not if each class covers new material and moves on.

**Implication:** Plan so that every key concept from a unit appears in *at least* three separate classes — introduced in one, retrieved and practised in a second, applied or interleaved in a third. Spacing is built into 3x/week only if you use it deliberately.

**Source:** Bjork, R.A. (1994). Memory and metamemory considerations in the training of human beings. In J. Metcalfe & A. Shimamura (Eds.), *Metacognition.* MIT Press.

---

### Black & Wiliam (1998) — Formative assessment and feedback loops
Black and Wiliam's synthesis of 250+ studies found that formative assessment — using evidence of student understanding to adjust instruction in real time — produced effect sizes of d = 0.40–0.70 depending on implementation quality. One of the highest-leverage interventions in education.

**The exit ticket finding:** Short written checks at the end of class give teachers diagnostic information about what actually landed and what did not. The value is only realised if the teacher uses the data to decide what to address next class.

**Implication:** The last 5–8 minutes of class should be an independent check — not a group discussion — so the teacher has individual-level evidence, not a collective impression.

**Source:** Black, P. & Wiliam, D. (1998). Assessment and classroom learning. *Assessment in Education, 5*(1), 7–74.

---

### Nuthall (2007) — The three encounters rule
Nuthall's 40-year classroom research programme found that students need at least **three complete encounters with new information, spaced across time,** for it to transfer to long-term memory. One rich lesson is almost never sufficient. An encounter that contains a misconception — from a peer, from incomplete explanation — may do more harm than good.

**Implication:** The 3x/week structure is an asset only if it is used to *revisit* rather than always *advance.* Moving on every class prevents the three encounters from occurring.

**Source:** Nuthall, G. (2007). *The Hidden Lives of Learners.* NZCER Press.

## Recommended 1-hour structure

| Phase | Time | What happens | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Retrieval warm-up** | 5–8 min | 3–5 short questions on prior content — no notes, low stakes | Testing effect; spaced retrieval; attention signal |
| **Direct instruction** | 15–20 min | New content with 2–3 fully worked examples; I do → We do | Cognitive load theory; schema-building before problem-solving |
| **Interleaved practice** | 20–25 min | Problems mixing today's content with prior weeks | Interleaving effect; desirable difficulty; teacher can circulate and give in-class feedback |
| **Exit ticket** | 5–8 min | 2–3 independent questions, collected | Formative data; one more retrieval event; informs next class |

## Using the 3x/week schedule deliberately

The gaps between classes are not dead time — they are the spacing mechanism. Use them:

| Day | Retrieval warm-up covers |
|---|---|
| Monday | Friday's content + something from 2 weeks prior |
| Wednesday | Monday's content |
| Friday | Wednesday's content + something from 2 weeks prior |

This creates spaced retrieval without additional planning. The two-week lookback addresses material before it falls below the retrieval threshold.

## On engagement specifically

The evidence on engagement is thinner than the evidence on memory, but the two are linked through **desirable difficulty** (Bjork) and **self-determination theory** (Deci & Ryan, 1985):

- Tasks that are too easy produce boredom and shallow encoding
- Tasks that are too hard produce avoidance and cognitive overload
- Tasks in the learner's zone of proximal development (Vygotsky) produce both engagement and encoding
- Frequent low-stakes retrieval gives students visible evidence of their own progress, which predicts intrinsic motivation

**Cold calling:** Research on distributed attention (and Lemov's classroom application) suggests that students pay attention more consistently when they expect to be called on — and that this is not threatening when wrong answers are handled with curiosity rather than judgment.

**Source:** Deci, E.L. & Ryan, R.M. (1985). *Intrinsic Motivation and Self-Determination in Human Behavior.* Plenum.

## What the research does NOT support

- **Learning styles** (visual / auditory / kinaesthetic) — Pashler et al. (2008) reviewed this directly and found no credible evidence that matching instruction to learning style improves outcomes. Teaching to "VAK" profiles is not supported.
- **Pure discovery learning as a first pass** — Productive failure works *after* students have a knowledge base. Inquiry without prior knowledge tends to produce cognitive overload, not insight. Direct instruction is not the enemy of deeper learning — it is its prerequisite.
- **Long direct instruction blocks** — Working memory degrades after approximately 10–20 minutes of receiving new information without active processing. The 20-minute cap on direct instruction is a cognitive limit, not a stylistic preference.
- **Massed practice** — Studying all of one topic before moving on produces better same-day performance and worse long-term retention. Spacing and interleaving feel worse and work better.

---

## Alberta / Canada angle

- Alberta's curriculum redesign toward competency-based outcomes is compatible with this framework: retrieval practice, spaced practice, and formative feedback all support deep competency development rather than surface coverage.
- The three-encounters structure maps onto unit design: an outcome introduced in one lesson, practised in a second, and transferred or applied in a third is the minimum for lasting learning.
- Indigenous education principles in Alberta (e.g., First Nations, Métis, Inuit frameworks) emphasise relational and experiential knowledge — the structured collaborative practice phases (reciprocal teaching, peer work during guided practice) are consistent with these values, provided the context is made culturally meaningful.
