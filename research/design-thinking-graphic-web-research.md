# Design Thinking, Graphic Design, Web Design, and Sketching, Grades 5–9: Extending the STEAM Skill Base

*Research summary — September 2026*

---

## Why this matters

`steam-progression-research.md` sequences coding, robotics, spatial/mechanical reasoning, and the engineering design process across grades 5–9. That file treats "design" mostly as a process wrapper around a physical build (design–build–test–redesign for a robot or a construction task). This summary asks a narrower question: what does the evidence say about teaching **visual and interface design skills themselves** — sketching, graphic design principles, and web/UX design — as trainable competencies for this age band, with **iterative design thinking** as the connective thread rather than a decorative add-on?

The risk this file is meant to guard against is the same one the coding/robotics file names: treating "design thinking" as a poster on the wall (a five-step diagram: empathize–define–ideate–prototype–test) rather than a set of habits built through repeated, structured practice with real feedback. The literature below is explicit that this is exactly the failure mode design-thinking programs fall into when implemented shallowly.

---

## Core finding

**Design thinking's evidence base is strongest not for the process diagram itself but for two specific mechanisms inside it: (1) forcing multiple concept variants before committing to one, which counteracts a well-documented fixation effect, and (2) structured critique cycles, which are what actually drives iteration quality.** Sketching functions in this literature not as an art skill but as a *thinking tool* — a low-cost way to externalize and revise ideas before they're expensive to change. Graphic design and web/UX design have much thinner K–12-specific evidence than coding or robotics; the strongest available evidence is adjacent (fixation, prototyping, critique, adult/university design pedagogy) rather than age-specific. That gap should be treated honestly rather than papered over — see "What it doesn't support" below.

## Key studies and findings

### Carroll, Goldman, Britos, Koh, Royalty & Hornstein (2010) — Design thinking in a real middle school classroom
This ethnographic study (*International Journal of Art & Design Education*, 29(1), 37–53), part of Stanford's "Taking Design Thinking to Schools" research project, followed an interdisciplinary design curriculum taught by university instructors inside a public charter middle school. It found design thinking produced engagement and content learning gains when treated as a recurring practice with real affective stakes (ambiguity tolerance, empathy for a user, comfort with failure) — but the paper is explicit that these habits took sustained, repeated exposure to build, not a single unit, and that the *affective* dimension (tolerating the discomfort of not having an answer yet) was as hard to teach as the procedural steps.

**Implication:** Design thinking should be treated the same way the existing STEAM file treats the engineering design cycle — a recurring habit practiced across the year, not a one-off "design thinking day" — and time should be deliberately spent on the uncomfortable parts (not knowing the answer, redoing a "finished" design) rather than rushing past them to a clean final product.

**Source:** Carroll, M., Goldman, S., Britos, L., Koh, J., Royalty, A., & Hornstein, M. (2010). Destination, imagination and the fires within: Design thinking in a middle school classroom. *International Journal of Art & Design Education, 29*(1), 37–53. Stanford's REDlab (Research in Education & Design) hosts the full text.

### Jansson & Smith (1991) — Design fixation
This is the foundational empirical study on design fixation, published in *Design Studies* (12, 3–11). Across four experiments with senior engineering students and practicing engineers, participants shown a single example solution (even one with flaws explicitly pointed out) produced new designs that unconsciously reproduced the example's specific features, compared to a control group given no example. Providing an example did not reduce fluency (the number of ideas generated) — it narrowed the *range* of those ideas toward the example's features.

**Implication:** Showing students one "model answer" or exemplar design before they sketch their own is not a neutral scaffold — it measurably narrows what they generate. Any lesson that shows an example graphic, layout, or web mockup before asking students to design their own should either show multiple, deliberately varied examples, or withhold the example until after a first independent attempt.

**Source:** Jansson, D.G., & Smith, S.M. (1991). Design fixation. *Design Studies, 12*(1), 3–11.

### Dow, Glassco, Kass, Schwarz, Schwartz & Klemmer (2010) — Parallel prototyping
Published in *ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction* (17(4)), this study had novice designers create graphic web advertisements either serially (one prototype, get feedback, revise, repeat) or in parallel (create several variants, then get feedback on all of them at once) before producing a final version. Parallel prototypers produced higher-quality final designs (measured by both click-through data and subjective ratings), generated more divergent ideas, and reported higher task confidence and self-efficacy than serial prototypers — despite doing comparable total work.

**Implication:** This is the direct evidence-based counter to Jansson & Smith's fixation problem: instead of "sketch one idea, get feedback, refine it," structure early design tasks (a logo concept, a webpage wireframe, a poster layout) as "generate 3–5 distinct concepts before choosing one to develop." This is a concrete, gradeable structure, not just a slogan — and it was tested specifically on a graphic/web design task (ad design), making it one of the more directly transferable studies in this file.

**Source:** Dow, S.P., Glassco, A., Kass, J., Schwarz, M., Schwartz, D.L., & Klemmer, S.R. (2010). Parallel prototyping leads to better design results, more divergence, and increased self-efficacy. *ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction, 17*(4), Article 18.

### Tversky — Sketching as a thinking and communication tool
Barbara Tversky's body of work (Stanford; summarized in her 2019 book *Mind in Motion: How Action Shapes Thought*, and in papers such as "Thinking with Sketches") establishes sketching as a distinct cognitive act, not a rendering skill: in design ideation, sketches support "appositional reasoning" — the back-and-forth between a design problem and emerging solution ideas — that happens differently, and often more productively, than reasoning in words alone. A rough external sketch lets a designer notice unintended features, relationships, or problems in their own idea that were invisible while it was still just a mental image.

**Implication:** This reframes sketching in the sequence away from "can this student draw well" toward "does this student externalize an idea fast and roughly before committing to it." A required rough sketch step before any digital tool (graphic design software, web mockup tool) is not a warm-up — it's doing cognitive work that the polished digital version can't replace, and rushing to the computer too early may skip it entirely.

**Source:** Tversky, B. (2019). *Mind in Motion: How Action Shapes Thought.* Basic Books. See also Tversky's papers on diagrammatic reasoning and sketching in design cognition (Stanford Psychology / REDlab).

### Design critique / "crit" pedagogy — Goldschmidt and the design-studio literature
Research on studio critique (most notably Goldschmidt, Hochman & Dafni's studies of the design studio "crit" as teacher–student communication, published in *AI EDAM*) documents the crit as the central mechanism by which design students acquire judgment, not just technique — critique sessions are where students learn to see their own work the way an audience or user will, which is otherwise very hard to self-generate. This literature is concentrated in university-level architecture, industrial design, and HCI studio education; it has not been systematically studied at the K–12 level, but the underlying mechanism (external, structured feedback exposing gaps invisible to the maker) is consistent with the general feedback literature already used elsewhere in this workspace.

**Implication:** A structured peer-critique protocol (specific prompts: "what is this trying to do," "where does it succeed," "where would a first-time user get confused") should be a recurring, scheduled step for graphic and web design tasks — not an optional add-on — mirroring the "peer-reviewed design critique step" already specified for grades 7–8 in the existing STEAM progression table.

**Source:** Goldschmidt, G., Hochman, H., & Dafni, I. The design studio "crit": Teacher–student communication. *AI EDAM (Artificial Intelligence for Engineering Design, Analysis and Manufacturing).* (K–12-specific critique research is thin — flagged in "What it doesn't support" below.)

### Drawing as a trainable perceptual skill (not innate talent)
Academic research on children's drawing instruction (e.g., work summarized in *Frontiers in Psychology* on STEAM-based drawing skill transfer, and studies on scientific/observational drawing instruction for ages 5–8) supports the general claim that observational drawing accuracy improves with direct, purposeful instruction — children do not intuitively know which features of a subject are salient to represent, and even simple instructional interventions measurably increase observational and representational accuracy. This is a narrower and more defensible claim than the popular-press framing found in Betty Edwards's *Drawing on the Right Side of the Brain* (1979/2012), whose specific "right-brain/left-brain" neurological mechanism has been superseded by later neuroscience (hemispheric lateralization does not map onto "artistic" vs. "analytic" thinking the way the book claims), even though many of its practical exercises (contour drawing, drawing negative space, drawing upside-down to disrupt symbolic shortcuts) remain widely used and are plausible on cognitive-load grounds rather than the book's stated brain-hemisphere rationale.

**Implication:** Drawing/sketching skill should be taught as a set of explicit, practiced techniques (observational strategies, proportion checks, redrawing from multiple angles) rather than framed to students as "some people are creative and some aren't." Betty Edwards's exercises can be borrowed for their practical value, but the workspace should not cite her book's neuroscience claims as evidence — cite the observational-drawing-instruction literature instead if a research grounding is needed.

**Source:** Frontiers in Psychology (2022), "A study of practical drawing skills and knowledge transferable skills of children based on STEAM education." Additional supporting work on observational drawing instruction in early-elementary science contexts (*International Journal of Science Education*, 2025, "Developing young children's perceptual observations and representations: draw like a scientist"). Edwards, B. (1979/2012). *Drawing on the Right Side of the Brain.* Tarcher/Putnam — cited here only to flag it explicitly as popular-press, not peer-reviewed, per the workspace's evidence standard.

## Effect sizes and outcome data

- **Parallel prototyping (Dow et al. 2010):** reports quantitative gains in both objective (click-through rate on final ad designs) and subjective (rated design quality) measures for the parallel-prototyping condition versus serial, plus higher self-reported task confidence — one of the more directly quantified findings in this file, and unusually relevant because the task studied was graphic/web ad design specifically.
- **Design fixation (Jansson & Smith 1991):** the finding is about feature-similarity/narrowing rather than a single effect-size number, but it is a well-replicated effect in subsequent design-cognition research (see the 2020 *Journal of Mechanical Design* replication on design fixation from provided vs. self-generated examples), which strengthens confidence it is not a one-off result.
- **Design thinking in middle school (Carroll et al. 2010):** qualitative/ethnographic — the study documents engagement and behavioral change (increased tolerance for ambiguity, willingness to iterate) rather than a quantified effect size. Treat its claims as directional, not as a number to cite.
- **Drawing instruction:** effect data in the K–12 drawing-instruction literature is more localized (accuracy on specific observational-drawing tasks in early-elementary science contexts) than a broad "drawing ability" measure — there is no equivalent here to Uttal & Cohen's d ≈ 0.4–0.5 for spatial training in the companion file. This is a real evidence gap, not an oversight.
- **Graphic design principles instruction and web/UX design for ages 10–15:** no controlled outcome studies were found specific to this age band. The principles themselves (grid, hierarchy, contrast, type) are well established in professional design-education practice and adult/university pedagogy, but claims of what happens when they're taught to 10–15-year-olds specifically should be treated as reasonable extrapolation, not confirmed by dedicated research.

## What it looks like in practice — a grade 5–9 progression

This progression fades each strand's own concrete-to-abstract scaffold, the same way the companion file fades coding (block-to-text) and robotics (tangible-to-sensor-driven). It is meant to run **parallel to**, not instead of, the existing coding/robotics/construction/engineering-design table — design and sketching work should sit inside the same engineering-design-cycle units already scheduled, functioning as the "define the problem visually" and "communicate the solution" steps of that cycle, plus its own dedicated sessions where the product itself is visual/interface-based (a poster, a game UI mockup, a simple webpage).

| Grade band | Sketching | Graphic design | Web design / UX | Design thinking process |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **5–6** | Daily/weekly rough sketching as externalization, not evaluated for artistic quality — observational drawing tasks (draw the mechanism you built, draw your robot's planned path) paired with explicit technique instruction (proportion checks, redrawing from a second angle) | Concrete, physical-first: paper collage/cut-paper layout exercises teaching grid, hierarchy, and contrast before any software; explicit vocabulary named (hierarchy, contrast, alignment) the same way CT vocabulary is named in the coding strand | Paper-prototype only: students sketch a screen/app idea on paper (no software), walk a peer through it, peer "clicks" by pointing — building the concept of a user flow before any digital tool exists | Full design cycle with a *required multiple-concept step*: no single "first idea" gets built — every task requires 3+ rough sketch variants before one is chosen (direct application of Dow et al. and Jansson & Smith) |
| **7–8** | Sketching integrated into the engineering design cycle as the default "propose a solution" step for every unit, including non-visual ones (sketch your code's logic flow, sketch your robot's sensor layout) | Software introduced (e.g., a free/simple graphic design tool) but grid/hierarchy/contrast taught first on paper and only then transferred to software — first structured peer critique step using specific prompts, not free-form feedback | Low-fidelity digital wireframing tool introduces the paper-prototype ideas in digital form; first explicit "who is this for and what do they need" user-need framing exercise before building | Peer critique formalized as a recurring protocol (mirrors the existing 7–8 "peer-reviewed design critique step" in the companion file); design thinking applied to open-ended briefs with competing constraints, same as the engineering-design column |
| **9** (HS-ready) | Sketching used as a professional-style communication tool: annotated concept sketches presented to justify a design decision, not just generate one | Independent application of design principles to a real deliverable (e.g., branding/promotional material for a school project or the STEAM course itself) with a rubric addressing grid, hierarchy, contrast, and type as named criteria | A real, simple website or app-mockup project applying basic accessibility/user-centered principles (e.g., contrast for readability, clear navigation) — framed explicitly as evidence-thin territory for this age group (see limitations below), so keep the bar realistic: usable and accessible, not professional-grade | Design thinking initiated independently by the student as a project-management framework, mirroring the companion file's Grade 9 column; documented iteration history (multiple concepts generated, critique received, what changed and why) becomes an explicit deliverable, not just a process students went through invisibly |

**Cross-cutting practice, every grade:** never show a single example/model before an independent first attempt (Jansson & Smith); require multiple rough concepts before refining one (Dow et al.); build in a scheduled critique step with specific prompts rather than open-ended "what do you think" (Goldschmidt et al.); treat sketching as a required externalization step even in coding/robotics units, not just in visually-branded "design" units.

## What it doesn't support

- **It does not support "design thinking" taught as a five-step poster with no recurring practice.** Carroll et al.'s middle-school study found the affective and behavioral gains came from sustained exposure with real stakes, not from teaching the steps as content to memorize — this is the same failure mode the companion file already names for the engineering design cycle, and it applies just as directly here.
- **It does not support showing a finished example before students attempt their own design.** Jansson & Smith's fixation effect is specific and well-replicated: an example (even a flawed one, even with its flaws pointed out) measurably narrows what students generate. If an exemplar is needed for clarity, show several deliberately different ones, or show it only after a first independent attempt.
- **It does not have strong, age-specific research for web design/UX instruction in the 10–15 age band.** The UX/UCD literature found is either about designing products *for* children (a different question — what makes a children's app usable) or university/professional UX pedagogy, not about *teaching* UX design skills *to* middle schoolers. The web design column in the table above should be treated as a reasonable extrapolation from adjacent evidence (parallel prototyping, critique, user-need framing), not as something with dedicated K–12 outcome studies behind it. Keep ambition here modest and be honest with the teacher that this is the thinnest-evidence strand in the file.
- **It does not support citing Betty Edwards's right-brain/left-brain mechanism as evidence**, even though her practical exercises are widely used and plausible. If drawing-instruction research grounding is needed for a specific claim, cite the observational-drawing-instruction literature (e.g., the *Frontiers in Psychology* STEAM drawing study, or the *International Journal of Science Education* "draw like a scientist" study) instead.
- **It does not support K–12-specific evidence for the design-studio "crit" as a pedagogy.** The Goldschmidt et al. line of research is university/professional-studio research (architecture, industrial design, HCI). The implication drawn for this workspace (a structured, prompt-based peer critique protocol) is a reasonable transfer of the mechanism, not a claim that the crit format itself has been validated with 10–15-year-olds.
- **It does not identify a specific graphic design or web design tool/platform.** As with the companion file's stance on coding/robotics platforms, the evidence supports the sequencing and pedagogical principles (paper before software, multiple concepts before refinement, structured critique), not a specific product — tool choice should follow budget and continuity, not a claim that a specific tool produces these outcomes.
- **This file does not cover rubric design for evaluating visual/design work.** General rubric design is now in `rubric-design-research.md`. Grading graphic or web design fairly (naming grid, hierarchy, contrast and type as criteria without rewarding taste) is still a separate, thinly researched problem.

## Alberta/Canada angle

As with the companion STEAM file, Alberta's K–9 curriculum does not have a dedicated K–9 visual/graphic/web design strand — design and visual communication concepts appear piecemeal through Fine Arts (visual art outcomes), ELA (visual/multimodal texts), and, at the junior high level, CTF options that can include design-adjacent courses (e.g., Communication Technology). This means a school running this expanded STEAM sequence is again building supplementary scope rather than delivering a mandated program, which has the same two practical implications named in the companion file: (1) there is no provincial scope-and-sequence to align pacing to, so the table above is a reasonable default rather than a mandated one; and (2) framing the Grade 9 web/graphic design capstone around outcome structures used in Alberta's CTF Communication Technology strand (rather than a specific tool or platform) gives the sequence a real downstream target regardless of which high school program a student later enters.
