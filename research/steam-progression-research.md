# STEAM Progression, Grades 5–9: Scaffolding Toward Robotics, Coding, Design, and Construction
*Research summary — September 2026*

---

## Why this matters

"STEAM" is often built backward from the equipment a school owns rather than from how children actually develop the underlying capacities — spatial reasoning, computational thinking, engineering habits of mind. A robotics kit handed to a Grade 5 class with the same task structure used in Grade 9 will either be too abstract to access or, if simplified, will teach the tool without building the transferable skill. The research base treats coding, robotics, and design/construction as convergent expressions of a smaller number of underlying competencies that develop on their own timelines. A scaffolded 5–9 sequence should be built from those timelines, not from unit themes.

This file covers four strands that recur across the STEAM literature — computational thinking, spatial/mechanical reasoning, the engineering design process, and making/construction — and what the evidence says about sequencing each from upper elementary into a high-school-ready state.

---

## Core finding

**Concrete manipulation must precede and run alongside abstraction, and the delay in removing concrete supports is the most common scaffolding error.** Across coding (block-based before text-based), robotics (physical/tangible before screen-only), and engineering design (build-first iteration before formal design documentation), the research consistently shows two failure modes: introducing abstraction too early (students manage syntax or notation instead of the underlying concept) and *never* removing the concrete scaffold (students plateau at a level below what they're developmentally ready for). The sequencing problem for grades 5–9 is therefore not "what topics in what order" but "when does each concrete scaffold get faded, for this specific competency."

## Key studies and findings

### Papert (1980) and Resnick (2017) — Constructionism and the creative learning spiral
Papert's constructionism argues that children learn most durably when building a public, shareable artifact (a program, a robot, a structure) rather than receiving instruction about a concept in the abstract. Resnick's "creative learning spiral" (Imagine → Create → Play → Share → Reflect → Imagine) operationalizes this for coding and making specifically, and underlies the design of Scratch.

**Implication:** Every grade band should produce an artifact the student can demonstrate and explain, not just complete an exercise. The "share" and "reflect" steps are frequently the ones cut under time pressure — they are where the transferable learning is consolidated, not the build step itself.

**Source:** Papert, S. (1980). *Mindstorms: Children, Computers, and Powerful Ideas.* Basic Books. Resnick, M. (2017). *Lifelong Kindergarten.* MIT Press.

---

### Bers (2018, 2020) — Coding as a developmental language, tangible-before-screen sequencing
Bers's work (KIBO robotics, ScratchJr) establishes that children as young as 4–7 can grasp sequencing, loops, and conditionals when the interface is physical/tangible (arranging wooden blocks that program a robot) rather than screen-based. The competency (algorithmic sequencing) is present well before the fine-motor and abstraction demands of text-based coding are appropriate. Her longitudinal work with elementary populations found that early tangible coding experience predicted comfort with more abstract coding later, but only when the *concept* (sequence, loop, conditional, debugging) was named and made explicit — not left implicit in the play.

**Implication:** For a 5–9 sequence, the youngest band (Grade 5) does not need "easier" programming languages so much as programming *interfaces* that are more concrete than the ones used by Grade 8–9 — and the vocabulary (sequence, loop, conditional, variable, debug) should be introduced explicitly at first exposure, however concrete the medium, so it transfers forward.

**Source:** Bers, M.U. (2018). *Coding as a Playground.* Routledge. Bers, M.U. (2020). *Beyond Coding.* MIT Press.

---

### Grover & Pea (2013); Weintrop et al. (2016) — Computational thinking in K–12
Grover and Pea's widely-cited review found that computational thinking (decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction, algorithm design) is teachable across K–12 but that most implementations conflate "using a coding tool" with "developing computational thinking," and that explicit instruction in the CT vocabulary and transfer tasks (applying decomposition to a non-coding problem) produced stronger transfer than tool time alone. Weintrop and colleagues extended this specifically into STEM classrooms, showing CT practices (e.g., using computational models, systematic testing) map cleanly onto practices already valued in science and math — meaning CT does not need its own siloed block, but the mapping needs to be made explicit or it goes unnoticed by students.

**Implication:** Block coding time in Grades 5–6 should be paired with the same vocabulary used later in text-based coding (Grades 8–9) and in science/math classes, so students recognize decomposition and pattern recognition as the same skill across contexts rather than relearning it per subject.

**Source:** Grover, S. & Pea, R. (2013). Computational thinking in K–12: A review of the state of the field. *Educational Researcher, 42*(1), 38–43. Weintrop, D. et al. (2016). Defining computational thinking for mathematics and science classrooms. *Journal of Science Education and Technology, 25*, 127–147.

---

### Uttal & Cohen (2012); Newcombe (2010) — Spatial reasoning as the hidden prerequisite for robotics and construction
Uttal and Cohen's meta-analysis found spatial reasoning (mental rotation, spatial visualization) is both trainable through deliberate practice and a strong predictor of later STEM achievement and persistence — independent of general ability. Newcombe's work argues spatial reasoning is the most under-taught STEM prerequisite in K–12, despite being highly malleable with short, repeated practice (unlike some other cognitive predictors). Construction tasks (building with constraints, assembling mechanisms, orienting a robot's sensor field relative to its planned path) are among the highest-yield spatial reasoning activities identified in this literature, more so than worksheet-based spatial training.

**Implication:** Physical construction and mechanical assembly tasks in Grades 5–7 are not "warm-up" activities before the "real" robotics/coding work — they are building a measurable prerequisite (spatial reasoning) for later robotics performance, and should be sequenced deliberately rather than treated as filler or reward activities.

**Source:** Uttal, D.H. & Cohen, C.A. (2012). Spatial thinking and STEM education. *Psychology of Learning and Motivation, 57*, 147–181. Newcombe, N.S. (2010). Picture this: Increasing math and science learning by improving spatial thinking. *American Educator, 34*(2), 29–35.

---

### Cunningham & Kelly (2017); Fortus & Krajcik — The engineering design process as a teachable habit of mind, not a one-time unit
Cunningham and Kelly's analysis of engineering-education implementations found that the engineering design process (define problem → brainstorm → design → build → test → redesign) is frequently taught as a poster or a single unit, which produces only surface familiarity with the *steps* rather than the underlying "engineering habits of mind" (systems thinking, attention to constraints, tolerance for productive failure, optimizing under trade-offs). Fortus and Krajcik's design-based science work found that engineering design tasks embedded repeatedly across a multi-year sequence — with escalating constraint complexity — built the habits of mind that a single capstone project does not.

**Implication:** The engineering design cycle should recur across every grade 5–9 year, with the *constraints* escalating (budget, material limits, competing design requirements, sensor/actuator limits) rather than being taught once as a standalone process unit and assumed to transfer.

**Source:** Cunningham, C.M. & Kelly, G.J. (2017). Epistemic practices of engineering for education. *Science Education, 101*(3), 486–505. Fortus, D. et al. (2004). Design-based science and student learning. *Journal of Research in Science Teaching, 41*(10), 1081–1110.

---

### Blikstein (2013) — Digital fabrication, making, and the "low floor, high ceiling, wide walls" principle
Blikstein's studies of maker/fabrication programs (3D printing, laser cutting, simple electronics) found the most durable STEAM engagement occurred in environments meeting Resnick's "low floor, high ceiling, wide walls" criterion: easy to start, room to grow in sophistication, and multiple valid paths to a solution. Programs that instead specified a single correct build (kit instructions with one right answer) produced short-term engagement but did not transfer to independent design behavior — students could follow instructions but froze when given an open brief.

**Implication:** Even in earlier grades, some fraction of construction/robotics tasks should be open-ended (multiple valid solutions) rather than 100% guided-build, or the sequence will produce instruction-followers rather than designers by Grade 9.

**Source:** Blikstein, P. (2013). Digital fabrication and 'making' in education: The democratization of invention. In Walter-Herrmann & Büching (Eds.), *FabLabs: Of Machines, Makers and Inventors.* transcript Verlag.

## Effect sizes and outcome data

- Spatial training interventions: Uttal & Cohen's meta-analysis reports effect sizes in the moderate range (roughly d = 0.4–0.5) for spatial-reasoning training transferring to spatial task performance, with some evidence of transfer to broader STEM measures — one of the more robust "trainable predictor" findings in this literature.
- Computational thinking: effect data is more mixed and implementation-dependent; Grover & Pea's review is explicit that many CT interventions lack transfer measures at all, which is itself a finding — outcome claims in this space should be treated cautiously until a given curriculum has been evaluated with a transfer task, not just a completion rate.
- Engineering design and PBL: Fortus & Krajcik and related design-based science work report gains in both content knowledge and design-process measures for multi-year embedded implementations, but single-unit "engineering day" implementations show little durable effect — the dosage and recurrence matter as much as the activity type.

## What it looks like in practice — a grade 5–9 progression

The literature does not converge on one named curriculum sequence, but the four strands above imply a consistent shape: each strand fades a concrete scaffold roughly one grade band at a time, and all four strands should run in parallel every year rather than being taught as separate units (e.g., "robotics term" then "coding term").

| Grade band | Coding | Robotics | Design/construction | Engineering design process |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **5–6** | Block-based (Scratch/MakeCode), explicit CT vocabulary named alongside every activity (sequence, loop, conditional, debug) | Tangible or block-programmed robot (e.g., programming a robot's *path* with blocks, not text); emphasis on sensor concept, not sensor code | Guided-build with escalating constraints (fixed materials, then material budget); spatial/mechanical assembly tasks (gears, levers, simple linkages) as deliberate spatial-reasoning practice | Full design cycle run 2–3 times per year on small, low-stakes problems; explicit naming of "constraint," "trade-off," "iteration" |
| **7–8** | Transition band: block-to-text (e.g., MakeCode's block/JavaScript toggle, or Python via a beginner-friendly IDE); first independent debugging tasks | Sensor-driven programming (line-following, obstacle avoidance) — first tasks requiring students to reconcile a mental model of physical space with code logic | Open-ended briefs with multiple valid solutions (Blikstein's "wide walls"); first use of CAD or simple 3D design tools tied to a physical build | Design cycle with competing constraints (cost vs. strength vs. weight); first peer-reviewed design critique step |
| **9** (HS-ready) | Text-based coding as primary mode (Python or similar), with block tools available only as scaffolding for struggling students, not the default | Full sensor + actuator integration project (autonomous or semi-autonomous task) mirroring the structure of HS robotics competition tasks (FIRST/VEX-style) without requiring a specific platform | Independent design-build-test-redesign project with a real external constraint (budget, material sourcing, or a rubric modeled on an HS CTS/CTF outcome) | Design cycle used as a project-management framework the student initiates without prompting, including documenting iteration history |

**Cross-cutting practice, every grade:** pair every build/code task with a share-and-reflect step (Resnick), and keep at least one open-ended task per unit even in Grade 5 (Blikstein) so "follow the instructions" is never the only mode students practice.

## What it doesn't support

- **It does not support "more screen time coding = more computational thinking."** Tool time without explicit CT vocabulary and transfer tasks shows weak or unmeasured transfer (Grover & Pea). A sequence built around maximizing hours in a coding platform, without the explicit-naming and transfer step, is not supported by this evidence base.
- **It does not support a single "engineering unit" as sufficient.** The design-based science literature is specific that dosage and recurrence across years matter; a one-off capstone project, however well-run, does not produce durable engineering habits of mind on its own.
- **It does not support skipping the concrete/tangible stage for younger grades to "get to real coding/robotics faster."** Bers's findings are specific that the developmental value of tangible interfaces is in building the concept, not just entertaining younger students — rushing past it produces students who can operate a tool without the underlying model.
- **It does not identify one correct platform or kit.** The evidence supports the sequencing principles (concrete-to-abstract fading, escalating constraints, open-ended tasks alongside guided ones) but is agnostic on Scratch vs. MakeCode, or which robotics kit — platform choice should be driven by budget and continuity across grades, not by claims that a specific product produces the outcomes above.
- **This file does not cover assessment design for STEAM/project work.** General rubric design is now in `rubric-design-research.md` (including Goldberg 2014, a worked revision of an engineering design portfolio rubric). Outcome evidence for rubrics on open-ended builds specifically is still thin.

## Alberta/Canada angle

Alberta's K–9 curriculum currently addresses computational thinking and coding concepts primarily through the CTF (Career and Technology Foundations) courses at the junior high level and through cross-curricular competencies (problem solving, digital literacy) named in the redesigned curriculum, rather than a dedicated K–9 CS strand — meaning a school running a dedicated STEAM sequence in grades 5–9 is building supplementary scope, not implementing a mandated program. This has two practical implications: (1) there is no provincial scope-and-sequence to align to for pacing, so the grade-band table above is a reasonable default rather than a curriculum-mandated one; (2) framing the Grade 9 capstone explicitly around the kind of outcome structure used in Alberta high school CTS/CTF robotics and design courses (rather than a specific competition platform) gives the sequence a real downstream target without depending on which high school program a given student ends up in.
