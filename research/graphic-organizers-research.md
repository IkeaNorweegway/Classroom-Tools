## 1. Topic and Why It Matters

A graphic organizer externalises the structure of a text or concept — hierarchy, sequence, comparison, cause-effect — into a visual layout, so the student doesn't have to hold that structure in working memory while also processing content. This matters most for two overlapping groups: students with reading disabilities or LD (working memory and comprehension monitoring are frequently the actual bottleneck, not content difficulty), and ELL students (organizers scaffold conceptual structure independent of language proficiency). It's also one of the few strategies with converging effect sizes across multiple independent meta-analyses, which is uncommon in education research.

---

## 2. Core Finding

Graphic organizers work by **offloading working memory**, not by simplifying content. The student still has to determine the relationships (hierarchy, sequence, comparison); the organizer just removes the burden of tracking those relationships purely in memory while also reading and reasoning. This is why the effect is consistently stronger when the organizer is **learner-generated** (the student builds or fills it in) rather than handed over pre-filled as a reference — a pre-filled organizer removes some of the very thinking that produces the effect.

Graphic organizers are classified as a **hard (static) scaffold**: planned in advance, embedded in the material, available to every student regardless of their moment-to-moment state — as opposed to a contingent scaffold like a teacher's real-time hint. This matters for print materials specifically, since print can only ever deliver hard scaffolds (see `_meta-context.md` → Scaffolding Architecture).

## 3. Key Studies

- **Hattie (2009)**, *Visible Learning* — graphic organizers, **d = 0.57**, comfortably above Hattie's d = 0.40 "hinge point" (the threshold he uses to separate strategies worth prioritizing from those that aren't). Synthesized from earlier meta-analytic work including Kim et al. (2004).

- **Kim, Vaughn, Wanzek, & Wei (2004)**, *Journal of Learning Disabilities* — meta-analysis establishing significant positive effects of graphic organizers on reading comprehension specifically for students with learning disabilities and ELL students. This is the foundational study behind the Hattie d = 0.57 figure and the general claim that organizers work for both populations, not just one.

- **Jiang & Grabe (2007)** — positive effects of graphic organizers on second-language reading comprehension specifically. Relevant wherever ELL and LD populations overlap in a classroom.

- **Dexter & Hughes (2011)**, *Learning Disability Quarterly* — meta-analysis of graphic organizers for students with LD, Grades 4–12, 16 studies / 808 participants, across English, science, social studies, and math. This is the study that isolates the learner-generated effect specifically: **g = 0.59** (memory outcomes), **g = 0.63** (comprehension outcomes) for learner-generated use, higher than teacher-provided/pre-filled use.

- **Dexter, Park, & Hughes (2011)**, *Learning Disabilities Research & Practice* — the same research group's meta-analysis focused specifically on graphic organizers in **science instruction** for adolescents with LD. Confirms the effect transfers to science content specifically — directly relevant to this workspace's Grade 6/7 science materials.

## 4. Effect Sizes / Outcome Data

| Source | Population | Outcome | Effect size |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hattie (2009) | General | Achievement (synthesized) | d = 0.57 |
| Kim et al. (2004) | LD + ELL | Reading comprehension | Significant positive (meta-analytic; feeds Hattie figure) |
| Dexter & Hughes (2011) | LD, Grades 4–12, learner-generated | Memory | g = 0.59 |
| Dexter & Hughes (2011) | LD, Grades 4–12, learner-generated | Comprehension | g = 0.63 |
| Dexter, Park & Hughes (2011) | LD, adolescents, science specifically | Comprehension | Positive, consistent with above (science subgroup analysis) |

Reference frame (What Works Clearinghouse): small ≈ 0.2–0.4, medium ≈ 0.5–0.8. Graphic organizers sit consistently in the medium range across every source above — one of the more reliably-replicated effects in this workspace's evidence base, alongside worked examples (also d = 0.57, Hattie).

## 5. What It Looks Like in Practice

- **Learner-generated, not pre-filled.** The student fills in, sorts, or builds the organizer — this is where the g = 0.59–0.63 effect comes from specifically (Dexter & Hughes, 2011). A pre-labeled reference diagram is a legitimate visual aid, but it is a different tool doing a different job (see caveat below) — don't conflate the two when claiming the evidence.
- **Word banks reduce retrieval demand without eliminating the thinking.** Supplying the term set but requiring the student to place each term correctly still requires the relational thinking the effect depends on; supplying pre-filled answers does not.
- **Works across content areas** — English, science, social studies, math (Dexter & Hughes, 2011) and specifically in science content for adolescents with LD (Dexter, Park & Hughes, 2011).
- **Works for both LD and ELL populations independently** (Kim et al., 2004), so an organizer built for one population's needs (e.g., an IPP/reading-gap student) is not wasted effort for a broader class with ELL students in it.
- **Pair with the diagram, not instead of it, for severe reading gaps.** For a student who can't yet decode the surrounding text, the organizer's visual structure plus an audio/read-aloud pairing (see `ld-ipp-reading-gap-research.md`) does more than the organizer alone.

---

## 6. What It Doesn't Support

- **It doesn't support handing over a fully pre-labeled diagram and calling it evidence-based on the strength of these studies.** The effect sizes above are specifically for learner-generated/fill-in use. A pre-labeled reference image (e.g., a polished illustration used purely for orientation) is a reasonable design choice for a different reason — visual clarity, reducing extraneous cognitive load, building initial familiarity — but it's not the mechanism these studies measured. If a material uses both (a labeled reference image alongside a separate fill-in organizer), that's two different tools serving two different purposes, not double-counted evidence.
- **It doesn't support organizers as a substitute for content-area intervention or reading instruction.** Organizers offload working memory around content the student is already engaging with; they don't teach decoding or build reading level.
- **The evidence doesn't isolate organizer *type* (Venn diagram vs. hierarchy chart vs. sequence chain) as differentially effective.** The studies aggregate across organizer types; matching organizer shape to the concept's actual structure (comparison → Venn/table, process → sequence/cycle, structure-function → labeled diagram) is a design inference from cognitive load theory, not a separately-measured effect.
- **Most studies are Grades 4–12 aggregated, not grade-specific.** Effects for a Grade 7 student specifically are inferred from the broader Grades 4–12 band, not isolated.
