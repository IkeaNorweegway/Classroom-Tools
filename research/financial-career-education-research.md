# Financial and Career Education — Research Summary
*Research session: October 2026. Covers the financial-education meta-analyses (Fernandes, Lynch & Netemeyer 2014 → Kaiser, Lusardi, Menkhoff & Urban 2022), school-based financial education, the OECD Career Readiness indicators (2021), and the Gatsby Benchmarks. Written for the CEFL 7–9 course (`_courses/cefl/_context.md`).*

---

## How to Read This File

This file is the subject-specific evidence base for CEFL. The general project design evidence is in `research/project-based-learning-research.md`; read both before building CEFL materials.

The key findings that matter most:
1. **Financial education works, but modestly, and knowledge moves more than behaviour.** About 0.2 SD on knowledge and 0.1 SD on behaviour across 76 RCTs.
2. **The old "it barely works and decays fast" finding came from a small sample.** The newer meta-analysis finds effects 3–5× larger and no clear evidence of rapid decay, but no proof against decay either.
3. **Teach close to the decision.** "Just-in-time" teaching tied to a specific choice is the best-supported way to protect against decay, which is why CEFL places its teaching moments inside projects.
4. **Career readiness is about doing, not just learning about.** Teenagers who explore, experience and think about work (talking with people in jobs, visiting workplaces, part-time work, volunteering) have better adult outcomes. These are associations, not proven causes.

---

## Topic and Why It Matters

CEFL 7–9 asks students to understand pay, tax, budgets, interest and credit, and to explore careers and post-secondary pathways. Two questions decide how to teach it:
- **Does financial education change what students know and do?** If yes, how much, and what makes it stick?
- **Which career activities in school are linked to better outcomes later?**

The answers set the shape of the course: short, concrete teaching moments placed next to a real decision, and projects that make students talk to real people, look at real postings and costs, and plan.

---

## Core Finding

**Financial education reliably raises financial knowledge (~0.2 SD) and has smaller but real effects on behaviour (~0.1 SD).** These effects are comparable to other education interventions and are cheap per student. They are largest when teaching is concrete and tied to an upcoming decision. **Career activities that put teenagers in contact with real work and real people are linked to lower unemployment, higher wages and higher job satisfaction in adulthood.**

## Key Studies

### 1. Fernandes, Lynch & Netemeyer (2014) — the pessimistic baseline

- Meta-analysis of 168 papers (201 studies) in *Management Science* 60(8), 1861–1883.
- Interventions to improve financial literacy explained **only 0.1% of the variance** in financial behaviours, with weaker effects in low-income samples.
- **Decay:** even large programs had negligible effects on behaviour **20 months or more** after the intervention.
- **Recommendation:** "just-in-time" financial education, delivered right before the decision it is meant to help (e.g., before taking a loan), instead of general courses far from the decision.

### 2. Kaiser, Lusardi, Menkhoff & Urban (2022) — the current best estimate

- *Journal of Financial Economics* 145(2), 255–272. **76 randomized experiments, 33 countries, over 160,000 people.** RCTs only.
- **Weighted average effects: ~0.20 SD on financial knowledge and ~0.10 SD on financial behaviours.** Knowledge effects are comparable to math and reading interventions; behaviour effects are comparable to health-behaviour interventions.
- **After correcting for publication bias:** ~0.15 SD on knowledge and ~0.06 SD on behaviour. The effects remain different from zero.
- At least **3× (up to 5×) larger** than Fernandes et al. (2014).
- **Decay:** no clear evidence of rapid decay; effects were measured on average ~30 weeks after the intervention and up to two years. The authors say the evidence neither confirms nor rules out decay.
- **Cost:** mean ~$60 per participant (median ~$23), "low cost" on Kraft's scale for a "medium" education effect.

### 3. Kaiser & Menkhoff (2020) — school programs specifically

- *Economics of Education Review* 78, 101930. 37 (quasi-)experiments in schools.
- **+0.33 SD on financial knowledge, +0.07 SD on financial behaviour.** Restricting to 18 randomized experiments: ~0.15 SD on knowledge, ~0.07 SD on behaviour.
- More intensive programs and smaller classes are associated with larger effects, with diminishing returns.

### 4. Frisancho (2020/2023) — a large school RCT with long follow-up

- Randomized trial of a high-school financial education program in Peru (*Economic Journal*, 2023).
- Raised financial literacy, and credit-bureau records three years later showed **limited but positive long-lasting changes in behaviour**: treated students were less likely to have negative records from unpaid or delinquent bills.
- Spillovers: teachers borrowed more from banks and were less often delinquent; in poorer households, parents' default probability fell.

### 5. OECD Career Readiness indicators (Covacevich, Mann, Santos & Champaud, 2021)

- OECD Education Working Paper No. 258. National longitudinal datasets from eight countries, following teenagers into adulthood, controlling for achievement, social background, gender and geography.
- Teenagers who **explore, experience and think about** their futures in work have **lower unemployment, higher wages and greater career satisfaction** as adults. International evidence supports **11 of 14** candidate indicators.
- **Exploring the future:** career conversations, career talks and job fairs with people from the world of work, workplace visits and job shadowing, career-related research.
- **Experiencing the future:** part-time work, work placements, volunteering.
- **Thinking about the future:** career certainty, career ambition, career alignment (whether a student's education plans match their career goal).
- A related OECD summary reports students who interacted with employers during school later earned **typically 5–10% more** than comparable peers (secondary source; check before citing formally).
- **Caveat:** associations, not proven causes.

### 6. Gatsby Benchmarks (Holman, 2014)

England's eight benchmarks of good career guidance, part of its national careers strategy since 2017: a stable careers program; learning from career and labour market information; addressing the needs of each student; **linking curriculum learning to careers**; **encounters with employers and employees**; **experiences of workplaces**; encounters with further and higher education; personal guidance. Useful as a checklist for what a school career program should contain; it is a framework, not an effect-size study.

## Effect Sizes or Outcome Data — At a Glance

| Source | Type | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Kaiser, Lusardi, Menkhoff & Urban 2022 | Meta-analysis, 76 RCTs | ~0.20 SD knowledge, ~0.10 SD behaviour |
| Same, bias-corrected | Meta-analysis | ~0.15 SD knowledge, ~0.06 SD behaviour |
| Kaiser & Menkhoff 2020, schools | Meta-analysis, 37 studies | +0.33 SD knowledge, +0.07 SD behaviour |
| Kaiser & Menkhoff 2020, school RCTs only | 18 RCTs | ~0.15 SD knowledge, ~0.07 SD behaviour |
| Fernandes et al. 2014 | Meta-analysis | 0.1% of variance in behaviour; decay by ~20 months |
| Frisancho 2023 | School RCT, Peru | Fewer delinquent credit records 3 years later |
| OECD 2021 | Longitudinal, 8 countries | 11 of 14 teenage career indicators linked to better adult outcomes |
| Hattie hinge point (for reference) | Benchmark | 0.40 |

**The honest read:** these effects sit well below Hattie's 0.40 hinge. Financial behaviour is hard to move with any education program. That is a reason to make the teaching concrete and decision-linked, and to judge CEFL by what students can do with real pay stubs, budgets and plans, not to expect large test-score jumps.

## What It Looks Like in Practice

1. **Teach next to a decision.** Each CEFL teaching moment comes right before students use it in their project (pay stub, budget, pathway cost). This is the just-in-time principle applied inside a classroom.
2. **Use real, dated numbers.** Real Alberta minimum wage, real tax, CPP and EI rates, real tuition. Print the year on every figure; rates change every January.
3. **Concrete before abstract.** A worked pay stub before the idea of "deductions"; a real budget before the idea of "financial security."
4. **Practise the behaviour, not just the knowledge.** Building a budget, reading a pay stub, comparing two offers or two pathways. Behaviour effects are small, so give students rehearsals of the actual task.
5. **Put students in contact with real people and workplaces.** Every Career Exploration and Career Opportunities project includes a real conversation, guest, workplace visit, or real job posting (OECD "exploring" indicators).
6. **Count experiences.** Part-time work, volunteering and school roles are evidence-backed career activities. Ask students to audit and plan them.
7. **Check career alignment.** Ask whether a student's education plan actually leads to the career they named (OECD "thinking" indicator). The Post-Secondary Pathways projects do this.
8. **Avoid pseudo-context.** A "real-world" problem with invented numbers that no real person would face is decoration (see `research/three-act-math-research.md`). Use real postings, real prices and real rates.

## What It Doesn't Support

- **Expecting big behaviour changes from a short course.** Behaviour effects average ~0.1 SD.
- **Teaching finance as abstract vocabulary far from any decision.** This is the version most likely to decay.
- **Treating career exploration as a worksheet about careers.** The OECD indicators are about contact and experience, not reading about jobs.
- **Assuming causation from the OECD indicators.** They are strong, controlled associations, not experiments.
- **Treating "financial literacy" as only an individual responsibility.** Fernandes et al. found weaker effects in low-income samples; budgets and goals need to be realistic for every student's situation. Avoid scenarios that assume family wealth.

## Alberta / Canada Angle

- **Curriculum:** Alberta's draft CEFL 7–9 (June 2026) is the first stand-alone career and financial literacy subject for Grades 7–9; in K–6 the content sits in Physical Education and Wellness.
- **Canadian data sources for CEFL materials:**
  - **ALIS / CAREERinsite** (Alberta government): occupation profiles, wages, outlook, education paths.
  - **Statistics Canada:** labour force and wage data.
  - **CRA:** tax brackets, payroll deductions (CPP, EI), T4s.
  - **alberta.ca:** minimum wage, employment standards, the provincial budget.
- **Key 2026 figures used in Grade 7 materials** (re-check each January):
  - Alberta minimum wage $15.00/hour; $13.00/hour for students under 18 working up to 28 hours in a school week or during school breaks.
  - Federal tax: 14% on the first $58,523; basic personal amount $16,452.
  - Alberta tax: 8% on the first $61,200; basic personal amount $22,769.
  - CPP: 5.95% on earnings above the $3,500 yearly exemption; starts the month after an employee turns 18.
  - EI: 1.63% of insurable earnings; deducted at any age.
  - Alberta Budget 2026–27: total expense about $84 billion; health about $34.4 billion; education about $10.8 billion.
- **No Canadian RCT** of school financial education was found in this session; the causal evidence is international.
