Building the Canadian State

Grade 7 Social Studies  ·  Practice Workbook — Answer Key  ·  Alberta Draft Curriculum 2026

Teacher Copy — Not for Students

Learning Outcome: Students examine the deliberate acts of state building.

Guiding Question: How was the Canadian state built after Confederation?

Model answers and marking notes for every question. Each card says which notes section must be taught before the question opens. The student copy shows no section labels: announce which questions are open each session.

For opinion and judgement questions, the model answer is one strong example. Accept any position backed by accurate evidence from the unit.

Part A — Core and Extended Questions

Q1★ Core
Opens after Section 1 — British North America before 1867
AnswerA British Columbia · B Rupert's Land · C Province of Canada · D Nova Scotia · E Newfoundland.
Rupert's Land was not a colony: it was land granted to the Hudson's Bay Company for the fur trade (1670).
Q2★ Core
Opens after Section 6 — Filling the West and new provinces
AnswerManitoba 1870 → British Columbia 1871 → Prince Edward Island 1873 → Alberta 1905 → Newfoundland 1949 → Nunavut 1999.
Q3★ Core
Opens after Section 3 — Expanding west
AnswerAny three: land for farms and settlers; resources (furs, timber, soil); stopping the U.S. from taking the region first; a route to British Columbia (which joined in 1871 on the promise of a railway).
Q4★★ Extended
Opens after Section 6 — The National Policy and the CPR
AnswerThe railway carried settlers west; the settlers grew wheat, which the railway carried east to sell; settlers used the money to buy goods made in eastern factories, which were protected by tariffs. Each part of the National Policy needed the others.
Q5★ Core
Opens after Section 2 — Why the colonies joined
AnswerFor example: "The American Civil War has left the United States with a huge army, and some Americans talk of taking the whole continent. Alone, Nova Scotia is weak; joined with the other colonies we can defend ourselves and build a railway for trade."
Q6★ Core
Opens after Section 4 — The Red River Conflict
AnswerRow 1 effect: Louis Riel and the Métis stop the surveyors, take Upper Fort Garry and form a provisional government.
Row 2 cause: the provisional government bargains with Canada; Parliament passes the Manitoba Act.
Row 3 effect: many Métis lose the land they were promised and move farther west, to the South Saskatchewan River.
Q7★★ Extended
Opens after Section 6 — Settlement
AnswerSupport: it connected BC (kept the 1871 promise); carried settlers west and wheat east; moved troops in 1885.
Left out: Chinese workers who did the most dangerous work in BC, and hundreds died; or First Nations and Métis whose land it crossed.
Q8★★ Extended
Opens after Section 1 — Two empires
AnswerEach war moved the boundaries. In 1713 Britain took over most of Acadia (renamed Nova Scotia); in 1759 it won at the Plains of Abraham and in 1763 France gave New France to Britain. With France gone, the colonies and territories were all British.
Q9★ Core
Opens after Section 5 — Treaties
AnswerA treaty is an agreement between a First Nation and the Crown (later the Canadian government). For example, Treaty 6 (1876) set aside reserves, promised yearly payments and a "medicine chest", and opened land for settlement.
Q10★★ Extended
Opens after Section 4 — Perspectives on Riel
Answera) A: many English-speaking Ontarians or the federal government. B: Métis, many French Canadians.
b) A: he formed a government without Ottawa's approval and led the 1885 fighting at Duck Lake and Batoche. B: surveys ignored Métis river lots, petitions to Ottawa got no answer, and the Manitoba Act's land promises were not kept.
c) Either, if supported by evidence.
Q11★★ Extended
Opens after Section 2 — Was Canada independent?
AnswerIn 1867 Canada became a self-governing Dominion, not a fully independent country. It ran its own affairs at home, but Britain still controlled foreign affairs (wars and treaties); when Britain went to war in 1914, Canada was at war too. Control of foreign policy came in 1931.
Q12★★ Extended
Opens after Section 6 — New provinces
AnswerIn 1870 few settlers lived there and there was no railway. After the CPR (1885) and the free-land campaigns, so many settlers arrived that they needed their own provincial governments, so the two provinces were carved out of the Northwest Territories in 1905.
Q13★★ Extended
Opens after Section 3 — Was the West empty?
AnswerRight: there was farmland for settlers, and the government did offer land. Leaves out: First Nations and Métis peoples, who lived there and had treaties covering the land. Why: the government wanted settlers to come quickly to fill the West and strengthen Canada's claim, so it showed the land as empty.
Q14★★ Extended
Opens after Section 5 — Two views of a treaty
AnswerThe treaty was negotiated through interpreters and written in English by the Crown; First Nations leaders relied on what was said aloud and understood the agreement as sharing the land in peace. Each version is evidence of one side's understanding, so historians compare both to understand what was agreed.

Part B — Challenge Zone

QS1★★ Extended
Opens after Sections 3, 4 and 5
AnswerAccept any ranking with reasons. Example: Rupert's Land first (it gave Canada the whole West; without it, nothing else was possible), treaties second (they opened the land for settlement), Manitoba third. The price: First Nations and Métis peoples, who were not consulted.
QS2★★ Extended
Opens after Sections 2 and 6
AnswerBuilt: he helped plan Confederation (1864–67), took over the West, and created the National Policy (tariffs, the CPR, settlement). Harmed: First Nations (treaties, the Indian Act, hunger on reserves), Métis (scrip, 1885), or Chinese railway workers.
QS3★★ Extended
Opens after All sections
AnswerStrong answers name three or more deliberate acts (the BNA Act, Rupert's Land, the Manitoba Act, treaties, the Indian Act, the National Policy, the CPR, free land, new provinces), explain why the government acted (unity, defence, resources, settlement), and include how First Nations or Métis experienced at least one of them.
