Q4 ★★ Extended

Cluster 3 — Particle model applied to heat · Analyze

A railroad track is laid at 20°C. Each section is 18.6 m long. A 1 cm expansion gap is left between sections. On a July afternoon the track reaches 55°C.

Student claim to evaluate

"We don't need the gaps. The track might get a little longer. But it will just stay in place. No big deal."

(a) Use the particle model. The temperature rises from 20°C to 55°C. Explain what happens to the metal particles in the rail. Be specific about vibration and spacing.

(b) Why would removing the gaps make the track buckle? Use your particle-level explanation. What is the track trying to do, and what happens when it can't?

(c) A new engineer asks a question. Why must the gap be sized for the hottest expected summer temperature? Why not the temperature when it was installed? What would you tell them?

Q5 ★ Core

Cluster 5 — Specific heat capacity · Apply

Two matching cups sit in direct sunlight. One is full of dry sand and one is full of water. Both start at 18°C. After 20 minutes you measure: sand = 45°C, water = 24°C.

(a) Which material has the higher specific heat capacity? How do you know from the data?

(b) Both cups received the same amount of energy from the sun. Use specific heat capacity. Explain why the sand temperature rose so much more than the water temperature.

(c) A student suggests filling a solar collector with sand instead of water: "Sand gets way hotter — it'll store more energy." Is the student right? Explain what specific heat capacity really tells us about storing energy.

Q6 ★★ Extended Alberta

Cluster 6 — Applications · Evaluate

The Red Deer River is downstream from a power plant. For three summers in a row, many fish have died there. The plant pulls in cold river water to cool its equipment. Then it releases the water 8°C warmer. The plant manager says: "Just wait — the river will fix itself in a few weeks."

(a) Warmer water holds less dissolved oxygen. Use the particle model to explain why warm water can't hold as much dissolved gas.

(b) The Red Deer River is a large body of water. Use specific heat capacity to explain why it takes a long time to cool back down. Even if the plant stops releasing warm water tomorrow, the river stays warm for a while. Why?

(c) Judge the plant manager's claim. Name at least two things that affect whether the river ecosystem recovers. Is "just waiting" a good scientific response? Explain.

  • Design A: 10 cm solid concrete
  • Design B: 10 cm fiberglass batt insulation
  • Design C: 10 cm solid wood framing

(a) Use what you know about conduction. Rank the three designs from best to worst at slowing heat loss through the wall. Give reasons for your ranking — don't just list them.

(b) Which mode of heat transfer does fiberglass insulation mainly reduce? How does it do this? What is happening inside the insulation batt?

Q12 ★★ Extended Alberta

Cluster 5 — Specific heat capacity · Evaluate

Vancouver, BC (49°N) has much milder winters and cooler summers than Edmonton, AB (53°N). This is true even though both cities are at similar latitudes.

(a) Use the term specific heat capacity correctly. Explain how the Pacific Ocean helps keep Vancouver's climate mild. Why does the ocean resist big temperature swings?

(b) Edmonton has much bigger temperature swings than Vancouver (−40°C in winter, +35°C in summer). Vancouver rarely sees either extreme. Explain why. Your answer must use the term thermal mass or thermal reservoir.

(c) Prediction: Climate change is warming ocean surface temperatures. It is also making them more variable. Suppose the Pacific Ocean's heat storage became less steady — its surface temperature swung more from season to season. What would happen to Vancouver's climate? Be specific.
