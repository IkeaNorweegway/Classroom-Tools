⚑ Your decision: one card per group, or one per student?
The student lab is written for one mounted card per group, because one lily only gives 3 sepals and 3 petals. Lily stems usually carry 3–5 blooms. If you give each group a whole stem, every student can press and mount their own card, which makes the card a stronger individual assessment piece. You'd need about 30 archival cards, and roughly double the archival paper and blotter.
⚠ Safety
  • Cats: every part of true lilies (Lilium) and daylilies (Hemerocallis) is toxic to cats, including pollen and vase water. A small amount can cause kidney failure. Don't send lily parts home with students who have cats, and consider a note home.
  • Pollen: stains skin, clothing, and paper. Lift it with tape or a dry tissue; don't rub, and don't wet it first. Ask about pollen allergies before Day 1 and have gloves ready.
  • Blades: students use small scissors only. The teacher cuts all ovary sections.
  • No eating. Wash hands at the end of Day 1 and of mount day.

Pressing a Lily: What to Expect

Lilies are fleshy, so they press less neatly than thin garden flowers. That is useful for Q4. Parts are ready when they feel papery and room temperature. Damp parts feel cool. Allow 10–14 days, and up to 3 weeks for thick parts.

ProblemCause and fix
Black or fuzzy spots (mould)Blotter stayed wet. Swap more often, keep the stack in a dry spot away from sinks, and use spare parts to re-press.
Tepals turn brown or see-throughNormal for lilies: they hold a lot of water and dry slowly. Faster, more frequent blotter swaps keep more colour. Photograph the fresh tray on Day 1 so students can compare.
Orange stains on archival paperLoose pollen. Tap the anthers well, or press the stamens on their own small archival slip.
Ovary slice shrivelsExpected. It is mostly water. Keep slices 2–3 mm. The sketch on p4 is the real record of the chambers.
Style stays roundedThick and stiff. It still mounts fine. Splitting it lengthwise helps it lie flat.
Parts crack on mount dayVery dry parts are brittle. Slide a thin paper strip underneath to lift them. Don't use fingers or tweezers on the tepals.

Formative Signals: What to Listen For

If you hear…Then…
"The flower is pretty for us."Expected at the predict step. Don't correct it; Q9 does the refuting with the students' own evidence. Prompt with "Who else visits flowers?"
Pollination and fertilization used as the same thing (Q7)The most common error. If more than a few students do this, reteach the boundary (pollen lands on the stigma vs. pollen joins the ovule) before Exit Ticket 4.
Stamen and pistil swappedAnchor it on the tray: the powdery orange part makes pollen, the one in the middle with the green swelling makes seeds.
"The seeds come from the stem / soil."Send them back to the ovary slice: "Where did you see ovules?"

Answer Key: Student Lab

Page 1: Predict

Accept any prediction with a reason. It gets revisited in Q9. Target idea: the colour, spots, and scent are for pollinators (insects such as bees, butterflies, and moths).

Page 2: From memory (matches Notes Section 4)

SepalProtects the flower while it is still a bud.
PetalAttracts pollinators (colour, pattern, scent).
StamenMale part. The anther makes pollen; the filament holds it up.
PistilFemale part. The stigma receives pollen; the ovary holds ovules, which become seeds.

Observation boxes: pollen is orange-brown, sticky-powdery dust. The stigma feels sticky or wet. The ovary slice shows 3 chambers, with pale, bead-like ovules in rows (usually 2 per chamber in one slice).

1.How did you decide which tepals were sepals?
They are the outer ring: attached lower and further out, and they covered the outside of the bud. They are often a little narrower, sometimes with a greener tip or ridge.
Full marks: at least one observable piece of evidence tied to position. "They looked different" alone is not enough.
2.How the stigma felt, and why that helps.
Sticky. Pollen grains brushed off a pollinator stick to it and stay there, so pollen is caught and held where it is needed.
3.Estimate the seeds; what must happen first?
Accept any reasoned estimate built from the count. For example, ovules per slice × about how many slices fit along the ovary. A real lily ovary holds dozens to a few hundred ovules. First: pollination (lily pollen reaches the stigma), then fertilization (pollen joins with the ovule).
Full marks: shows how the estimate was made and names both steps.
4.Which part will press best and which worst?
Best: thin parts with little water, such as the leaf, a filament, or the thin edge of a tepal. Hardest: the ovary or pistil (thick and full of water). The fleshy base of the tepals is hard too.
Look for: reasoning that links thickness and water content to how easily a part dries flat.
5.Why swap the blotter?
The blotter soaks up water until it is full. A wet blotter can't pull out any more, and it keeps the parts damp, so they brown or go mouldy. Dry blotter keeps drawing water out.
6.Why acid-free paper?
Acid slowly breaks down paper and the specimen: it yellows, turns brittle, and can damage the plant. Acid-free paper keeps the specimen whole for a very long time, so future scientists can still study it. For example, they can compare plants across decades to see how species or ranges have changed.
Full marks: links the acid to damage over time, and the damage to losing a scientific record.
7.Trace a pollen grain.
Anther makes pollen → pollen sticks to the bee → bee visits another lily → pollen rubs onto the sticky stigma. Pollination ends here: pollen has landed on the stigma. → pollen grows a tube down the style → reaches an ovule inside the ovary → joins with the ovule. This is fertilization. → the ovule becomes a seed and the ovary becomes the seed pod (fruit).
Scope: "pollen tube" and "sperm/egg" are not required at this level. Accept "pollen travels down the style to the ovule." The key is putting the boundary in the right place.
8.Which part makes the most glucose, and where did the flower's energy come from?
The leaf (green, with chloroplasts). Accept the green ovary as a minor second. The leaves used photosynthesis (light + water + carbon dioxide → glucose + oxygen) to make glucose. The phloem carried that glucose to the flower to build it. The coloured tepals do little or no photosynthesis.
Interleaves LO 7-2-03. Watch for "the flower got energy from the soil", which is the unit's core misconception resurfacing.
9."Lilies are big and colourful so people will buy them."
Disagree. Evidence from the dissection: bright colour and spots that pollinators can see (spots lead toward the nectar); strong scent (many Oriental lilies are moth-pollinated and smell strongest at night); nectar grooves at the base of each tepal as a reward; anthers held out on long filaments where a visitor brushes them; sticky stigma in the visitor's path. Wild lilies had these traits long before people sold flowers.
Full marks: two pieces of dissection evidence, each linked to pollinators. Accept "partly agree" if the student separates the original purpose (pollinators) from what breeders later exaggerated.
★ Selective breeding
People probably made bigger or brighter: bloom size, new colours, more buds per stem, longer vase life, even pollen-free varieties. There first for pollinators: colour, spots, scent, nectar, and the position of the stamens and stigma.
Interleaves LO 7-2-05 (selective breeding = people choosing which plants reproduce).

★ Display idea
Mounted cards make a strong class "herbarium" wall or binder. If you keep them, store them flat in a folder away from light, and the colours will last years. Label each card with the date, so next year's class can see how the specimens held up.
