Each region, its own economy

An economy is the way a place makes, buys and sells goods and services. After 1867 Canada's economy changed fast. The country expanded west, built new industries (businesses that make things), and traded more with other countries.

Different parts of Canada built different economies, because each region had different natural resources: useful things found in nature, like soil, trees, fish and minerals.

When Canada took over the West, millions of acres of prairie became available for agriculture (farming). Farmers grew wheat, which could be sold around the world. Later, in the 1900s, Alberta's economy came to depend on   and gas.

Macdonald's plan

In 1879 Prime Minister Sir John A. Macdonald brought in the National Policy. It had three parts that worked together:

  1. Tariffs: taxes on   goods, so Canadian-made goods would be cheaper by comparison and people would buy Canadian.
  2. A railway across the country to connect the regions.
  3. Settlement of the West, so farmers would grow wheat and buy goods from eastern factories.
Misconception

"The National Policy helped every region of Canada equally."

Why this fails: Tariffs made imported goods more expensive. Prairie farmers had to buy machinery, tools and clothes at higher prices, mostly from protected factories in Ontario and Quebec, while selling their wheat at world prices they could not control. Many farmers felt the plan was built for the East.

Correct understanding: The National Policy helped some regions more than others. Factories in Central Canada gained most; many Prairie farmers and Maritimers paid   prices.

Section 3  ·  The Railway That Tied Canada Together

Learning target: I can explain how the Canadian Pacific Railway supported economic growth.

Vocabulary

Canadian Pacific Railway  
Transportation  
Market  
Trade  

Connecting the regions

The Canadian Pacific Railway (CPR) was finished on November 7, 1885, when the "last spike" was driven at Craigellachie, British Columbia. The government helped pay for it with money and about 25 million acres of land.

The railway changed the economy in three ways:

  • Moving goods. Wheat could travel from the Prairies to ports, and then to buyers around the world. Factory goods could travel west.
  • Moving people. Settlers could reach the Prairies in days instead of months.
  • Connecting markets. A market is a place where people buy and sell. The CPR turned the whole country into one connected market.

Towns grew wherever the railway stopped. Calgary, for example, grew quickly after the CPR arrived in 1883.

Learning target: I can compare the opportunities and challenges of tariffs and free trade.

Vocabulary

TermDefinition in your own wordsOne example
Free trade

Producer  
Misconception

"A tariff is a fee the other country pays."

Why this fails: A tariff is collected when goods enter Canada. The importer pays it, and the cost is usually passed on to Canadian buyers as a higher price.

Correct understanding: Tariffs are usually paid, in the end, by   in the country that charges them, through higher prices.

Worked example: how a tariff changes a choice

A plough made in the United States costs $100. A plough made in Ontario costs $120. Canada adds a 30% tariff on imported ploughs.

  1. Find the tariff: 30% of $100 = $30.
  2. Add it on: $100 + $30 = $130 for the American plough.
  3. Compare: the Ontario plough ($120) is now cheaper, so more farmers buy Canadian.

Who wins? The Ontario factory and its workers. Who pays? The farmer, who pays $120 instead of $100.

Free trade

Free trade means trading with no tariffs. Consumers (buyers) get more choice and lower prices, and producers can sell to bigger markets. But some local businesses may close if they can't compete with cheaper imports.

Canadians argued about this for a long time. In the 1911 election, Prime Minister Wilfrid Laurier proposed freer trade with the United States, called reciprocity. He   the election, partly because many voters feared it would hurt Canadian factories and tie Canada too closely to the U.S. Canada and the U.S. finally signed a free trade agreement in 1988.

Vocabulary

TermDefinition in your own wordsOne example
Gross domestic product (GDP)  
Equalization payments  
Inequality  
Misconception

"GDP is how much money a country has saved."

Why this fails: GDP counts what a country produces in one year, not what it has in the bank. A country can have a big GDP and still owe a lot of money.

Correct understanding: Gross domestic product is the total value of all the goods and services produced in a country in one  .

Worked example: a tiny GDP

A small town in one year grows $5,000 of wheat, builds $3,000 of furniture and gives $2,000 of haircuts.

GDP = $5,000 + $3,000 + $2,000 = $10,000.

If the town grows more wheat next year, its GDP goes up. Canada's real GDP is about $3 trillion a year.

Sharing between regions

Some provinces have more valuable resources or bigger industries, so they collect more tax money. That creates inequality between regions.

Misconception

"Equalization means Ottawa gives every province the same amount of money."

Why this fails: Provinces that can raise plenty of money on their own get nothing. Only provinces below the national average get payments, and the amounts differ.

Correct understanding: Equalization payments come from federal taxes paid by Canadians everywhere and go to provinces that can raise less money on their own, so every province can offer similar services, like schools and hospitals.

Equalization began in 1957, and since 1982 it has been written into Canada's Constitution. Alberta has not received equalization payments in decades, because its oil and gas give it a strong economy. Many Albertans debate whether the system is fair, which connects to the regional tensions you saw in Section 2.

What banks do

Banks help people and businesses manage money. They keep savings safe, make loans, and help people invest: put money into something that can grow. A farmer might borrow to buy land; a factory might borrow to build a new building.

Some of Canada's banks are older than Canada itself. The Bank of Montreal opened in 1817 and the Bank of Nova Scotia in 1832. The Royal Bank of Canada, the Canadian Imperial Bank of Commerce, the National Bank of Canada and the Laurentian Bank also grew from banks started in the 1800s.

GDP and banking: a two-way street

When the economy produces more, people and businesses need more banking: more loans, savings and payments. And when banks lend money, businesses can produce more, so GDP grows. That's why we say GDP and banking have a reciprocal (two-way) relationship.

The Bank of Canada

Misconception

"The Bank of Canada is a bank where people open accounts."

Why this fails: You can't open a chequing account at the Bank of Canada. It is the government's central bank.

Correct understanding: The Bank of Canada, created in the 1930s, looks after the whole money system: it issues Canada's banknotes and works to keep the banking system and prices stable.

The Bank of Canada was created in 1934, during the Great Depression, when many people lost trust in money and banks. Look at any Canadian banknote: it says "Bank of Canada."
