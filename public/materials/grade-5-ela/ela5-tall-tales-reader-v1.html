How to use this reader

These five tales are the texts our class reads together. Each one is about a hero who is bigger, stronger, faster or louder than any real person could be. Tall tales were first told out loud, around campfires and in logging camps, and every teller made the hero a little bigger. The versions here are new retellings written for this class.

Paul Bunyan and the Winter of the Blue Snow

Tale 1 · A logging-camp tale

1Paul Bunyan was born in the woods of Maine, or maybe in Quebec, depending on who is telling the story. Everyone agrees on one thing: he was big. On his first day, he was too heavy for the cradle, so his parents rocked him in a lumber wagon pulled by twelve horses. By the time he was a week old, he was wearing his father's boots. By the time he was a year old, his father was wearing his old boots as slippers, and they were still too big.

2When Paul grew up, he became the greatest logger who ever swung an axe. He could chop down a whole row of pine trees with one swing. He combed his beard with a pine tree, and his voice was so loud that when he called his workers to breakfast, the trees in the next valley fell over.

3Then came the Winter of the Blue Snow. It started on a Tuesday, when the sky turned the colour of a robin's egg and blue flakes as big as dinner plates came floating down. Nobody had ever seen blue snow before. Then the cold came. It was so cold that the flames in the campfires froze solid, and the cook had to break them into pieces and pass them around like candy. It was so cold that when the loggers talked, their words froze in the air and fell to the ground in icy chunks. A logger would say "Good morning!" and you would have to pick it up and carry it inside to find out what he said.

4Out in a snowdrift, Paul heard something whimpering. He dug down with his bare hands and found a baby ox, shivering and turned completely blue from the cold. Paul tucked the calf inside his coat and carried it back to camp. He named her Babe. The blue never washed out, but Babe grew so fast that if you watched her for an hour, you could see her get bigger. By spring she measured forty-two axe handles between the eyes.

5The blue snow kept falling, and the camp had a new problem: the loggers were starving. Hungry loggers cannot work, and Paul had a whole forest to cut. His cook, Sourdough Sam, could not make pancakes fast enough. So Paul built a griddle so wide that you could not see across it on a foggy day. To grease it, Sam strapped slabs of bacon to the boots of fifty boys and sent them skating back and forth across the hot iron. The pancakes came off the griddle by the wagonload, and Babe hauled them to the men.

6With full stomachs, the loggers went back to work. Babe pulled whole forests of logs to the river at once. Paul straightened a crooked logging road by hitching Babe to one end and having her pull until all the curves came out. The camp cut more wood that winter than any camp in history.

7Finally spring came. The sun warmed the camp, and every frozen word that had fallen all winter began to thaw at once. For three days the camp was the noisiest place on earth. "Good morning!" and "Pass the syrup!" and "Watch out for that tree!" all came bursting out together. The loggers had to stuff moss in their ears until the last words melted away.

8People say Babe's hoofprints filled with rain and became the ten thousand lakes of Minnesota. Others say that is just a tall tale. But if you are ever in the north woods and you hear a strange shout with nobody around, it might be one last frozen word from the Winter of the Blue Snow, finally melting.

Words to know

griddle a flat iron pan for cooking pancakes · axe handle an old way of measuring, about 90 cm · whimpering making small, sad crying sounds

Sally Ann Thunder Ann Whirlwind

Tale 2 · A frontier tale

1Sally Ann Thunder Ann Whirlwind could laugh the bark off a pine tree. Folks along the river said she could wade the Mississippi without getting her knees wet, carry a steamboat on her back, and grin a hungry bear right out of the woods. When she was born, she was already talking, and the first thing she said was, "Let me at it!"

2Sally Ann did not have time for fancy clothes. She wore a hornets' nest for a bonnet, with the hornets still inside, and she said they kept her thinking fast. For a belt she used a rattlesnake, and when the snake complained, she told it to hush. "A belt has one job," she said, "and talking isn't it."

3One spring morning, Sally Ann was walking through the forest when she heard someone calling for help. "Help! Somebody! Anybody!" She followed the voice and found a young man named Davy Crockett stuck in the top of a tree. He had climbed up to get a better look at the sunrise, and his head had wedged between two branches. He had been stuck there for a whole day.

4Most people would have run for a ladder. Sally Ann looked at the tree, looked at Davy, and laughed so hard that the branches shook. "Hold still," she called. She grabbed a handful of rattlesnakes from a nearby rock, tied them together into a long rope, and threw one end up into the branches. Then she pulled. The tree bent like a fishing rod, and Davy popped loose like a cork from a bottle. He landed in a pile of leaves.

5Davy stood up and brushed himself off. He had told stories about wrestling bears and riding lightning, but he had never met anyone like Sally Ann. "Ma'am," he said, "you are the bravest, strongest, most surprising person I ever met." Sally Ann said, "Well, you're the stuck-est," and she laughed again. They were married that summer.

6People in the settlement thought Sally Ann would settle down after the wedding. She did not. That winter a huge grizzly bear came into the settlement and stole a whole barrel of honey. Everyone hid in their cabins. Sally Ann walked straight out into the snow. The bear stood up on its back legs and roared so loud that icicles fell off the roofs. Sally Ann did not roar back. She just smiled her widest smile, the one that could light a room at midnight. The bear looked at that smile, dropped the honey, and ran so fast it left its shadow behind.

7"How did you do it?" Davy asked her.

8"That bear was only hungry," Sally Ann said. "I was hungry and determined. Determined wins every time."

9To this day, some people say that when lightning flashes over the river on a summer night, it is just Sally Ann Thunder Ann Whirlwind, grinning at the storm to show it who is boss.

Words to know

settlement a small new town · determined not giving up, no matter what · bonnet an old-fashioned hat tied under the chin

John Henry, the Steel-Driving Man

Tale 3 · A railroad workers' tale

1In the 1870s, railroad companies were racing to lay tracks across the United States. When a mountain stood in the way, workers had to dig a tunnel straight through it. They did it by hand. One worker held a long steel spike against the rock. Another swung a heavy hammer down on it, again and again, to drive a hole deep into the stone. Then the holes were filled with explosives to blast the rock away. This work was called steel driving. It was hard and dangerous, and many of the men who did it were Black workers who had been born into slavery and were now free, working for low pay.

2The strongest steel driver of them all was John Henry. The songs say that when John Henry was a baby, he picked up a hammer from the floor, held it in his tiny hand, and said, "This hammer is going to be the death of me." Everyone laughed, because babies don't talk like that. But John Henry grew up big and strong, and that hammer stayed in his hand.

3When John Henry swung his twenty-pound hammer, it whistled through the air like the wind before a storm. He could drive steel faster than any two men together. He sang while he worked, and the other workers kept their rhythm to his song. His partner, a young man named Li'l Bill, held the steel spike. Li'l Bill trusted John Henry completely, because one missed swing could crush a hand.

4One day, a salesman came to the Big Bend Tunnel in West Virginia with a new machine: a steam drill. It huffed and puffed and pounded the rock all by itself. "This drill can do the work of ten men," the salesman told the boss. "You won't need your steel drivers anymore."

5The workers looked at each other. If the machine took their jobs, how would they feed their families? John Henry stepped forward. "A man ain't nothing but a man," he said. "But before I let that steam drill beat me down, I'll die with this hammer in my hand." He challenged the machine to a race. Whoever drilled deeper into the mountain by sundown would win.

6The race began at sunrise. The steam drill roared and shook the ground. John Henry swung his hammers, one in each hand, and the sound rang out like church bells. Sparks flew. Dust filled the tunnel. Li'l Bill turned the steel after every strike. At noon, the machine was ahead. John Henry did not stop for water. He sang louder and swung harder. By mid-afternoon, they were even.

7When the sun went down, the boss measured the holes. The steam drill had driven nine feet. John Henry had driven fourteen. The workers cheered so loudly that the mountain echoed. John Henry had won.

8But as the crowd cheered, John Henry laid down his hammer and lay down on the ground. His heart had given everything it had. He died that night with his hammer in his hand, just as the song said he would.

9The railroad companies kept buying steam drills, and the machines did take over the work in the end. But the workers kept singing about John Henry. They sang about him in the tunnels, in the cotton fields and on the docks. For more than a hundred and fifty years, people have told his story to remember that a person's strength, courage and dignity matter, even when a machine can work faster.

Words to know

steel driving hammering a steel spike into rock to make a hole · dignity being treated, and treating yourself, as someone who matters · challenge to invite someone to a contest

Big Joe Mufferaw

Tale 4 · An Ottawa Valley tale

1Some tall-tale heroes were never real. Big Joe Mufferaw was. His real name was Joseph Montferrand, and he was born in Montreal in 1802. He grew up to be a timber raftsman on the Ottawa River, which runs between what is now Ontario and Quebec. In those days, huge rafts of logs were floated down the river to the sawmills, and the raftsmen rode on top, steering them through the rapids. It was hard, dangerous work, and Joe was the best at it.

2Joe was tall, strong and quick. People said he was the most powerful man in the Ottawa Valley, and that he always stood up for raftsmen who were being bullied. That much is probably true. But every time someone told a story about Joe, the story grew a little bigger.

3The first stories said Joe could lift a loaded wagon. A few years later, people said he could lift a loaded wagon with the horses still attached. A few years after that, they said he could lift the wagon, the horses and the driver, and hold them up while he drank a cup of tea. The raftsmen told these stories on long nights by the fire, and each teller tried to beat the last one.

4One story is famous all over the Valley. One night Joe walked into an inn in Bytown, the town that would later become Ottawa. The men there did not believe he was as strong as people said. So Joe took a running start, jumped, flipped in the air, and left the marks of his boots on the ceiling. Some people say you could still see those boot prints for years afterwards. Was it true? Nobody alive can say for sure, but the story was told so often that it became part of the Valley.

5The tales kept growing. People said Big Joe paddled his canoe from Mattawa to Ottawa, a trip of more than three hundred kilometres, in a single afternoon, and arrived before his own shadow. They said he once put out a forest fire by spitting on it. They said that when he sneezed, the leaves blew off the trees in the next county, and that is why autumn comes early in the Ottawa Valley.

6The French-speaking raftsmen called him Jos Montferrand. The English-speaking raftsmen could not quite say it, so they called him Joe Mufferaw. Both groups told stories about him, and both were proud of him. The stories crossed from one language to the other, just as the logs went down the river.

7Joseph Montferrand died in 1864, but Big Joe Mufferaw never really did. His stories have been told in English and in French, in kitchens and lumber camps, and in songs people still sing. A real man became a legend because people kept telling his story, and every teller made it just a little bit taller.

Words to know

raftsman a worker who rode and steered rafts of logs down a river · legend a story about the past that many people tell, which may or may not be true · rapids a fast, rough part of a river

Chinook Charlie and the Great Thaw

Tale 5 · A new Alberta tale (our mentor text)

1Everyone in southern Alberta knows that the winters can be bitter, but nobody remembers a winter like the Great Freeze of long ago. That January, it was so cold that the cows gave ice cream instead of milk. It was so cold that people's shadows froze to the sidewalk, and they had to chip them loose with a spoon before they could go inside. The only person in the whole province who was not shivering was a ranch hand named Chinook Charlie.

2Charlie was taller than a grain elevator and skinnier than a fence post. His hat was so wide that a family of magpies lived on the brim. But the most amazing thing about Charlie was his breath. When he breathed out, it was as warm as a summer afternoon. When he whistled, the frost melted off every window for a mile.

3On the coldest day of the Great Freeze, the townspeople came knocking at Charlie's cabin. Their teeth were chattering so hard that they sounded like a hundred typewriters. "Charlie," said Mrs. Okafor, the mayor, "the river is frozen right down to the bottom. The cattle can't drink, the school bus is stuck in an icicle, and the hockey game has been cancelled because the puck froze to the ice. You have to do something!"

4Charlie scratched his head. A warm breath now and then would not be enough for a whole province. He needed one giant breath. So he walked west, toward the Rocky Mountains. He walked for two days. His boots crunched on the snow like he was walking on a mountain of cereal.

5At the top of the highest mountain, Charlie took the biggest breath of his life. He breathed in so much air that his chest swelled up like a hot-air balloon, and his hat blew all the way to Saskatchewan. Then he turned toward the frozen prairie and blew.

6A warm wind roared down the mountains. It howled across the foothills and raced over the fields. In ten minutes, the temperature jumped forty degrees. Snowmen melted into puddles before they could say goodbye. Icicles dripped like taps. The frozen river cracked, groaned and began to flow, and the cows lined up for a drink. People ran outside in their T-shirts, shouting and dancing in the mud.

7Charlie was so tired that he slept for a week on top of the mountain, snoring softly. Every snore sent another warm breeze down to the prairie.

8That is why, even today, when a warm wind suddenly blows down from the Rockies in the middle of winter, Albertans call it a Chinook. If you look west and see a long arch of clear sky above the mountains, that is Charlie's breath, still warming the prairie. And if your shadow ever freezes to the sidewalk, now you know who to call.

Words to know

Chinook a warm, dry wind that blows down from the Rocky Mountains in winter (a real Alberta weather event) · ranch hand a worker on a cattle ranch · foothills the hills at the bottom of the mountains
