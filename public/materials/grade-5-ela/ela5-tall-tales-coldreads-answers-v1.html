Cold Reads

Tall Tales · Two new tall tales you have not read before. Read the tale, then answer the questions. Use paragraph numbers in your answers.
Edition A
Answer key. Model answers show one strong response; accept others that are supported by the text.
How to give and score the cold reads

Cold read 1 goes in Lesson 14 (mid-unit), cold read 2 in Lesson 27 (end of unit). Each takes about 35 minutes. Students read silently; Edition C students hear the tale read aloud (an access support on these comprehension outcomes) and then reread the passage themselves. Do not pre-teach the passages. Score each question with the points shown; the codes say which outcome each question gives evidence for. Record by code, not by total: a student can be strong on CO4 and not yet on VO14. Use the results to set work-time groups for the next week.

Cold read 1 · Mighty Marguerite and the Peace River Mosquitoes

Mighty Marguerite and the Peace River Mosquitoes

A new tall tale from northern Alberta

1Up in the Peace River country of northern Alberta, the summers are short, the days are long, and the mosquitoes are famous. But no summer was ever as buzzing as the Summer of the Giant Mosquitoes. That year the mosquitoes grew as big as crows. They carried off picnic baskets. They drained a whole pond in one afternoon. One even tried to fly away with the town's fire truck.

2The only person who wasn't afraid of them was Mighty Marguerite. Marguerite ran the biggest farm in the valley, and she could plough a field faster than a tractor by pulling the plough herself. She once sneezed during harvest and blew a whole field of wheat into neat little stacks. When people asked how she got so strong, she just said, "I eat my porridge, and I don't complain."

3One July morning, the mayor came running up Marguerite's lane, swatting at the air. "Marguerite! The mosquitoes have surrounded the school. The children can't get out for recess, and the teachers are hiding under their desks!"

4Marguerite put down the cow she was carrying and wiped her hands on her overalls. "Well," she said, "I suppose somebody has to do something."

5She thought for a minute. Then she marched to her barn and pulled out the biggest cooking pot in Alberta, a pot so large that she used it as a swimming pool on hot days. She filled it with sweet-smelling berry jam, enough for a thousand breakfasts, and set it in the middle of an empty field.

6The mosquitoes smelled the jam from fifty kilometres away. They came in a cloud so thick that it blocked out the sun. Down they dove, straight into the pot, and there they stuck, buzzing and kicking in the sticky jam. Before they could pull themselves out, Marguerite clapped a giant lid on top, and that was that.

7The children ran out for recess, cheering. The teachers came out from under their desks. The mayor declared that every July the town would hold Marguerite Day, with free jam for everyone.

8As for the mosquitoes, Marguerite let them go in the fall, far out over the muskeg, after making them promise to stay small. They mostly kept their promise. But if a mosquito ever bites you in the Peace River country and it seems a little bigger than usual, it is probably one of their great-great-grandchildren, still holding a grudge.

1. What makes this story a tall tale? Give two features, each with an example from the text and its paragraph number. (TF7) [2 points]

Answer Any two with examples: a hero with impossible strength (Marguerite pulls the plough herself, ¶2; carries a cow, ¶4); exaggeration (mosquitoes as big as crows, ¶1); a real place (Peace River country, ¶1); explains something in nature (why Peace River mosquitoes are big, ¶8).

2. Fill in the story map. (TF3) [3 points]

ProblemMain event that solves itEnding
Giant mosquitoes surround the school; children and teachers are trappedMarguerite fills a giant pot with jam, the mosquitoes get stuck, and she puts a lid onThe children go out for recess; the town holds Marguerite Day; she lets the mosquitoes go

3. Write a gist sentence for the story: 20 words or fewer. (CO4) [2 points]

Answer e.g., Mighty Marguerite traps giant mosquitoes in a pot of jam to free the school, and the town celebrates her. (2 = who + problem/solution + end, own words, ≤ 20 words; 1 = missing one part or too long.)

4. Marguerite says, "I suppose somebody has to do something" (¶4). What does this show about her? Use evidence. (TF10, CO9) [2 points]

Answer She is calm and responsible: she doesn't panic like the mayor, she just gets to work. Evidence: she puts down the cow and wipes her hands (¶4), then marches to the barn with a plan (¶5).

5. "They came in a cloud so thick that it blocked out the sun" (¶6). What does this exaggeration help the reader understand? (VO14) [2 points]

Answer How huge and frightening the swarm was: there were so many mosquitoes that it looked like a storm.

6. Why does Marguerite choose jam? The text does not say. Make an inference and explain your thinking. (CO6) [2 points]

Answer Mosquitoes are attracted to sweet smells, and jam is sticky, so it would both attract them and trap them. Clue: "The mosquitoes smelled the jam from fifty kilometres away… there they stuck" (¶6).

7. How do the mayor and Marguerite react differently to the mosquitoes? (CO12) [2 points]

Answer The mayor panics: he comes "running… swatting at the air" (¶3). Marguerite stays calm and makes a plan (¶4–5).

Cold read 2 · Grandma Kowalchuk and the Blizzard Bread

Grandma Kowalchuk and the Blizzard Bread

A new tall tale from the Alberta prairie

1Everyone on the prairie east of Edmonton knew Grandma Kowalchuk's bread. Her loaves were so light that you had to tie them to the table to keep them from floating away, and so delicious that the crows lined up outside her window every morning with their beaks open. But the most famous loaf she ever baked was the one that saved the town of Willow Coulee.

2It happened during the Blizzard of the Century. The snow came down for nine days without stopping. It piled up past the windows, then past the roofs, then past the top of the grain elevator. The roads disappeared. The trains stopped. The town store ran out of flour, then milk, then everything else. People started eating their own mittens.

3"We have to get food from the city," said Mr. Dubois, who ran the store. "But nobody can drive through snow this deep."

4Grandma Kowalchuk listened. She was only as tall as a kitchen chair, but she had arms like fence posts from fifty years of kneading dough. "Nobody can drive through it," she said slowly, "but somebody could bake through it."

5The townspeople looked at each other. Was she joking?

6She was not. Grandma rolled up her sleeves and mixed the biggest batch of dough in Alberta history. She used her bathtub as a bowl and a hockey stick as a spoon. She set the dough by the stove, and it began to rise. It rose out of the bathtub. It rose out of the house. It rose up and over the snowbanks, and then it kept going, stretching across the prairie in a long, warm, golden road.

7Grandma lit a fire underneath it, and the whole road baked to a perfect crust. It was as hard as pavement on top and soft as a pillow inside, and it stretched all the way to Edmonton.

8Mr. Dubois drove the store truck along the bread road and came back with flour, milk, apples and five hundred new mittens. The town was saved. That night everyone ate warm bread around Grandma's table, and the crows got the crusts.

9When spring came, the bread road softened and sank into the fields. People say that is why the soil east of Edmonton grows such good wheat. And on cold winter mornings, if you stand very still, you can still smell fresh bread on the prairie wind.

1. Write a summary using Somebody · Wanted · But · So · Then. (CO4) [3 points]

SomebodyWantedButSoThen
Grandma Kowalchukto get food to the snowed-in townnobody could drive through the deep snowshe baked a giant loaf that became a bread road to Edmontonthe truck brought food and the town was saved

2. Describe Grandma Kowalchuk using two different ways an author shows a character (says, thinks, does, what others say or think). (TF10, CO11) [2 points]

Answer Says: "somebody could bake through it" (¶4): clever and confident. Does: mixes dough in a bathtub with a hockey stick (¶6): determined and inventive. Others: the townspeople wonder if she is joking (¶5): she surprises people.

3. Find a comparison (simile) in paragraph 7. What does it tell you about the bread road? (VO13) [2 points]

Answer "As hard as pavement on top and soft as a pillow inside": the road was strong enough to drive on but still soft, real bread.

4. "People started eating their own mittens" (¶2). What is the author showing instead of telling? (WR15, CO6) [2 points]

Answer That the town was desperately hungry and out of food.

5. Why do you think the townspeople wondered if Grandma was joking (¶5)? (CO6, CO9) [2 points]

Answer Because "baking through" snow sounds impossible; she is small ("only as tall as a kitchen chair," ¶4), and nobody had ever done anything like it.

6. Compare Grandma Kowalchuk with one hero from our reader. How are they alike and how are they different? (CO12, TF10) [3 points]

Answer e.g., Like Paul Bunyan, she solves a winter problem by feeding people with giant cooking (the griddle / the bread). Different: Paul is a giant; Grandma is tiny, and her power is her skill and cleverness, not her size.

7. Tall tales often explain something in nature. What does this tale explain? (TF7) [1 point]

Answer Why the soil east of Edmonton grows such good wheat (¶9).
