Tall Tales — Teacher Guide

Grade 5 ELA · Unit 1 · 30 lessons of 60 minutes (6 weeks) · Anchor topic: North American tall tales · SRSD strategy: POW + WWW, What = 2, How = 2
For the teacher

At a glance

Students read five tall tales (three retold folk heroes, one real Canadian raftsman who became a legend, and an original Alberta tale), learn how stories are built and how characters are shown, and use the SRSD strategy POW + WWW to plan, draft, revise and publish their own tall tale. The unit ends with a storytelling festival for a younger class, which is the real audience for the writing.

ReadingStory structure, character evidence, exaggeration and comparisons, summarizing, inference, theme, context and perspective, oral tradition
WritingSRSD: background → discuss → model → memorize → support → independent. Show don't tell, dialogue, exaggeration that shows
Word workGrade 5 affixes (-ous, -ious, -al, -ial, -ian, -ic, -ical, -ment, -ity, trans-, sub-, ex-, com-, -ant/-ent, -ance/-ence), 8 Tier 2 words, spelling from memory with WTW sorts, sentence combining
FluencyReader's theatre (Paul Bunyan and the Blue Snow), repeated reading, partner reading
Oral languageRetelling aloud, structured discussion, storytelling festival
AssessmentSix exit tickets; cold reads in Lessons 14 and 27; writing rubric with three samples; self-assessment graph

Why it is built this way (research/literacy-reading-writing-research.md): SRSD has the largest effect of any writing approach (1.17, §8); writing about reading every lesson (0.40; summaries 0.79 at elementary, §9); explicit strategy teaching kept brief and then applied (§3); one topic for the shared texts (§2); repeated reading with feedback and reader's theatre for fluency (§5); explicit spelling with spelling from memory (§7); grammar inside sentences (§10).

Copyright: every text is new. Paul Bunyan, Sally Ann Thunder Ann Whirlwind and John Henry are public-domain folk heroes retold in our own words; the line "A man ain't nothing but a man" is from the traditional ballad. Big Joe Mufferaw is based on the life and legends of Joseph Montferrand (1802–1864). Chinook Charlie, Mighty Marguerite and Grandma Kowalchuk are original. Do not add published picture books or song lyrics to the files (public repo); you can still read them aloud in class.

The daily block

MinPart
0–5Warm-up: retrieval from word work and earlier lessons, no notes
5–17Mini-lesson (anchor note + mentor text + think-aloud)
17–52Work time: reading and the notebook page; you run two groups and quick conferences
52–60Exit ticket or notebook "write about it"

Groups by need (set from the September screen, then from exit tickets and cold reads):

  • Decoding: students who misread long words. Long-word routine (circle prefix and suffix, underline vowels, say parts, say the word) with words from the tale, then read a paragraph. 3 times a week.
  • Fluency: accurate but slow or flat readers. Repeated reading from the fluency pack, then F&P leveled passages at about 95% accuracy. 3–5 times a week, 10 minutes.
  • Comprehension: students who decode well but miss meaning. Reread the day's grade-level tale with you; stop to summarize and find evidence.
  • On track: extension (compare two tales; write an extra exaggeration ladder) or writing conferences.

F&P and Words Their Way: keep guided reading groups, but group by need; use the reader's grade-level tales in mini-lessons and leveled books for practice and independent reading. Keep the WTW sort cycle; the word-work booklet adds explicit teaching, spelling from memory and morphology.

The editions

All three editions use the same tales, the same lesson numbers and the same question numbers, so a student can move between them and the Edition A answer key works for all three.

EditionWhoWhat's differentScaffold type
AMost studentsCore and stretch items—
B (scaffolded)Can think at grade level but needs support to show itWord box per anchor page; a filled model row (✎) in each chart; sentence starters for every written answer; a story frame on the draft pageWord box and starters are cognitive scaffolds: fade them. Week 1–2: full; Week 3–4: fold the word box under and cross out starters you no longer need; Week 5–6: Edition A notes, starters only on request
C (easy read)Reading well below Grade 5 (F&P Grade 1–3 range)Easy-read layout (short sentences, support strip, line height 2); retold tales for decoding and fluency; grade-level tale read aloud for comprehension; circle / draw / dictate optionsRead-aloud and dictation are access scaffolds on comprehension and composing outcomes (keep them). They are not used on fluency or spelling tasks.

Choose by task, not by child. A student can use Edition A for writing and Edition C for reading. Quick screen: read a grade-level tale aloud and ask three questions. Good listening comprehension + weak reading = decoding or fluency gap (Edition C reader + fluency group, Edition A or B for comprehension work). Weak either way = language or knowledge gap (Edition B, comprehension group, extra vocabulary).

Track B (modified outcomes) is only for a student whose IPP team has set modified outcomes; these editions keep the Grade 5 outcomes.

Error patterns to watch (course context): "a tall tale is a lie"; "the main idea is the topic"; "theme is one word"; "exaggeration = bigger numbers"; "revising = fixing spelling"; "longer is better"; "fluent = fast"; all dialogue in one paragraph; tense slipping into the present.

Week 1 · Meet the genre

1Hook: true or tall?TF7, OL2

ShowNo materials yet. Tell (don't read) the opening of Paul Bunyan (reader ¶1–3) as a storyteller would: straight face, slowing down for the frozen words.

Say"Is that true? How do you know? Who would tell a story like that, and why?"

DoNotebook L1: brain dump (5 min, no stakes), then the true/tall sort with a partner, then two predictions about what every tall tale has.

GroupsNone today; circulate and read the brain dumps. Note who says "a tall tale is a lie".

CheckCollect brain dumps; list the three most common ideas for tomorrow's warm-up.

2What makes a tall taleTF7, TF3

ShowAnchor note 1. Reader Tale 1 ¶1–4 on the board.

SayModel one feature: "In ¶2 he combs his beard with a pine tree. That's impossible, so it's exaggeration. I'll write ¶2."

DoShared reading of ¶1–4 (you read, students follow). Notebook L2 feature hunt with partners; "write about it" alone.

GroupsDecoding group: long-word routine with whimpering, completely, measured.

CheckStudents check their L1 predictions. Address the "tall tale = lie" error with the anchor note's error box.

3Cold writeWR14 (baseline)

ShowNothing; this is a baseline.

Say"Write a short tall tale about any hero. This is your starting point. In two weeks we'll see how much you've grown."

Do20 minutes of writing (Notebook L3). Then read Tale 1 ¶5–8 aloud; quick talk about the ending.

GroupsEdition C students may draw and dictate a sentence.

CheckDon't grade. Keep the cold writes; students count their story parts in L13.

4How a story is builtTF3, WR14

ShowAnchor note 2. Think aloud: "What goes wrong? The cold is bad, but in ¶5 the author says 'a new problem'. That's the signal."

DoNotebook L4 story map, then "write about it": is the cold or the hunger the main problem?

GroupsFluency: start repeated reading with Passage 1.

CheckLook for students who name the cold as the problem; correct in tomorrow's warm-up.

5Summing upCO4

ShowAnchor note 5. Model the John Henry gist (anchor) then do Paul Bunyan's SWBST together for the first two boxes.

Say"A summary is not the whole story. If it is longer than the best paragraph, cut."

DoNotebook L5: finish SWBST, write a gist sentence, count words.

GroupsComprehension group: summarize ¶5–6 together before the whole tale.

CheckExit ticket 1.

Week 2 · Characters and language

6Getting to know a characterTF10, CO11

ShowAnchor note 3. Read Tale 2 aloud with expression (students follow).

SayModel one row: "She says 'Determined wins every time.' Her words show she is determined. That's says."

DoNotebook L6 evidence chart, then "write about it" (the most important trait, two pieces of evidence). Flat or round?

GroupsReader's theatre: hand out parts; read-through.

CheckWatch for body words (strong, tall) as traits; redirect to inside traits.

7What does it really mean?VO13, VO14, VO15

ShowAnchor note 4. Think aloud the hot-air balloon example: what's compared, what's in common, why the author chose it.

Say"We never stop at 'it's a simile'. What does it make you picture?"

DoNotebook L7 table, then each student writes their own hyperbole.

GroupsDecoding: mysterious, ferocious (link to -ous in word work).

CheckShare three student hyperboles as tomorrow's warm-up.

8Reading between the linesCO6, CO9, CO3

ShowAnchor note 6: clue + what I know = inference. Model Q1 aloud with the frame.

DoNotebook L8 questions 1–2; "monitor" box: which sentence did you reread and what helped?

GroupsComprehension group: inference with the frame on Tale 2 ¶6–8.

CheckLook for answers with no paragraph number; teach "point to it".

9John Henry: context and predictionCO14, CO18, CO7

ShowBackground (5 min): railroads in the 1870s; steel driving by hand; many workers were Black men freed from slavery, paid little. A photo of a tunnel entrance helps.

DoNotebook L9: two context facts; read ¶1–4 and predict; read to the end; confirm or revise.

SAY (after reading): "The first-day line in ¶2 was a clue. Good readers go back when the ending surprises them."

GroupsFluency: Passage 3 (the race).

CheckHandle John Henry's death respectfully; it is the heart of the theme, not a shock.

10Theme and points of viewCO11, CO12, CO13, TF9

ShowModel the difference between a topic (courage) and a theme (a sentence about courage).

DoNotebook L10: points-of-view chart; theme sentences; opinion with evidence plus one reason someone might disagree.

TalkPairs: "Was the race worth it?" Use the talk moves.

CheckExit ticket 2.

Week 3 · Tales grow; getting ready to write

11Big Joe: a real person becomes a legendOL1, OL2, CO14

ShowTale 4. Map of the Ottawa River (Mattawa, Bytown/Ottawa). Mention that the stories were told in French and English.

DoNotebook L11: the growing wagon story; real vs. legend sort; "write about it": why did raftsmen make the stories bigger?

GroupsReader's theatre rehearsal.

CheckListen for students who now see tall tales as an oral tradition, not lies.

12Tell it out loudOL3, OL4, OL6, OL8

ShowModel a 60-second retelling with deliberate voice: a pause before the biggest exaggeration, a gesture, eye contact.

DoNotebook L12: cue card, voice plan, partner retelling and feedback. Then groups of four discuss "Is it fair to stretch the truth about a real person?" with talk moves; record a group answer.

CheckObservation notes for OL3, OL4 (who projects; who reads the card).

13SRSD stages 1–2: background and discussWR3, WR14

ShowAnchor note 7 (POW + WWW). Read Tale 5 as the mentor text; find each WWW part together.

Say"Good writers plan with these seven parts. Let's see how many our cold writes had."

DoNotebook L13: parts hunt; students count parts in their L3 cold write and set a goal. Show the three rubric samples and talk about what makes each level.

GroupsEdition B/C: count parts with you.

CheckExit ticket 3. Start daily mnemonic practice (30 seconds, chorally, then from memory).

14Cold read 1TF7, TF3, TF10, CO4, CO6, CO9, CO12, VO14

DoCold read 1 (35 min), silent and independent. Edition C: read the Edition A tale aloud first. Remaining time: POW + WWW memory practice in pairs.

CheckScore by code tonight; set Week 4 groups from the results.

15Reader's theatre and memorizeOL3, OL4, TF18

DoPerform Paul Bunyan and the Blue Snow (two casts if needed). Notebook L15 fluency self-rating; write POW + WWW from memory.

Say"Fluent means it sounds like a storyteller, not a race car."

CheckStudents who can't yet write POW + WWW from memory practise again tomorrow. Don't move to modelling until most can.

Week 4 · Model and plan

16SRSD stage 3: model itWR3, WR12, WR14

ShowPlan and write a new tall tale in front of the class, thinking aloud, using the script below. Write on chart paper or the board.

DoNotebook L16: students record what you did and what you said to yourself, then write their own three self-talk statements.

CheckKeep your chart; it's the model for the next two weeks.

Think-aloud script for Lesson 16 (adapt freely)

"P: Pick my idea. What place do I know well? The hockey rink. What job could my hero have? A Zamboni driver. What's amazing? She drives so fast she can flood the whole rink in one second. OK: Zippy Zara. O: Organize my notes. Who: Zippy Zara, the Zamboni driver, and the coach. When: the big game last winter. Where: our town's rink. What does she want? The ice is melting during the championship and she wants to save the game. What happens? She drives so fast the Zamboni makes a snowstorm, then she freezes the ice with her ice-cold stare… then a third thing. How does it end? The game is saved and the ice is so slippery the puck is still sliding today. How do they feel? The crowd goes wild; Zara is so dizzy she drives home backwards. Do I have all seven parts? Who, when, where, want, happens, ends, feel. Yes. W: Write and say more. I'll start with when and where… I'm stuck on how to show the ice melting. Let me look at my notes. I could show it: 'Puddles spread across the ice like spilled juice.' Good, that part is done. Did I show, not tell?"

17SRSD stage 5: we plan togetherWR3, WR14

ShowThe class picks a hero (vote among three ideas) and you scribe the WWW plan as students suggest notes.

DoNotebook L17: copy the class plan; write the class's best exaggeration.

CheckStudents should be able to say what part is missing before you add it.

18Show, don't tellWR15, WR13, WR11

ShowAnchor note 8; the typewriter line from Tale 5 ¶3. Model one rewrite from telling to showing.

DoNotebook L18: three rewrites; exaggeration ladder (real → bigger → tall tale that shows a trait).

CheckExit ticket 4. Watch for "very very very"; redirect to an action or sense detail.

19My plan: P and OWR3, WR14, TF20

DoNotebook L19: brainstorm (a place I know, a job, an ability, a problem in nature or the community), pick one, organize all 7 parts. Encourage local places: a real Alberta place makes stronger tales (and connects to land and place).

GroupsConference with Edition B/C students first; they use the story frame.

CheckNo drafting until the plan has all 7 parts.

20Dialogue and exaggerationsCV2, CV3, WR16, VO15

ShowAnchor note 9. Mentor: Tale 2 ¶7–8.

DoNotebook L20: two lines of dialogue for their tale, three exaggerations that show, one comparison.

CheckExit ticket 5.

Week 5 · Draft and feedback

21Draft: beginning and problemWR12, WR14

SayQuick reminder of self-talk statements. "Skip lines. Say more."

DoDraft (Notebook draft pages), 30 minutes. Conference with 4–5 students: one teaching point each.

GroupsEdition C students may dictate; the outcome here is composing, so dictation is an access support.

22Draft: eventsWR12, WR15

ShowTwo-minute mini-lesson: events that build (three steps, each bigger), from the rubric Sample C.

DoContinue drafting; conferences.

23Draft: resolution and ending; self-checkWR12, WR14

DoFinish the draft. Count parts, tick the checklist, colour the progress graph (cold write vs. draft). Self-assess with the single-point checklist.

SayModel attributing progress to strategy: "You went from 3 parts to 7 because you used POW + WWW."

24Structured peer feedbackWR7, OL6, OL7

ShowModel the routine with a volunteer: author reads aloud; listener uses the checklist, then the two sentence starters.

DoNotebook L24 in pairs; then the author writes one change to make first.

CheckListen for vague feedback ("it's good") and push for "where?"

25ReviseWR7, WR11

ShowModel one revision on your Zippy Zara draft (telling → showing; a stronger verb).

Say"Revising is not fixing spelling. That's tomorrow."

DoNotebook L25: at least two revision moves; before/after sentence.

CheckExit ticket 6.

Week 6 · Publish and perform

26EditWR8, CV2, CV4, CV12–14

DoNotebook L26: one read-through per editing target (dialogue, past tense, capitals, my spelling patterns, joining words). Three words fixed, with the pattern.

GroupsPull students who have tense slips; practise with the Week 2 word-work item.

27Cold read 2CO4, TF10, CO11, VO13, WR15, CO6, CO9, CO12, TF7

DoCold read 2 (35 min). Edition C: read the A tale aloud first. Early finishers: final copy.

CheckCompare with cold read 1 by code; note growth for reporting.

28Publish and self-assessWR9, WR1

DoFinal copy (handwritten or typed), title, illustration with a caption. Self-assess with the rubric (Notebook L28). Score with the rubric and give one written feedback point they can use in the next unit.

29Storytelling festivalOL3, OL4, OL1

DoStudents tell their tales (not read them) to a Grade 2 or 3 class in small circles, using cue cards. Arrange the partner class at least a week ahead.

CheckObservation checklist: projects voice; pauses for effect; uses a gesture; looks at the audience.

30Look back, look aheadWR7, CO2

DoNotebook L30: finish the progress graph, reflect on what helped (strategy, self-talk, feedback), set a goal for the next unit, choose a tall tale for independent reading. Self-check box.

SayConnect the strategy forward: "In the next unit you'll learn a new POW partner, TIDE, for information writing."

Notes on the tales

John Henry: historians think he may have been a real worker on the C&O Railway's Big Bend Tunnel in West Virginia (about 1870–1872); no record proves it. Much of the tunnel work was done by African American workers, including convict labour. Big Joe Mufferaw: Joseph Montferrand (1802–1864) was a real raftsman and strongman; the boot prints on an inn ceiling are a legend the Ottawa Valley still tells. The Stompin' Tom Connors song is a good read-aloud or listen in class (not reproduced here). Chinook winds are real: warm, dry downslope winds east of the Rockies that can raise the temperature by 20 °C or more in hours. The "Chinook arch" is a band of clear sky over the mountains.
