Alberta Tonight: a reader's theatre newscast

Parts: Anchor 1 · Anchor 2 · Reporter in the Rockies · Reporter in the Badlands · Reporter in the Boreal · Doctor Lee (geographer) · Weather Reporter · Everyone

Alberta Tonight

ANCHOR 1Good evening, and welcome to Alberta Tonight, the news show that covers every corner of the province.

ANCHOR 2Tonight: ice that feeds three oceans, bones in the hills, and a forest bigger than all the other regions put together.

ANCHOR 1Let's go live to the Rocky Mountains. Reporter, where are you?

ROCKIES REPORTER(shivering) I'm standing on the Columbia Icefield, and it is cold up here! This ice is the largest in the Rockies. The meltwater under my boots flows toward three different oceans: the Pacific, the Arctic and the Atlantic.

ANCHOR 2Three oceans! Any wildlife?

ROCKIES REPORTERA mountain goat is watching me from the rocks above the treeline. I'm keeping my distance. Back to you!

ANCHOR 1From ice to dust. Let's check in with our reporter in the badlands.

BADLANDS REPORTER(whispering) I'm in the Red Deer River valley, where the hills are bare and striped. A palaeontologist beside me has just spotted something poking out of the rock.

ANCHOR 2Why are you whispering?

BADLANDS REPORTERBecause it might be a dinosaur bone. Erosion uncovers new fossils every spring. (louder) It IS a bone! Back to the studio!

ANCHOR 1Exciting. Now, up north to the Boreal Forest.

BOREAL REPORTERI'm on a boardwalk over the muskeg, a huge, spongy wetland. All around me are spruce, pine and aspen, and the forest goes on for hundreds of kilometres. A moose just walked by. It did not stop for an interview.

ANCHOR 2How big is this forest, exactly?

BOREAL REPORTERMore than half of Alberta. That's bigger than all the other regions put together! Back to you.

ANCHOR 1To help us understand all this, we have Doctor Lee, a geographer. Doctor, how do the regions get along?

DOCTOR LEELike a team. Snow in the mountains becomes river water for cities in the Parkland and farms in the Grassland. No region stands alone.

ANCHOR 2Any advice for our viewers?

DOCTOR LEEGet outside and look at the land where you live. Then ask: how did it get this way?

ANCHOR 1And now the weather. Weather Reporter?

WEATHER REPORTERLook west for a chinook arch, folks. A warm, dry chinook is on the way. Expect the temperature to jump twenty degrees and your snowman to melt by suppertime.

ANCHOR 2That's our show. For Alberta Tonight, I'm—

ANCHOR 1—and I'm—

EVERYONE—and that's the news from across Alberta!

Rehearsal checklist: I can say every place name and topic word in my part I stress the key fact in each line I pause at commas and dashes I sound confident, like a reporter

Repeated reading passages

Read to your partner, who marks any missed word. Count the words read in one minute; if you finish early, start again and keep counting. Read it again, and again. Sound like a reporter: clear, with the important words stressed.

Passage 1 · The Grassland

TextWords
Stand in the Grassland and you can see for kilometres: a huge blue sky, rolling grass, and hardly a tree.20
The Grassland is the driest part of Alberta, and its summers are the hottest.34
Few trees can grow with so little water, but grasses can.45

Passage 2 · The chinook

TextWords
Sometimes in winter, strong winds push air down the east side of the mountains.14
As air sinks, it gets squeezed and warms up.23
The result is a chinook: a warm, dry wind that can raise the temperature by 20 degrees or more in a few hours.46
Snow melts and disappears fast, sometimes in a single afternoon.56

Passage 3 · The Boreal Forest

TextWords
The Boreal Forest covers the north of Alberta.8
It is the largest region, bigger than all the other regions put together.21
Winters are long and very cold, and summers are short.31
Between the forests lie lakes, rivers and huge wetlands called muskeg.42
Moose, wolves, lynx and black bears live in the forest.52

Passage 4 · How ice shaped Alberta

TextWords
Earth's climate grew colder.4
Each winter, more snow fell than melted the next summer.14
The snow piled up and pressed down into ice.23
Huge ice sheets formed, more than a kilometre thick in places.34
As the ice moved, it scraped the land like a giant bulldozer.46
It picked up rocks and boulders and carried them hundreds of kilometres.58
