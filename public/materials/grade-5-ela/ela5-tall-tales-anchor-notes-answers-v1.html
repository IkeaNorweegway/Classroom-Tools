1 · What makes a tall tale?

Learning target: I can tell what makes a story a tall tale and explain why people told them. (TF7, TF3, OL2)

A tall tale is a story about a hero whose body or abilities are impossible / far beyond real life. The teller tells it with a straight face, as if it were true.

Feature of a tall taleExample from Paul Bunyan (Tale 1)
A hero with amazing size, strength or skillPaul combs his beard with a pine tree (¶2)
Exaggeration (stretching the truth on purpose)It was so cold the words froze in the air (¶3)
A real place, job or timeLogging camps in the north woods
A problem solved in an amazing wayBoys skate on the griddle with bacon on their boots (¶5)
Often explains how something in nature came to beBabe's hoofprints became the lakes of Minnesota (¶8)

Why were tall tales told? Workers on the frontier, in logging camps and on rivers told them out loud to pass long nights, to laugh at hard jobs, and to show pride in their work. Each teller made the hero a little bigger, so the tales grew.

Watch out "A tall tale is just a lie."

Why it happens Tall tales say impossible things as if they were true.

Instead A tall tale is a genre. Everyone knows it is exaggerated. The fun is seeing how far the teller can stretch it.

Quick check. Is "The Three Little Pigs" a tall tale? Explain using one feature from the table.

Answer No. The pigs are not a legendary hero with impossible human abilities, and the story is not told as if it were true. (Other answers can work if they use a feature.)

2 · How a story is built

Learning target: I can name the parts of a story and find them in a tale. (TF3, WR14)

Most stories, including tall tales, follow the same path:

PartWhat it doesIn Paul Bunyan
BeginningIntroduces the main character and the setting (where and when)Paul is born; he becomes the greatest logger (¶1–2)
ProblemSomething goes wrong or the character wants somethingThe Winter of the Blue Snow: it is so cold, and the loggers are starving (¶3, ¶5)
EventsMany steps, with lots of details, as the character tries to fix itPaul finds Babe; builds the giant griddle; the loggers get back to work (¶4–6)
ResolutionThe problem is solvedThe loggers are fed and cut more wood than ever (¶6)
EndingHow things end up, and how the character feels or what changesSpring comes and the frozen words thaw (¶7–8)
Watch me find the problem

I ask: What goes wrong? In paragraph 3 everything freezes, but the cold alone is not the problem Paul has to fix. In paragraph 5 the author says "the camp had a new problem: the loggers were starving." The word problem is a signal. That is what Paul solves.

Watch out "The problem is the first bad thing that happens."

Why it happens Stories often have small troubles before the main one.

Instead The main problem is the one the resolution fixes. Check: does the ending solve it?

Quick check. In Chinook Charlie (Tale 5), what is the problem, and what is the resolution?

Answer Problem: the Great Freeze; the river is frozen, the cattle can't drink and the town is stuck (¶3). Resolution: Charlie blows a giant warm wind from the mountains and everything thaws (¶5–6).

3 · Getting to know a character

Learning target: I can describe a character using what they say, think and do, and what others say about them. (TF10, CO11, CO9)

Authors don't just tell us what a character is like. They show us. We find out in four ways:

WayQuestion to askSally Ann (Tale 2)So she is…
What they sayWhat do their words show?"Determined wins every time." (¶8)determined / confident
What they thinkWhat is on their mind?She thinks the bear is "only hungry" (¶8)calm / clever
What they doWhat do their actions show?Walks straight out to the bear when everyone hides (¶6)brave
What others say or thinkHow do other characters see them?Davy: "the bravest, strongest, most surprising person" (¶5)surprising / strong

A trait is a word for what a character is like inside (brave, stubborn, kind), not what they look like (tall, strong).

Tall-tale heroes are usually flat characters: they stay the same from start to finish. A round character is more layered and can change during the story.

Watch out "Sally Ann is strong, so her trait is strong."

Why it happens Body and abilities are easy to see.

Instead Look for what her actions show about her inside: she is brave, confident, quick-thinking.

Quick check. Write one trait for John Henry (Tale 3) and the evidence that shows it. Name the way (say, think, do, others).

Answer Example: John Henry is determined. He keeps swinging without stopping for water when the machine is ahead (¶6, do). Or: proud/courageous, "before I let that steam drill beat me down, I'll die with this hammer in my hand" (¶5, say).

4 · Exaggeration and comparisons

Learning target: I can explain what an exaggeration or comparison means and why the author used it. (VO13, VO14, TF15)

TypeWhat it isExampleWhat it really means
HyperboleA huge exaggeration that is not meant to be taken literally"It was so cold that the flames froze solid."It was colder than anyone could imagine
SimileCompares two things using like or as"The hammer whistled through the air like the wind before a storm."The hammer moved very fast and powerfully
MetaphorCompares two things by saying one is the other"Sally Ann's smile was a lantern at midnight." (our example)Her smile was bright and warm
Watch me explain it

"Charlie's chest swelled up like a hot-air balloon" (Tale 5, ¶5).

  1. What two things are compared? Charlie's chest and a hot-air balloon.
  2. What do they have in common? Both get huge and round when they fill with air.
  3. Why did the author choose it? It helps us picture how giant his breath was, and it is funny.

The question that matters is never just "Is it a simile?" It is: What does it make you picture, and why did the author choose it?

Quick check. "When Big Joe sneezed, the leaves blew off the trees in the next county." What type is it? What does it show about Joe?

Answer Hyperbole. It shows Joe is impossibly big and powerful, and it explains, as a joke, why autumn comes early in the Valley.

5 · Summarizing a story

Learning target: I can write a short summary of a story in my own words. (CO4)

A summary tells the most important parts of a story in a few sentences. It leaves out small details and uses my own words.

Step 1: the gist. One sentence, 20 words or fewer: who + what happens + how it ends.

Step 2: Somebody · Wanted · But · So · Then

SomebodyWantedButSoThen
John Henrywanted to keep the steel drivers' jobsa steam drill could do the work of ten menhe raced the machine and wonhe died, and people still sing about him

Summary rules:

  • Delete small details and repeats (the twenty-pound hammer, the sparks).
  • Replace lists with one word ("cattle can't drink, bus stuck, hockey cancelled" → "the whole town was stuck").
  • Keep the order of events.
Watch me

Gist (19 words): John Henry raced a steam drill to save the workers' jobs; he won, but died from the effort.

Watch out "A summary retells everything that happened."

Why it happens Every part feels important while you read.

Instead A summary keeps only what someone needs to understand the story. If it is longer than the story's best paragraph, cut.

Quick check. Write a gist sentence (20 words or fewer) for Big Joe Mufferaw.

Answer Example: A real raftsman named Joseph Montferrand became the legend Big Joe Mufferaw as people kept telling bigger stories about him.

6 · Reading between the lines

Learning target: I can make an inference and back it up with evidence from the text. (CO6, CO9, CO5)

An inference is something the author does not say straight out, but I can figure out.

Clue in the text + what I already know = inference

Clue in the textWhat I knowInference
"The workers looked at each other. If the machine took their jobs, how would they feed their families?" (Tale 3, ¶5)People need jobs to earn money for foodThe workers are worried / scared about the steam drill
Everyone hides in their cabins when the bear comes (Tale 2, ¶6)People hide when they are afraidThe settlers think the bear is dangerous, but Sally Ann does not

Answering with evidence:

Sentence starters

I think   . In paragraph  , the text says "   ." This shows   because   .

Watch out "If it isn't written in the story, I can't know it."

Why it happens Some answers are stated; others are implied.

Instead Good readers use clues and what they know to work out what the author implies, then point to the clue.

Quick check. How does Paul feel about Babe when he finds her? Use the frame.

Answer I think Paul cares about Babe. In paragraph 4, he "tucked the calf inside his coat and carried it back to camp." This shows he wants to keep her warm and safe, even though he is busy.

8 · Show, don't tell

Learning target: I can show a feeling or a fact through details instead of telling it. (WR15, WR13, WR11)

Telling names it: It was very cold. · Charlie was tired. Showing lets the reader see, hear or feel it: It was so cold that people had to chip their shadows off the sidewalk with a spoon. · Charlie slept for a week on top of the mountain, snoring softly.

Tale 5, paragraph 3

"Their teeth were chattering so hard that they sounded like a hundred typewriters."

What is the author showing here instead of telling? The townspeople are freezing cold.

Ways to show:

  • an action (teeth chattering, stamping boots)
  • a sound or sense detail (boots crunched like cereal)
  • dialogue (what someone says)
  • an exaggeration that proves the point (cows giving ice cream)

Watch out "Showing means adding more adjectives: very very very cold."

Why it happens Extra describing words feel like more detail.

Instead Show with something that happens, a sense detail or a comparison, so the reader works it out.

Quick check. Turn this telling sentence into showing: Sally Ann was brave.

Answer Example: When the grizzly roared and everyone ran for their cabins, Sally Ann walked straight out into the snow and smiled at it.

9 · Writing dialogue

Learning target: I can punctuate dialogue so the reader knows who is speaking. (CV2, CV3, WR16)

Tale 2, paragraph 7–8

"How did you do it?" Davy asked her.

"That bear was only hungry," Sally Ann said. "I was hungry and determined."

Dialogue rules:

  1. Put quotation marks around the exact words someone says.
  2. The end punctuation goes inside the closing quotation mark: ," ?" !"
  3. Use a comma, not a period, before the closing mark when a tag follows: "Hold still," she called.
  4. Start a new paragraph each time a different person speaks.
  5. Use the tag to show how it is said: whispered, roared, muttered, grinned.

Watch out All the talking goes in one paragraph.

Why it happens It feels like one conversation.

Instead New speaker, new line. It helps the reader keep track of who is talking.

Quick check. Fix the punctuation: help us charlie said the mayor the river is frozen

Answer "Help us, Charlie," said the mayor. "The river is frozen!"
