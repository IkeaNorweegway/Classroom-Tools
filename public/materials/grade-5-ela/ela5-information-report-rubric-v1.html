Sample reports (all about the lungs)

Sample A · Meets

Sample A

The Lungs

The lungs are important. They help you breathe. You have two lungs.

The lungs take in oxygen. Air goes in your nose and down your windpipe to your lungs. Oxygen goes into your blood.

Breathing out gets rid of carbon dioxide. The diaphragm helps you breathe.

The lungs are very important and you should take care of them.

Source: Body Systems Reader

Why it is "Meets"

Organization: there is an introduction, body paragraphs and a conclusion, but the introduction has no hook and paragraph 3 mixes two subtopics. Facts: accurate and in the writer's own words; one source. Text features: a title only. Conventions: correct, but the science words aren't explained ("The diaphragm helps you breathe": how?).

Sample B · Proficient

Sample B

The Lungs: Your Body's Air Bags

Take a deep breath. Did you feel your chest get bigger? That was your lungs filling with air. The lungs are the main organs of the respiratory system.

The path of air

Air takes a path to get to your lungs. First, it goes in your nose, where hairs trap dust. Next, it travels down the windpipe (trachea), which splits into two tubes. Finally, the tubes branch into millions of tiny air sacs called alveoli. The air is now ready to give the blood its oxygen.

The great swap

In the air sacs, an important swap happens. The walls of the air sacs are very thin, so oxygen passes into the blood. At the same time, carbon dioxide passes out of the blood to be breathed out. Without this swap, your cells would run out of oxygen.

Keeping lungs healthy

Exercise makes your breathing muscles stronger. For example, swimming trains you to take deep, steady breaths. Staying away from smoke also protects your lungs.

The lungs bring in the oxygen every cell needs. Breathe deep and keep them healthy!

Diagram: the path of air, with labels: nose, windpipe, lungs, air sacs. Glossary: alveoli: tiny air sacs in the lungs. Sources: Body Systems Reader, Text 3; a children's hospital website.

Why it is "Proficient"

Organization: a question hook; one subtopic per paragraph with a heading; each paragraph has a topic sentence, facts, a detail ("for example, swimming…") and an ending; transitions (first, next, finally). Facts: accurate, in the writer's own words, two sources. Text features: headings, bold word, glossary, labelled diagram. Conventions: parentheses for the second name; correct.

Sample C · Excellent

Sample C

The Lungs: Your Body's Air Bags

You breathe about 20,000 times a day, and you almost never think about it. Each breath is a delivery: your lungs bring in the oxygen that every one of your cells needs, and they take away the waste gas your cells make. Here is how two spongy organs in your chest pull it off.

The path of air

Before air reaches your lungs, it takes a carefully cleaned path. It enters through your nose, where hairs and sticky mucus trap dust like a filter on a furnace. Then it flows down the windpipe (trachea), which splits into two branches, one for each lung. Inside, the branches keep dividing, like an upside-down tree, until they end in about 300 million tiny air sacs called alveoli. By the time air arrives, it is clean, warm and ready.

The great swap

The real work happens in the alveoli. Their walls are thinner than tissue paper, and each sac is wrapped in capillaries. Because the walls are so thin, oxygen slips from the air into the blood, while carbon dioxide slips from the blood into the air. If you spread out all the surfaces inside an adult's lungs, they would cover about half a tennis court, which is why so much oxygen can cross so quickly. As a result, every breath refuels your blood.

The muscle that does the work

Your lungs can't move on their own. Instead, the diaphragm, a dome-shaped muscle underneath them, does the pulling. When it tightens and flattens, the chest gets bigger and air rushes in. When it relaxes, air is pushed out. That is why hiccups, which are sudden jerks of the diaphragm, make you gulp air.

Keeping lungs healthy

Lungs get better at their job when you use them. Running, swimming and skating train your breathing muscles to take deeper breaths. Avoiding smoke matters too: it damages the alveoli, and they cannot grow back.

So the next time you take a deep breath, remember the swap happening inside you, millions of times over. Your lungs never take a day off, so take care of them.

Diagram: the respiratory system, labelled (nose, windpipe, lungs, branches, alveoli, diaphragm). Caption: "Air travels from the nose to the alveoli, where oxygen enters the blood." · Sidebar: "Did you know? You breathe out about a cup of water vapour every day; you can see it on a cold Alberta morning." · Glossary: alveoli, diaphragm, capillaries. Sources: Body Systems Reader, Text 3; a children's hospital website; a library book about the human body.

Why it is "Excellent"

Organization: a hook with a number that sets up the whole report; each paragraph has TIDE with vivid details (a furnace filter, tissue paper, a tennis court); transitions show cause ("because", "as a result"); the conclusion links back to the hook. Facts: accurate, explained, from three sources, in the writer's voice. Text features: headings, bold words, glossary, a labelled diagram with a caption that explains, and a local sidebar. Conventions: correct; science words used precisely and explained.
