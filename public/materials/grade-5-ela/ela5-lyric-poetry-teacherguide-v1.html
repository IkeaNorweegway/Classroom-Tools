Lyric Poetry — Teacher Guide

Grade 5 ELA · Unit 5 · 20 lessons of 60 minutes (4 weeks) · Anchor topic: stories of place and land · Planning routine: Place, Senses, Feeling, Compare, Shape
For the teacher

At a glance

Students listen to, read and perform lyric poems about places and feelings: four public-domain poems (E. Pauline Johnson, Christina Rossetti, Robert Louis Stevenson, Emily Dickinson) and four new poems set in Alberta. They learn what a lyric poem is, how stanzas organize it, how comparisons and sound create feeling, and how poets connect land to personal experience. Each student memorizes and recites a poem and writes two lyric poems, at least one about a place that matters to them. The unit ends with a Poetry Café.

About the planning routine. Place · Senses · Feeling · Compare · Shape is this workspace's routine, built from the research on modelling, explicit planning and self-talk (research §8). It is not a published SRSD strategy and has not been tested as one. Teach it with the same moves: model it, practise it together, then fade.

About the poems. The four older poems are in the public domain and were checked against published editions: Rossetti (Sing-Song, 1872, Project Gutenberg), Stevenson (A Child's Garden of Verses, 1885, Wikisource), Dickinson (The Complete Poems, 1924 edition, Wikisource) and Johnson (Flint and Feather, 1914 edition, Wikisource; first collected in The White Wampum, 1895). The Alberta poems and Coulee in October are new.

E. Pauline Johnson (Tekahionwake) was a Mohawk poet and performer from the Six Nations of the Grand River (1861–1913). Introduce her as a poet first, in her own right; her performances across Canada, including the prairies, connect to this unit's recitation focus. For stories of place and land (TF20), invite students to write about places that matter to them and their families. If you'd like to include local First Nations, Métis or Inuit perspectives on land, invite a Knowledge Keeper through your school's protocol rather than asking students to write from cultures not their own.

The daily block, groups and editions

The block keeps its shape, with one change: the warm-up is often choral reading of a poem (fluency for everyone). Groups: fluency (echo reading, then repeated reading of a short poem), comprehension (poem talk with heavy support: what do you picture? what do you feel? which words?), recitation coaching. Edition B: word box per anchor page, ✎ model rows, starters (stanza-based evidence frames). Edition C: short poems and single stanzas with word supports; echo reading; a poem frame; drawing and dictation.

Error patterns: "a poem has to rhyme"; naming feelings instead of showing them; device-spotting ("that's a simile") without saying what it means; reading line by line with a stop at every line end; memorizing without understanding; "poems are just for girls / for little kids" (address by choosing a wide range).

Think-aloud script for Lesson 11 (adapt freely)

"Place: where do I have a strong feeling? The bus stop on the first cold morning of the year. Senses: I see breath clouds, frost on the bench, the yellow bus far away. I hear boots stamping, someone's music leaking out of headphones. I smell exhaust and cold. I feel my fingers stinging. Feeling: underneath, it's actually… belonging. Everyone's cold together. Compare: our breath clouds are like… speech bubbles with nothing written in them yet. Does that fit belonging? Yes: everyone's about to say something. Shape: stanza 1, waiting; stanza 2, the bus arriving; stanza 3, the feeling. Let me try a first line… 'Seven kids, seven clouds of breath.' I'll end the line on breath: strong word. Good. That stanza's done."
