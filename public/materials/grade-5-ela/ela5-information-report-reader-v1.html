Your Body: A Team of Systems

Text 1 · Overview

1Your body is made of trillions of tiny living units called cells. Cells that do the same job group together to make tissues, like muscle tissue. Tissues work together to make organs, like the heart or the stomach. And organs work together in teams called body systems.

2Each body system has its own job, but no system works alone. They depend on each other the way players on a team do.

The systems and their jobs

3The circulatory system (heart, blood and blood vessels) carries oxygen and food to every cell. The respiratory system (nose, windpipe and lungs) brings oxygen into the body and takes carbon dioxide out. The digestive system (mouth, stomach and intestines) breaks food down into nutrients the body can use.

4The skeletal system (bones) holds the body up and protects soft organs. The muscular system moves the bones. The nervous system (brain, spinal cord and nerves) controls all the other systems and lets you think, feel and remember.

How the team works together

5Think about running for a ball. Your brain sends a message through your nerves to your muscles, which pull on your bones. Your muscles need extra oxygen, so you breathe faster. Your heart beats faster to carry that oxygen in your blood. The energy for all of it came from food your digestive system broke down earlier. Six systems, one kick.

Did you know? Scientists estimate an adult body has about 30 trillion cells. If you counted one cell every second, it would take you almost a million years.
Glossary

cell the smallest living unit of the body · tissue a group of cells that do the same job · organ a body part made of tissues that does a special job · system organs that work together

The Heart: A Pump That Never Rests

Text 2 · Circulatory system

1Your heart is a muscle about the size of your fist, and it is one of the hardest-working parts of your body. It beats about 100,000 times a day, every day of your life, without you ever having to think about it.

A pump with four rooms

2The heart has four hollow spaces called chambers. The two upper chambers, the atria, receive blood. The two lower chambers, the ventricles, push blood out. Small flaps called valves let blood flow in only one direction. The "lub-dub" sound a doctor hears through a stethoscope is the sound of the valves closing.

Two loops

3Blood travels in two loops. In the first loop, the right side of the heart pumps blood to the lungs, where it picks up oxygen. In the second loop, the left side pumps that oxygen-rich blood out to the whole body. Then the blood comes back to the heart, and the trip starts again. One full trip around the body takes about one minute.

Blood vessels

4Blood travels through tubes called blood vessels. Arteries carry blood away from the heart. Veins carry it back. Tiny capillaries, thinner than a hair, connect them and deliver oxygen to each cell. If all the blood vessels in an adult were laid end to end, they would stretch about 100,000 kilometres, more than twice around the Earth.

Try it: Press two fingers gently on the inside of your wrist below your thumb. The beat you feel is your pulse, the push of blood each time your heart squeezes. Count the beats in 15 seconds and multiply by 4.

Keeping it healthy

5Like any muscle, the heart gets stronger with exercise. Activities that make you breathe hard, like running, swimming or skating, make the heart pump more blood with each beat.

Breathing In, Breathing Out

Text 3 · Respiratory system

1Every cell in your body needs oxygen, and every cell makes a waste gas called carbon dioxide. Your respiratory system solves both problems at once. Most people breathe about 20,000 times a day.

The path of air

2When you breathe in, air enters through your nose or mouth. Hairs and sticky mucus in your nose trap dust, so the air is cleaner when it goes down your windpipe (also called the trachea). The windpipe splits into two tubes, one for each lung. Inside the lungs, the tubes branch again and again, like an upside-down tree, until they end in millions of tiny air sacs called alveoli.

Why the air sacs matter

3The alveoli are wrapped in capillaries. Because the walls of the air sacs and the capillaries are so thin, oxygen can pass from the air into the blood. At the same time, carbon dioxide passes from the blood into the air sacs. As a result, when you breathe out, you get rid of the carbon dioxide.

What makes you breathe

4You don't have to think about breathing because a dome-shaped muscle under your lungs, the diaphragm, does the work. When the diaphragm tightens and moves down, the space in your chest gets bigger, so air rushes in. When it relaxes and moves up, the space gets smaller, so air is pushed out.

When you exercise

5When you run, your muscles use oxygen faster. Because of this, your brain signals you to breathe faster and more deeply. That is why you puff and pant after a race.

Did you know? If the inside surface of an adult's lungs were spread flat, it would cover about half a tennis court. All that surface is what lets so much oxygen get into the blood so fast.

Bones and Muscles

Text 4 · Skeletal and muscular systems

1Your bones and your muscles are partners. Bones give your body its shape and strength. Muscles give it movement. Neither can do much without the other.

Bones: the frame

2An adult skeleton has 206 bones. A newborn baby has about 300, but many of them join together as the baby grows. Bones protect soft organs: your skull guards your brain, and your ribs form a cage around your heart and lungs.

3Bones are alive. They have blood vessels and nerves, and they can repair themselves when they break. Inside many bones is soft marrow, which makes new blood cells, millions of them every second.

Muscles: the movers

4You have more than 600 muscles. Skeletal muscles are attached to bones by tough cords called tendons. Muscles can only pull, never push. That is why they work in pairs. When you bend your arm, the muscle on the front of your upper arm (the biceps) tightens and pulls. To straighten it, the muscle on the back (the triceps) pulls the other way while the biceps relaxes.

5Not all muscles are skeletal muscles. Heart muscle pumps blood, and smooth muscle lines your stomach and blood vessels. These muscles work without you deciding to move them.

Alike and different

6Bones and muscles are both living tissues, both need food and oxygen from the blood, and both get stronger when you use them. But they are different in one big way: bones are hard and do not change shape, while muscles are soft and can contract (get shorter) and relax.

Did you know? The smallest bone in your body is in your ear. The stirrup is about the size of a grain of rice.

Keeping them strong

7Running, jumping and climbing make bones denser and stronger. Calcium, found in foods like milk, yogurt and leafy greens, helps build bone.

Glossary

marrow soft tissue inside bones that makes blood cells · tendon a tough cord that joins muscle to bone · contract to tighten and get shorter

The Brain: Your Control Centre

Text 5 · Nervous system

1Right now, your brain is reading these words, keeping your heart beating, controlling your breathing, and remembering what you had for breakfast, all at once. The brain is the control centre of the nervous system.

What the brain is like

2An adult brain weighs about 1.4 kilograms, about as much as a big bag of flour. It is soft, wrinkly and pinkish-grey. It contains about 86 billion nerve cells, called neurons, which pass messages to each other using tiny electrical and chemical signals.

Three main parts

3The cerebrum is the biggest part. It handles thinking, learning, memory, the senses and the movements you choose to make. The cerebellum, at the back, controls balance and coordination; it helps you ride a bike without falling. The brain stem connects the brain to the spinal cord and controls things you don't think about, like breathing and heartbeat.

The message highway

4The spinal cord runs down the middle of your backbone. Nerves branch out from it to every part of your body. When you touch something hot, nerves in your skin send a message up the spinal cord. In some cases, the spinal cord sends a message straight back to pull your hand away before your brain even notices. This fast reaction is called a reflex.

Did you know? Some nerve messages travel more than 100 metres per second. That is faster than a race car.

Taking care of your brain

5Sleep is when the brain sorts and stores what you learned during the day; children your age need about 9 to 11 hours a night. A helmet protects your brain when you bike, ski or skate.

From Sandwich to Energy

Text 6 · Digestive system

1Your body can't use a sandwich as a sandwich. It has to be broken down into tiny pieces, called nutrients, small enough to pass into your blood. That is the job of the digestive system, and it takes from several hours to about two days from start to finish.

Step 1: the mouth

2Digestion begins before you swallow. Your teeth cut and grind food, and saliva (spit) softens it and starts to break down starches. Your tongue pushes the soft lump to the back of your throat.

Step 2: the esophagus

3When you swallow, the food enters the esophagus, a muscle tube about 25 centimetres long. Muscles squeeze in waves to push the food down to your stomach. Because of this squeezing, you could even swallow while standing on your head.

Step 3: the stomach

4The stomach is a stretchy muscle bag. It churns food and mixes it with strong acid and juices that break it down further. After a few hours, the food has become a thick liquid.

Step 4: the small intestine

5Next, the liquid moves into the small intestine, a narrow tube about 6 metres long, coiled up in your belly. Here, most of the nutrients pass through the walls into the blood, which carries them to every cell.

Step 5: the large intestine

6Finally, what is left moves into the wider large intestine, where water is soaked back into the body. The waste that remains leaves the body when you go to the bathroom.

Did you know? Your stomach makes a new lining every few days so its own acid doesn't digest it.

The Body's Delivery System

Text 7 · A model report (our mentor text)

Introduction

1Imagine a delivery truck that visits every home in the country once a minute, every minute, for your whole life. Your body has one. It is called the circulatory system, and it is made of the heart, the blood and the blood vessels. Together, they deliver what every cell needs and carry away what it doesn't.

The heart

2The heart is the engine of the circulatory system. It is a muscle about the size of your fist, and it beats about 100,000 times a day. Inside, it has four chambers and valves that keep blood moving in one direction. For example, the right side sends blood to the lungs, and the left side sends it to the rest of the body. Without this pump, blood would not move at all.

The blood

3Blood is the cargo of the delivery system. Red blood cells carry oxygen from the lungs, and the liquid part of blood, called plasma, carries nutrients from food. White blood cells fight germs. In fact, a single drop of blood holds millions of red blood cells. Blood picks up waste, like carbon dioxide, and takes it away to be removed.

The blood vessels

4The blood vessels are the roads. Arteries carry blood away from the heart, and veins bring it back. The smallest vessels, capillaries, are thinner than a hair, so oxygen and nutrients can pass through their walls into the cells. If you lined up all the blood vessels in an adult, they would circle the Earth more than twice.

Conclusion

5The heart, the blood and the blood vessels work as a team to keep every cell alive. The next time you feel your pulse, remember: that beat is the delivery truck making its rounds.

Sources: Body Systems Reader, Text 2 (our class); The Circulatory System, a library book about the human body.
What makes it a model

The introduction hooks the reader and names the topic. Each body paragraph is one subtopic, built with TIDE: a Topic sentence, Important evidence, Detail (an example, a number, a comparison), and an Ending sentence. The conclusion sums up and ends with a thought. Headings, bold words and a source list are text features.
