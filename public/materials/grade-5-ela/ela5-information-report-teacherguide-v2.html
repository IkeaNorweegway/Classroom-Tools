Information Report — Teacher Guide

Grade 5 ELA · Unit 2 · 30 lessons of 60 minutes (6 weeks) · Anchor topic: regions of Alberta · SRSD strategy: POW + TIDE
For the teacher

At a glance

Students read six information texts about Alberta's six natural regions (Grassland, Parkland, Foothills, Rocky Mountain, Boreal Forest, Canadian Shield) and a model report, learn how information texts are organized (topic and subtopics, main idea, cause and effect, compare and contrast, sequence) and how text features work, then research one region, or one special place in a region, and write a multi-paragraph report with POW + TIDE. They check sources for reliability, take notes in their own words, and publish with text features. The unit ends with a Regions Expo for another class.

ReadingText features, topic and subtopics, main idea and summary, cause and effect, compare and contrast, topic vocabulary from context and word parts, evaluating sources
WritingSRSD with POW + TIDE: research questions, notes, TIDE paragraphs, introduction and conclusion, transitions, text features (including a labelled map), sources list
Word work-al/-ial, ex-/in-, trans-/sub-, -ous/-ic, -ment/-tion; Tier 2 and Tier 3 words; capitals for place names, commas in lists, subject–verb agreement, parentheses
FluencyAlberta Tonight newscast (reader's theatre), repeated reading of information passages
Oral languageStructured discussion, Regions Expo talk
AssessmentSix exit tickets; cold reads in Lessons 14 and 27; report rubric with three samples

Why it is built this way (research/literacy-reading-writing-research.md): text structure instruction (0.96 for struggling readers, §3), summary writing (0.79 at elementary, §9), SRSD (§8), and one topic for the shared texts so knowledge builds (§2). Alberta's land suits every text structure: weather is cause and effect, neighbouring regions invite comparison, the Ice Age is a sequence. The topic links to Social Studies (geography of Alberta) and to Science (landforms, weather, living things); tell those teachers and use their vocabulary.

Gather before Lesson 1: a large printed natural regions map of Alberta (Alberta Parks publishes a free one) for the wall; students check their L1 guess about the school's region on it and use it in the Expo.

Gather before Lesson 11: library books on Alberta's regions, parks and animals at a range of levels, and 2–3 bookmarked child-friendly websites (the provincial and national parks sites, the Royal Tyrrell Museum, a science centre). Students need two reliable sources each. The Grassland, Rocky Mountain and Boreal Forest have the most child-friendly sources; the Canadian Shield and the Foothills have the fewest, so steer confident researchers there or let them research one place inside a region (the badlands, the Columbia Icefield, a park near the school). The model report is on the Foothills: a student may still choose it, but needs their own sources and subtopics.

Elders and Knowledge Keepers as sources (optional, strongly worth it): the program names Elders and Knowledge Keepers as sources of knowledge. If you can invite one to speak about the land where the school stands, follow your division's protocol for the invitation and the gift. Students may cite what they learned as a spoken source (name and Nation, with permission). Teach which Treaty area the school is on (6, 7 or 8) in Lesson 1.

Facts in the texts are rounded. Sources disagree: Text 3 says plainly that the famous 1962 Pincher Creek chinook is reported as "one hour" by some sources and "a few hours" by others. That is a good lesson: check two sources, and cite them. Names and spellings from First Nations languages (Niitsitapi, Áísínai'pi, Nakoda) follow common usage; check them with your division's First Nations, Métis and Inuit lead.

The daily block

Same shape as every unit: warm-up (5) · mini-lesson (12) · work time with two groups and conferences (35) · exit or write-about-it (8). Groups by need: decoding (long-word routine on topic words and place names: sub-al-pine, Ath-a-bas-ca, re-gion-al, Ca-na-di-an), fluency (repeated reading, leveled non-fiction), comprehension (grade-level text with heavy support; main idea and text structure), on track (extra research, a second sidebar, conferences).

Week 1 · How information texts work

1Hook: Alberta by numbersCO1, OL6

ShowThe natural regions map. "If we drove from the Montana border to the Northwest Territories, about 1,200 km, what would we see out the window?" Collect ideas. Name the Treaty area the school is on.

CheckNote misconceptions to address (Alberta is all prairie; the north is all tundra; chinooks come from the south; the mountains are cold because they're in the north).

Week 2 · Structures, words and sources

6Cause and effect: mountains make the weatherTF11, CO6

ShowAnchor note 4. Signal words. Model the first row of the chart. Act out the air: students "rise" (stand up on tiptoe), "cool" (shiver), "drop snow" (flutter fingers), then "sink" (crouch) and "warm up".

DoRead Text 3. Notebook L6: cause-effect chart; explain why the Grassland is dry.

Talk"Who has seen a chinook arch, or watched snow vanish in a day? What happened?"

Week 4 · Model and plan

16SRSD stage 3: model itWR3, WR4, WR20

ShowTurn your own notes on the three zones of a mountain (Text 5) into a TIDE paragraph, thinking aloud (script below). Write it on chart paper.

DoNotebook L16: record what you did and said; write three self-talk statements.

Think-aloud script for Lesson 16 (adapt freely)

"My subtopic is the three zones of a mountain. T: what is this paragraph about, in one sentence? 'A mountain in the Rockies has three zones, and each one is its own little world.' I: which facts matter most? Montane: lowest and warmest, forests and meadows, elk. Subalpine: spruce and fir, deep snow. Alpine: above the treeline, low plants, pikas. My notes say 'the height where it becomes too cold and windy for trees to grow'… that's almost the book's sentence. Let me say it my way: 'Above the treeline, no tree can stand the cold and the wind.' D: can I add a comparison so the reader can picture it? 'Climbing from the valley to the peak is a bit like travelling north toward the Arctic: the higher you go, the colder it gets and the smaller the plants.' E: how do I wrap it up? 'From valley to peak, one mountain holds three different worlds.' Do I have T, I, D and E? Yes. Good, that paragraph is done."

29Regions ExpoOL3, OL5, OL6

CheckObservation: projects voice; explains the map or diagram; answers a question.
