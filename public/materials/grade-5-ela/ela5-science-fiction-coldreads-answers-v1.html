Science Fiction · Two new stories you have not read before. Read the story, then answer. Use paragraph numbers.
Edition A
Answer key. Model answers show one strong response; accept others that are supported by the text.
How to give and score the cold reads

Cold read 1 in Lesson 14, cold read 2 in Lesson 27, about 35 minutes each. Silent and independent; Edition C students hear the Edition A story read aloud first, then reread the short version. Don't pre-teach. Score with the points shown; record by code.

Cold read 1 · The Last Paper Book

The Last Paper Book

A new science fiction story

1In the city of Port Aurora, in the year 2110, nobody read paper books anymore. Stories floated in the air above your wrist, glowing, and turned their own pages when your eyes reached the bottom. They could even read themselves out loud in any voice you liked.

2Eli Ramos liked his reader-band fine. But he loved his grandfather's apartment, because it was the only place he knew that still had a shelf of paper books. They were yellowed and soft at the edges, and they smelled like dust and something sweet.

3"Why do you keep these?" Eli asked one rainy Saturday. "They don't even light up."

4His grandfather, Lolo Ramon, pulled one down and handed it to him. "Because nobody can change them," he said. "Your band updates its stories every night. Did you know that? If someone decides a story should be different, it's different by morning, and you'd never know."

5Eli laughed. "Why would anyone change a story?"

6Lolo shrugged. "People have their reasons. Here, read this one. Then read the same story on your band, and tell me what you find."

7The book was called The River Children. Eli read it on Lolo's couch all afternoon, turning the crackly pages, while the rain tapped on the window. At the end, the children in the story stood up to the mayor who wanted to dam their river, and they won.

8That night, he opened The River Children on his band. It was the same, almost word for word, until the last chapter. On his band, the children didn't stand up to the mayor. They agreed that the dam was a good idea, and everyone went home happy.

9Eli sat very still. His stomach felt hollow.

10The next Saturday, he came back to Lolo's apartment with a notebook and a pencil, which he'd had to borrow from a museum gift shop. "Lolo," he said, "can you teach me how to keep a story safe?"

11His grandfather smiled the widest smile Eli had ever seen. "First," he said, "we copy it by hand."

1. What makes this story science fiction? Give two features with evidence. (TF7) [2 points]

Answer Any two: set in the future (2110, ¶1); technology that doesn't exist (stories floating above your wrist, reader-bands that update, ¶1, ¶4); a "what if?" (what if stories could be changed without anyone knowing?); the technology changes a choice.

2. Who tells this story, and how do you know? (TF3) [1 point]

Answer A third-person narrator: it uses names and "he" (e.g., "Eli Ramos liked his reader-band fine," ¶2), never "I" outside dialogue.

3. How does the setting shape what Eli thinks at the beginning? (CO15, CO18) [2 points]

Answer In 2110, everyone reads on glowing bands, so Eli thinks paper books are strange and useless ("They don't even light up," ¶3).

4. Is Eli a round or flat character? Give evidence from the beginning and the end. (TF10, CO11) [2 points]

Answer Round: at the start he laughs at the idea that anyone would change a story (¶5); at the end he wants to learn to keep stories safe by copying them by hand (¶10).

5. "His stomach felt hollow" (¶9). What is the author showing about Eli? (WR15, CO6) [2 points]

Answer That he is shocked and upset, because he realizes the story on his band was secretly changed.

6. Write the theme of the story as a sentence. Give one piece of evidence. (TF9, CO9) [2 points]

Answer e.g., It's important to protect the truth, even when technology makes it easy to change. Evidence: the band's version of The River Children was changed, and Eli decides to copy the real one by hand (¶8, ¶10).

7. Why do you think the band's version of the story was changed? Make an inference. (CO6) [2 points]

Answer e.g., Someone in power didn't want people reading about children standing up to a mayor, so they changed it to make the dam seem good.

Cold read 2 · The Seed

The Seed

A new science fiction story

1The greenhouse on Mars Station Two was the only green place for fifty million kilometres. Rosa Tran, who was ten and the youngest person on the station, spent every free minute there, among the tomatoes and the bean vines climbing toward the grow-lights.

2Her favourite plant was the one nobody else cared about: a small, ugly cactus from Earth, with one sharp spine and no flowers at all. The station's botanist, Dr. Pell, wanted to compost it. "It takes up space," he said. "It's never going to do anything."

3"Please," Rosa said. "Just one more month."

4That night, the station alarms went off. A dust storm had cracked the greenhouse's water pipe, and the recycling system was down. The station had enough water for its people, but not for its plants. "We'll have to let the garden go," Dr. Pell said quietly. "We can replant from seeds when the system's fixed. In six months, maybe."

5For three weeks, Rosa watched the greenhouse wilt. The beans went brown. The tomatoes dropped their leaves. Only one plant stayed green: the ugly little cactus, which had stored water inside its thick stem, the way cacti do in Earth's deserts.

6And then, on the twenty-second day, it bloomed. A single flower opened at the top of the spine, bright pink, like a tiny sunrise.

7Forty years later, Dr. Rosa Tran stood in the middle of the first open-air garden on Mars, under a sky that was slowly turning from orange to blue. Around her grew hundreds of tough, water-saving plants, every one of them bred from a single ugly cactus that had once refused to die. A reporter asked her where the idea had come from.

8"From a plant everyone wanted to throw away," she said.

9Back on Mars Station Two, ten-year-old Rosa carried the flowering cactus to Dr. Pell. He looked at it for a long time.

10"Well," he said finally, "I suppose it did something after all."

1. Fill in the story map. (TF3, WR14) [3 points]

ProblemMain eventEnding
A dust storm breaks the water pipe; there's no water for the plantsEverything wilts except the cactus, which stores water and bloomsRosa shows Dr. Pell, who admits the cactus "did something after all"

2. Where does the flash-forward begin and end? What does it show? (TF8) [2 points]

Answer Begins "Forty years later…" (¶7) and ends before "Back on Mars Station Two" (¶9). It shows that the cactus led to the first open-air garden on Mars, bred by Rosa.

3. Why do you think the author put the flash-forward before the last scene? (TF8, WR10) [2 points]

Answer So readers know how important the little cactus will become, which makes Dr. Pell's last line funny and meaningful.

4. How do Rosa and Dr. Pell see the cactus differently at the start? (CO12) [2 points]

Answer Dr. Pell thinks it is useless ("It's never going to do anything," ¶2); Rosa cares about it and asks for more time.

5. "Like a tiny sunrise" (¶6). What does this comparison help you picture? (VO13) [1 point]

Answer A small, bright, warm, pink flower that feels hopeful, like the start of a new day.

6. Write a theme for this story as a sentence. (TF9) [2 points]

Answer e.g., Things (or people) that others overlook can turn out to be the most important.

7. Compare Rosa with Maya from The Homework Helper. How are they alike or different? (CO12, TF10) [2 points]

Answer e.g., Both make a choice that matters, but Rosa is determined from the start while Maya changes her mind after a mistake.
