Teacher Guide

Immigration After Confederation

Grade 7 Social Studies  ·  Citizenship · Suggested pacing 5–6 weeks, 3 classes a week (~17 classes)

Suggested length: ~17 classes (5–6 weeks, 3 classes a week)    Tier: 1    Unit colour: Teal (Citizenship)

Guiding question: Did post-Confederation immigration shape the development of the Canadian state?

Learning outcome: Students examine immigration in post-Confederation Canada. Local skill codes IM1–IM6 are in the course context file.

Big idea for students to leave with: Immigration built the West: farms, towns and the wheat economy. But the government chose who could come, and some of its choices were based on race. Opportunity and discrimination happened at the same time, often from the same government.

What this unit is really about: Students often hold either a proud story ("Canada welcomed everyone") or a cynical one ("it was all racism"). The curriculum wants both halves, judged with evidence. The two skill threads that run through every section are cause and consequence, and fact vs. opinion. The unit ends with a fairness judgement (IM5), so students need the opportunities (Sections 1–3) clearly before the restrictions (Sections 4–5).

Lesson Sequence

Section 1 — Wanted: Settlers (2 classes)
Opening: the last, best West
Show Pre-Knowledge Check, then a real 1900s Department of the Interior poster ("The Last, Best West"; Library and Archives Canada has many).
Say "This was an advertisement. Who was it aimed at? Who is missing from the picture?"
Cue Students list every push and pull they can find in the poster.
Notes Section 1 push-pull diagram and Retrieval Check. Feeds Workbook Q1, Q8.
Section 2 — Free Land (2 classes)
The homestead deal
Show A quarter section drawn to scale on the playground or gym floor (160 acres is about 120 football fields; draw one "field" and multiply).
Say "For $10 and three years of very hard work, this could be yours. For many families in Europe, owning land was impossible."
Notes Section 2 steps diagram and misconception box ("free, empty land"). Feeds Workbook Q3, Q10.
Section 3 — Building the Prairies (3 classes)
Who came, and what they built
Show A map of Alberta place names. Students find Ukrainian, Francophone and other settlement names (Vegreville, Mundare, Saint-Paul, Bonnyville, Legal, Amber Valley).
Say "Many groups settled in blocs, near people who shared their language and faith. Ukrainian settlers cleared and farmed land many others had passed over."
Notes Section 3 chain diagram and Retrieval Check (IM1). Feeds Workbook Q5, Q9, Q11. Good place for the settler-stories inquiry.
Section 4 — Keeping Some Groups Out (3 classes)
Refutation first: welcomed equally?
Show Two items side by side, both from 1903: a recruiting poster and the head tax law raising the fee to $500.
Say "Same government, same year. What does that tell you about who Canada wanted?"
Notes Section 4 misconception boxes and timeline. Read the sensitive-content note first. Feeds Workbook Q2, Q7, Q12.
Why make these laws?
Show The reasons given at the time (jobs, wages) next to the beliefs behind them.
Say "Some discrimination was in people's heads. Some was written into the law. This section is about the second kind."
Notes Section 4 Retrieval Check (IM4). Give the mid-unit quiz after this section.
Section 5 — Conflict on the West Coast (3 classes)
The 1907 riots and the Komagata Maru
Show The cause–event–consequence diagram with the consequences covered. Students predict consequences first.
Say "Notice the link: the rule that came out of 1907 is the rule the Komagata Maru passengers challenged in 1914."
Cue "The passengers broke a rule on purpose. Was that right?" Link to the Grade 8 civic engagement unit.
Notes Section 5 Retrieval Check (IM3). Feeds Workbook Q6, Q13. Mention the 2006 and 2016 apologies.
Section 6 — Rights, Fairness and Evidence (3 classes)
Minority rights in schools
Show A short scenario: a school promises French classes, then a new majority votes to end them. Is that fair?
Say "That's the Manitoba Schools Question, 1890, and Ontario's Regulation 17, 1912. The question is whether a majority can take away rights a minority was promised."
Notes Section 6 notes. Feeds Workbook Q14.
Fact, opinion and fairness
Show The fact-or-opinion table, then the three fairness questions.
Say "Historians don't just feel. They check. Every source has facts you can confirm and opinions that show a point of view."
Notes Section 6 Retrieval Check (IM5, IM6) and home sheet "Fact or opinion?" Feeds Workbook Q4 and S2.
Consolidation (1 class)
Unit Synthesis
Show Unit Synthesis from memory, then the Pre-Knowledge Check.
Notes Workbook S1–S3, then the unit test.

Discussion and Source Activity Notes

Sensitive content (read before Sections 4 and 5)

  • Some students may have family histories connected to the head tax, the Komagata Maru, or anti-Black racism. Tell students what is coming and give them a way to step out.
  • Do not reproduce racist cartoons or slurs for effect. If you use a primary source with racist language, frame it first and focus on what it shows about the time.
  • Present people as actors, not only victims: the Komagata Maru passengers challenged the law on purpose; Japanese residents defended their street in 1907; Black families founded Amber Valley despite the government.
  • Good sources: Library and Archives Canada (head tax certificates, immigration posters), Simon Fraser University's Komagata Maru online archive, Alberta's Black Settlers resources.

Inquiry option 1 — Settler stories archive (best after Section 3)

  • Each student profiles one group: Ukrainian, Francophone, Black settlers from Oklahoma, Chinese, Sikh, Mennonite, Icelandic or another.
  • Include: push and pull factors, where they settled, what they built, and how they were treated.

Inquiry option 2 — Fair or unfair? (best after Section 6)

  • Groups judge one policy (homestead offer, head tax, Continuous Journey, P.C. 1911-1324, Manitoba Schools) using evidence cards sorted into fact and opinion.
  • Each group presents a judgement with two facts and one perspective.

Inquiry option 3 — Local history

  • Who first settled your town or neighbourhood, and why? Use local museum or archive sources, and record where each fact came from.

Assessment Notes

Formative checkpoints: exit tickets after each section; Exit Ticket 4 (laws as policy, not just prejudice) and Exit Ticket 6 (fact vs. opinion) are the most diagnostic.

What a strong extended response looks like: on Test Q15 and Workbook S3, a strong answer weighs at least one opportunity against at least one restriction, uses accurate dates and names, and makes a clear judgement backed by evidence rather than feeling.

Connections: Building the Canadian State introduced the settlers and the CPR; Building the Economy shows what their wheat did for the country. Grade 9 picks up immigration after the Second World War, the Citizenship Act of 1947, multiculturalism and the Charter.
