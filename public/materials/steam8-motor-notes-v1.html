Concept 1: Basic Circuits & Soldering Safety

I can: safely solder a basic joint and build a working circuit with a battery, switch, and motor.

📌 New Word: circuit

A complete loop a current can flow around. Break the loop anywhere (an open switch, a loose wire) and nothing runs.

📌 New Word: switch

A part that lets you open and close a circuit's loop on purpose, so the motor only runs when you want it to.

📌 New Word: solder joint

A permanent electrical connection made by melting solder onto two pieces of metal (like a wire and a terminal), so they conduct as one piece instead of just touching.

This is the first time in this course you've wired and powered a mechanism yourselves, instead of using a kit's pre-built motor — which is exactly why soldering safety comes first, before any wiring on a real circuit: proper iron handling, ventilation, and burn awareness, practiced on a simple low-stakes joint before anything functional is at stake.

Concept 2: Baseline — What Can the Motor Do Alone?

I can: measure a baseline capability and explain why it has to come before designing a mechanism.

Before you design any gear, lever, or pulley system, you measure exactly what the bare motor can do by itself — how much it can lift, or how far it can pull something against resistance.

📌 New Word: baseline

The bare motor's measured performance, before any mechanism is added. Not a warm-up exercise — it's the number every later decision is reasoned from. Without it, you'd be guessing how much mechanical advantage you actually need.

Concept 3: Mechanical Advantage — Gears, Levers, Pulleys

I can: explain how a gear, lever, or pulley system changes the force or speed a motor's output can achieve.

📌 New Word: mechanical advantage

What a machine gives you when it lets the same motor accomplish more than it could alone — but never for free. A gear train, lever, or pulley system can let a small motor lift a heavier load, at the cost of   (it takes longer / turns slower). This is the same force/distance trade-off you've seen in every simple machine, now applied to a powered mechanism for the first time — directly extending the structural-load reasoning from Grade 6's bridges unit into a mechanism that has an engine attached to it.

Predict, then test: Before you build your mechanism, predict: will your planned gear ratio or lever arrangement actually let the motor accomplish the stated task, given your baseline measurement?

Concept 4: Same Motor, Different Job

I can: explain why the fixed-motor constraint forces mechanical-advantage reasoning instead of "just use a stronger motor."

MISCONCEPTION

"If the motor can't do the job, I need a bigger, stronger motor."

Why this fails: swapping in a stronger motor isn't a real option in this unit — your motor is fixed, on purpose. If "get a stronger motor" were the answer, mechanical advantage wouldn't need to exist as an idea at all. Every real machine that lifts something heavier than its motor could lift directly (an elevator, a crane, a bike in low gear) is solving exactly this problem with gearing or leverage, not with a bigger engine.

Correct understanding: The same motor can accomplish a bigger job through mechanical advantage — a gear train, lever, or pulley system that trades some speed for more force (or the reverse). The constraint that the motor can't be swapped exists specifically to force this reasoning.

  1. Why can't you just use a stronger motor in this unit?
  2. In your own words, how does mechanical advantage let the same motor do a bigger job?
