Guided Notes

Migration is rarely just one decision — it is usually the result of two forces working at once. A push factor is a condition in a person's home country that makes them want to   — war, persecution, poverty, or the destruction of their community. A pull factor is a condition in the destination country that makes it   — safety, jobs, land, or family already living there.

After the Second World War (1945–51), one of the most significant groups to arrive in Canada were displaced persons — refugees, including  , who had lost their homes, families, and countries during the war and could not safely return. Canada's immigration policy gradually opened to admit them, though not without resistance and restriction at the time.

The result was a Canada with changing and increasingly   demographics. International migration after the war meant that Canada's   reflected a more diverse,   population than before — a shift that would only accelerate in later decades as immigration expanded further beyond Europe.

Section 2: Urbanization and the Growth of Industry

Learning target: I can explain how urbanization influenced the growth and transformation of Canadian industries after the Second World War.

Vocabulary

Guided Notes

Urbanization is the process by which a growing share of a population comes to live in   rather than rural areas. After the Second World War, Canada experienced a   boom that, combined with returning soldiers and new immigrants, sharply increased consumption and demand for development.

This growth did not just make cities bigger — it changed what Canada built. New and expanding industries included:

  • Public infrastructure — roads, sewers, schools, and utilities built to support growing suburbs and populations
  • Oil and gas exploration — the growth of the   industry, especially in Alberta
  • Hydroelectricity production — dams and generating stations to power new homes and factories
  • Automobile and paper production — manufacturing that both served and depended on a more urban, consumer population

Alberta connection

Urbanization and the petroleum industry grew together in Alberta: as cities like Calgary and Edmonton expanded, they needed the energy, jobs, and materials the oil and gas industry provided — and the industry, in turn, needed a growing urban workforce. Section 3: Environmental Consequences of Urbanization

Learning target: I can explain the social, economic, and environmental consequences and challenges of increased urbanization.

Guided Notes

Growth is never free. As Canadian cities and industries expanded after the war, they created lasting environmental impacts, including:

  • Pollution — air and water contamination from factories, vehicles, and energy production
  • Land consumption — farmland and wilderness converted into   and industrial sites
  • Land division — large natural areas broken into smaller parcels for development
  • Wildlife migration changes — roads, fences, and buildings disrupting the routes animals had used for generations
Common misconception
"Urbanization only has economic benefits — the environmental costs came later, with modern industry."

In fact, the environmental consequences of pollution, land division, and habitat disruption were already visible during the first postwar urban boom of the 1950s and 60s — they were simply less discussed and less regulated at the time than they are today.
