Teacher Guide

Canada's System of Government

Social Studies 9  ·  Systems  ·  Suggested pacing: 5 classes

Suggested length: 5 classes   Tier: 1   Unit colour: Systems blue

Guiding question: How is power organized and exercised in Canada's system of government?

Learning outcome: Students analyze how power is distributed and exercised in Canada's government, including how laws are made and how rights are protected.

Big idea for students to leave with: Power in Canada is deliberately split up and checked — between elected and appointed bodies, between federal and provincial governments, and between lawmakers and courts — so that no single part can act without limits.

What this unit is really about: Grade 9 students arrive with a vague sense that "the government makes laws" and not much underneath that. The real work here is showing them that power in Canada is never held in one place — it's deliberately fragmented and checked at every level: within Parliament (Crown, Commons, Senate all have to agree), between governments (federal vs. provincial jurisdiction), and against government entirely (the judiciary and the Charter). Keep coming back to that one thread — fragmentation and accountability — because it's what turns four disconnected-feeling topics (Parliament, lawmaking, federalism, courts) into one coherent system. The Alberta natural-resources thread is also your best hook for local relevance, so don't rush Section 3.

Lesson Sequence

Class 1 — Pre-Knowledge Check and Responsible Government
Opening: what do you already think?
Show The two Pre-Knowledge Check questions from Notes p. 1, projected: "What's the difference between federal and provincial responsibilities?" and "How do you think a bill becomes a law?" Students answer cold, no notes.
Say "I don't care if you're right here — I want your honest best guess on paper. At the end of the unit we're going to come back to this exact page and see how much has changed. That gap is the point."
Notes Notes Pre-Knowledge Check, p. 1.
Responsible government: why "one person decides" isn't how it works
Show A quick contrast: describe a school run by a principal who decides everything alone, unless the school board objects (this is the scenario Workbook Q7 uses) — then ask whether that sounds like how Canada is actually governed.
Say "In Canada, the government we elect isn't just the Prime Minister — it's an executive that has to keep the confidence of the elected House of Commons the whole time it's in power. Lose that confidence, and the government doesn't get to just keep going. It has to resign or call an election. That's what 'responsible' means here: responsible to elected representatives, not just responsible for itself."
Cue "Why not just let one person or one office make all the decisions? Wouldn't that be faster?" Use this to introduce why responsible government and the split of powers exist even though they slow things down.
Notes Notes Section 1 (responsible government, Crown/Commons/Senate, riding); Retrieval Check items 1–3; Workbook Q1–Q3, Q7.
Class 2 — How Laws Are Made
Mock bill-passing: putting the six steps in order
Show The six unordered steps from Notes Section 2 (bill drafting, first reading, second reading, committee stage, third reading, Senate approval, royal assent) as separate cards or strips. Students physically sequence them before checking the notes table.
Say "Every one of these steps exists so that a law gets debated, checked, and re-checked by different people before it applies to you. It's slow on purpose — that's the same idea as responsible government from last class, just applied to individual laws instead of the whole government."
Notes Notes Section 2, legislative process table; Retrieval Check item 1.
Beyond the formal process: orders in council and regulations
Show A recent-sounding example of a government decision that didn't require a brand-new bill (a regulation change, a ministerial order) — doesn't need to be a real named example, a plausible generic one works.
Say "Not everything government does goes through those six steps. Orders in council, regulations, and ministerial decisions let governments act under authority Parliament already gave them — that's how so much actually happens day to day without a new law every time."
Notes Notes Section 2, misconception box; Retrieval Check item 2; Workbook Q9.
Class 3 — Federalism and Natural Resources
Sorting federal vs. provincial responsibilities
Show The word bank from Notes Section 3 (Defence, Education, Banking and Currency, Healthcare, Trade and Commerce, Property and Civil Rights, Criminal Law, Municipalities, Citizenship, Natural Resources) as cards to sort into two columns.
Say "Canada splits power between federal and provincial governments on purpose — it's called federalism. Neither level can just take over the other's responsibilities. Watch for the ones that feel like they should be federal because they're 'important' — healthcare and education are actually provincial."
Notes Notes Section 3, sorting table; Workbook Q4.
Natural resources and why this matters in Alberta
Show The Connect Back box from Notes Section 3, prompting students to reread their Post-War Canada notes on Western alienation and the National Energy Program.
Say "Provinces control their own natural resources — who develops them, how, and who collects the revenue. For Alberta that's the whole economy. But when a resource decision touches something national, like the environment or interprovincial trade, you get a real jurisdictional fight. That's not a glitch in the system — it's what federalism looks like when the lines get contested."
Notes Notes Section 3, natural resources paragraph and Connect Back box; Retrieval Check items 1–2; Workbook Q5, Q8.
Class 4 — The Judiciary and Workbook Part A
Courts as a check on government
Show The three-tier court table from Notes Section 4 (lower courts, appeal courts, Supreme Court of Canada).
Say "The judiciary is the third check we've looked at this unit. Parliament makes laws, but courts decide whether those laws actually follow the Constitution — and the Charter of Rights and Freedoms gives them the power to strike a law down if it violates protected rights. That's a real limit on what an elected government can do, even with a majority."
Notes Notes Section 4, court-level table and Charter paragraph; Retrieval Check item 1; Workbook Q6.

Show Workbook Q1–Q6, with the word bank at the top of Part A visible.
Cue Circulate and prompt students stuck on Q5 (natural resources/Alberta) back to their Section 3 notes rather than answering for them.

Misconception Management

MisconceptionWhen to addressStrategy
"The Prime Minister can pass any law they want."Class 1Reinforce that all three parts of Parliament — the Crown, House of Commons, and Senate — must approve a bill, and that responsible government means the executive can lose power entirely if it loses the confidence of the House.
"All government rules are made the same way, through Parliament passing a full law."Class 2Contrast the six-step formal legislative process against orders in council, regulations, and ministerial decisions — faster tools that operate under authority Parliament has already granted, without a brand-new bill each time.
Students conflate the Prime Minister's role with a president's — assuming the PM is directly elected by the whole country and holds power independent of Parliament.Class 1Point out the PM is an MP first, elected only in their own riding, and holds office only as long as their party can command the confidence of the House — there is no separate national vote for the position.
Students assume healthcare and education must be federal responsibilities because they feel too important to be "just" provincial.Class 3Run the sorting activity before revealing answers, then explicitly flag healthcare and education as provincial — importance and jurisdiction are not the same thing.
Students think the Charter gives courts the power to write or rewrite laws they disagree with.Class 4Clarify the distinction: courts interpret and can strike down a law that violates the Charter, but they don't get to draft replacement legislation — that job stays with Parliament and the legislatures.

Discussion and Source Activity Notes

Opening discussion — "Why not just let one person decide?" (Class 1):

  • Pose it before any notes are taken, as a genuine question, not a rhetorical one — let a few students argue for speed/efficiency before pivoting to accountability.
  • Bridge directly into responsible government: slower isn't a flaw in the system, it's the system working as designed.
  • Keep this short (5 minutes) — it's a hook for the lesson, not the lesson itself.

Mock bill-passing simulation (Class 2):

  • Physically sequence the six steps as cards or strips before revealing the correct order from the notes table — students remember the sequence better after guessing wrong at least once.
  • Optionally assign roles (a "committee," a "Senate," a Governor General for royal assent) to a sample class rule and walk it through all six steps for a concrete feel.
  • Close by asking what would be lost if a government skipped straight from first reading to royal assent — this sets up the orders-in-council contrast that follows.

Federal/provincial jurisdiction sorting activity (Class 3):

  • Hand out the word bank terms as individual cards; have pairs sort them into federal and provincial columns before checking against the notes table.
  • Deliberately don't pre-warn students about the counterintuitive ones (healthcare, education) — let the sort surface the misconception naturally, then correct it.
  • Follow immediately with the Connect Back prompt on Western alienation and the National Energy Program so students see the sort has real stakes, not just a category exercise.
  • Use Workbook Q8 (federal water law vs. Alberta) as the debrief scenario — remind students it's intentionally unresolved.

Assessment Notes

Formative check: The Retrieval Check items embedded in each notes section (especially the "cover your notes, answer from memory" prompts) are a fast daily signal — use them as an exit-ticket check before moving to Workbook Part A. Workbook Q1–Q6 (Part A) confirm the same core knowledge in a graded format once students have had a chance to self-correct.

What a strong answer looks like: On Workbook Q8 (the federal water law vs. Alberta scenario) and the Unit Synthesis paragraph, a strong answer doesn't just pick a side — it names the actual jurisdictional tension (provincial authority over resources vs. a legitimate federal national-interest claim) and uses at least one specific concept from the unit (federalism, natural resources, responsible government, the judiciary) to justify the position taken.

Watch for: Answers that state a side confidently but never explain why — e.g. "Alberta should win because it's their resources" with no reference to federalism or jurisdiction. Also watch for students treating Q7's school-principal scenario as a simple right/wrong identification without connecting it back to the confidence-of-the-House mechanism specifically.

Using the workbook: Q7 uses a school analogy to make "responsible government" concrete. Q8 is intentionally unresolved — Alberta's provincial authority over resources and the federal government's national-interest argument are both real and defensible, so grade for reasoning, not for a particular side. Q9 works well paired with the Class 2 misconception discussion on orders in council.
