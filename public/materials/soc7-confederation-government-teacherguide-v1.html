Teacher Guide

Creating Canada's Government

Grade 7 Social Studies  ·  Systems · Suggested pacing 5–6 weeks, 3 classes a week (~17 classes)

Suggested length: ~17 classes (5–6 weeks, 3 classes a week)    Tier: 1    Unit colour: Indigo (Systems)

Guiding question: How was Canada's system of government created at Confederation?

Learning outcome: Students examine how the Quebec Conference, London Conference, and the British North America Act (1867) led to the creation of Canada. Local skill codes CG1–CG5 are in the course context file.

Big idea for students to leave with: Canada's government was designed on purpose, by people who had to compromise. The plan they wrote in 1864 still decides who runs your school, who prints your money and how your MP gets to Ottawa.

What this unit is really about: Students arrive with American TV civics: "we vote for the leader," "senators are elected," "the courts and the president are separate." This unit builds Canada's own design from the problem it solved (deadlock) up through the three branches and federalism. Keep the depth at the 1867 structure. The full law-making process, orders in council and the Charter belong to Grade 9; Social 8 compares Canada with the U.S. in detail.

Lesson Sequence

Section 1 — Designing a Country (3 classes)
Opening: Pre-Knowledge Check and the deadlock
Show Pre-Knowledge Check, in silence. Then a quick simulation: split the class into "Canada West" and "Canada East" with equal votes, and ask them to decide something they disagree on.
Say "When two equal sides can't agree, nothing gets decided. That's a deadlock, and it's exactly where the Province of Canada was in 1864."
Notes Notes p. 1, Section 1 misconception box and flowchart. Feeds Workbook Q9.
Three conferences
Show The flowchart with the conferences blanked. Students fill them as you tell the story.
Say "At Quebec they wrote 72 resolutions: how laws would be made, how regions would be represented, and who would have which powers. That's the blueprint for everything else in this unit."
Notes Section 1 Retrieval Check. Feeds Workbook Q2.
Section 2 — The Ideas Behind the Plan (2 classes)
Three big ideas
Show The three-card diagram. Give each card a classroom example (class council election, a vote of no confidence, more reps for a bigger grade).
Say "Each idea answers a question about power: who makes the laws, who keeps the government in check, and how seats are shared."
Cue "Canada West had more people but the same seats. Which idea would they want?" (Rep by pop.)
Notes Section 2 Retrieval Check. Feeds Workbook Q5, Q12.
Section 3 — The Three Branches (2 classes)
Make, carry out, interpret
Show The three-pillar diagram with the jobs covered. Ask students to match school roles (student council, principal, referee for disputes) to the pillars first.
Say "Splitting the jobs means no one part of government holds all the power. In Canada two of the pillars lean on each other: the PM and Cabinet sit in Parliament."
Notes Section 3 Retrieval Check. Feeds Workbook Q1, Q8 and home sheet "The three branches."
Section 4 — Inside Parliament (4 classes)
Refutation first: who do we vote for?
Show A sample federal ballot for a local riding. Ask: "Where's the Prime Minister's name?"
Say "You vote for your MP. The party with the most MPs forms the government, and its leader becomes Prime Minister."
Notes Section 4 misconception boxes. Feeds Workbook Q3, Q10.
The Senate and the Governor General
Show The Parliament structure diagram with "elected" and "appointed" blanked.
Say "Only MPs are elected. Senators are appointed and review every bill. The Governor General signs laws, but acts on the advice of the PM and Cabinet."
Cue "Why give the Maritimes 24 senators, the same as Ontario?" Link back to rep by pop.
Notes Section 4 Retrieval Check. Give the mid-unit quiz after this section.
Section 5 — Federalism (3 classes)
Who does what?
Show A pile of cards (schools, the army, money, hospitals, criminal law, cities, citizenship, natural resources). Groups sort them into Ottawa and Edmonton before seeing the table.
Say "French-speaking Canada East and the Maritimes wanted to keep control of things close to home, like schools. Federalism was the compromise."
Notes Section 5 table and Retrieval Check (CG2, CG3). Feeds Workbook Q6, Q11 and home sheet "Federal or provincial?"
Section 6 — The Courts (2 classes)
One law, one top court
Show Two "referees" in different classrooms make opposite calls on the same play. Ask how a league would fix that.
Say "The BNA Act allowed a general court of appeal. Parliament created the Supreme Court in 1875, but until 1949 some cases still went to London. That's sovereignty arriving step by step."
Notes Section 6 Retrieval Check (CG5). Feeds Workbook Q7, Q13.
Consolidation (1 class)
Unit Synthesis
Show Unit Synthesis from memory, then reread the Pre-Knowledge Check.
Notes Workbook S1–S3, then the unit test.

Discussion and Source Activity Notes

Inquiry option 1 — Confederation conference simulation (best after Section 5)

  • Assign delegations: Canada West, Canada East, Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, PEI, Newfoundland.
  • Each delegation gets a goal card (e.g. Canada East: protect French language and Catholic schools; PEI: don't get swallowed by bigger colonies).
  • Negotiate three questions: how seats are shared, what the Senate looks like, which powers go to the provinces. Compare the class's deal with the real BNA Act.

Inquiry option 2 — Build-a-Parliament

  • Students build a model or poster of Parliament with role cards: MP, senator, PM, Cabinet minister, Governor General, judge.
  • Each card says how the person gets the job and what they do.

Inquiry option 3 — "Who handles it?" campaign

  • Collect 20 real local or national news items. Sort them federal or provincial, then make a poster explaining the three hardest calls.

Assessment Notes

Formative checkpoints: exit tickets after each section. Workbook Q3 and Q10 are the clearest test of whether "we vote for the PM" has gone; a student who still reaches for it needs Section 4 retaught.

What a strong extended response looks like: on Test Q14 and Workbook S3, a strong answer explains what each event contributed (the problem it solved or the feature it created), not just the order of events.

Connections: Building the Canadian State uses the BNA Act; Armed Forces returns to "sovereignty developed gradually" with the navy, Vimy and the Statute of Westminster. Grade 8 compares Canada's system with the American one, and Grade 9 goes deeper into how laws are made and the Charter.
