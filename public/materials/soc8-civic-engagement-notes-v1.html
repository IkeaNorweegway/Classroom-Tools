Citizens and Democratic Citizenship

A citizen is a person who legally belongs to a country. Being a citizen comes with   (like voting) and   (like following laws and staying informed).

Democratic citizenship means more than just living in a democracy — it means being an active member of one. A democratic citizen does not just follow the rules. They help   them.

Civic engagement is any action a person takes to help their community, their country, or the world. Civic engagement is how democratic citizenship actually happens — it is citizenship in  .

The Three Parts of Engaged Citizenship

Engaged citizenship has three parts. Each part needs the one before it.

An issue is a problem or a question that affects a group of people and does not have one easy answer. Issues can be local, national, or global.

Misconception

"The only real way to help your community or country is to vote."

Riverbend's town council is deciding whether to fix the playground next month. The next election is not for two more years. If voting were the only real way to influence a decision, nobody could do anything about the playground until then — and by then, the decision will already be made. But Malik did not wait. He wrote a letter and started a petition. Voting also is not available to everyone: most students in this class cannot vote yet. If voting were the only meaningful action, none of them could be engaged citizens right now.

Correct understanding: Voting is one important form of civic engagement — but it is only one of many. The list below shows several others.

Misconception

"You have to be an adult (18 or older) to really get involved in issues."

Look at the list of forms below. Only one form on the entire list requires being 18 — voting, which you will learn about in the next section. Every other form is open to a person of any age, including students in this class right now.

Correct understanding: Age is a barrier for voting specifically, not for civic engagement in general.

Out of every form listed here, only voting has an age requirement. That is worth remembering going into the next section.

How Much Time Does Each Form Take?

Forms of civic engagement range from a single decision (what you buy) to an ongoing time commitment (campaign volunteering). None of these require being 18.

Look back at Section 2. Voting was the one form of civic engagement with an age requirement. This section explains exactly what that requirement is — and what else is required.

Voting is one of the most basic responsibilities of democratic citizenship. When citizens vote, they choose who   them and helps make decisions on their behalf.

Misconception

"If someone meets most of the requirements, they should still be allowed to vote."

Look at Sofia and Liam above. Sofia meets three of the four requirements — she is missing only age. Liam also meets three of four — he is missing only citizenship. If "most" were good enough, both of them could vote. But Canadian law requires all four, with no exceptions.

Correct understanding: All four voter eligibility requirements must be met at the same time. There is no partial credit for voting.

Misconception

"Global issues like migration, drought, or conflict happen far away and don't really affect life in Canada."

A drought overseas shrinks a country's wheat harvest. Wheat is bought and sold around the world, so a smaller harvest pushes the global price of wheat up — including the price Canadian grocery stores pay. Your family's bread costs more because of a drought you never even heard about. Or consider migration: in 1979, war and hardship pushed many people to leave Vietnam, often by boat. Canada welcomed close to 60,000 Vietnamese refugees over the next few years. Many settled in cities across the country, started businesses, and raised families. Their children and grandchildren are Canadian citizens today — they are neighbours, classmates, and coworkers. That is not "far away." That is Canada.

Correct understanding: Global issues connect to Canada in real, traceable ways — through the price of goods, through the people who come here, and through Canada's role in responding to problems that cross borders.

Six Global Issues That Affect Life in Canada

Not every global issue reaches Canada the same way. Some raise prices (economic disruption, drought/famine). Some bring new community members (migration). Some do both, plus disrupt the flow of goods (civil unrest, war).
