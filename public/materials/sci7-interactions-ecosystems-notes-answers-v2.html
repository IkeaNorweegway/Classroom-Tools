Section 1: Ecosystems and Their Components

Vocabulary

Term Model definition Example
Ecosystem All the living and non-living things in an area and the interactions between them A boreal forest; a prairie slough
Biotic factor Any living part of an ecosystem Aspen tree, wolf, mushroom, bacteria
Abiotic factor Any non-living part of an ecosystem Water, temperature, sunlight, soil
Habitat The place where an organism lives A beaver's pond; a burrowing owl's grassland burrow
Population All the individuals of one species living in an area All the wolves in Jasper National Park
Community All the populations of different species living in an area The wolves, elk, willows, and beavers of a river valley

Retrieval Check — Section 1

  1. Biotic = living (e.g., plants, animals, fungi, bacteria); abiotic = non-living (e.g., water, temperature, sunlight, soil). Two examples of each required.
  2. Individual → Population → Community → Ecosystem
  3. Any two valid differences, e.g., precipitation/moisture (boreal is wetter, grassland is drier); sunlight reaching the ground (shaded forest floor vs. open grassland); wind exposure; soil type.
  4. It leaves out plants, fungi, and bacteria, all the abiotic factors, and the interactions between them. An ecosystem is biotic + abiotic + interactions.
  5. Stretch: Abiotic changes: higher temperature, more evaporation, drier soil, less surface water, possibly less snow cover. Biotic effects: drought-stressed plants, fewer plant species, less food for grazers, species that cannot adapt move or die out, new (possibly invasive) species move in. Full answers link an abiotic change to a biotic effect.

Section 2: Energy Flow — Producers, Consumers, and Decomposers

Vocabulary

Term Model definition Example
Producer An organism that makes its own food, usually from sunlight Grass, aspen, algae
Consumer An organism that gets energy by eating other organisms Hare, lynx, wolf
Primary consumer A consumer that eats producers (herbivore) Snowshoe hare, moose
Secondary consumer A consumer that eats primary consumers Lynx, fox
Tertiary consumer A consumer that eats secondary consumers Great horned owl, golden eagle
Decomposer An organism that breaks down dead material and returns nutrients to the soil Bacteria, fungi
Food chain A single path showing who eats whom and where the energy goes grass → hare → lynx
Food web Many connected food chains showing all the feeding relationships in an ecosystem Boreal forest food web
Trophic level An organism's feeding position in a food chain Producers are level 1; herbivores are level 2

Retrieval Check — Section 2

  1. A producer makes its own food through photosynthesis. Producers are essential because they are the only way energy enters the food web — every consumer depends on them directly or indirectly.
  2. Secondary consumer (third trophic level): grass = producer, deer = primary consumer, wolf = secondary consumer. Correction: the v1 key said "tertiary consumer", which is wrong for this three-link chain.
  3. Accept either answer if it is well explained. Strongest answer: decomposers do get their energy from other organisms (so they are consumers in the broad sense), but they are treated as their own group because they feed on dead material and return nutrients to the soil rather than eating living prey.
  4. The arrows show the direction of energy flow — from the organism being eaten to the organism that eats it.
  5. Stretch: Any three linked effects, e.g., dams fail → ponds drain → wetland habitat is lost → waterfowl, fish, frogs, and muskrats decline; willow and aspen are no longer cut (more food for moose and hares at first); wolves lose a prey species and shift pressure to others; water table drops and streamside plants change.

Retrieval Check — Section 3

  1. Only about 10% of the energy at one trophic level is passed on to the next level; about 90% is used for life processes and lost as heat.
  2. Primary consumers: 5,000 units. Secondary consumers: 500 units.
  3. Pyramid, widest at the bottom: grass 10,000 → grasshoppers/ground squirrels 1,000 → meadowlarks/foxes 100 → hawks/coyotes 10. Accept any correct Alberta grassland organisms.
  4. So little energy reaches the top level that a predator must hunt over a large area to find enough prey.
  5. Stretch: (a) Meadowlarks lose their food and decline or move away. (b) Coyotes lose some prey, but not all coyotes are equally affected — coyotes are generalists, so those that can switch to voles, hares, or ground squirrels are less affected than those relying on meadowlarks. Strong answers also note that grass may increase with fewer grasshoppers grazing it.

Section 4: Biodiversity

Vocabulary

Term Model definition Example
Biodiversity The variety of living things in an area A wild meadow with dozens of plant and insect species
Species richness The number of different species in an area A pond with 12 fish species is richer than one with 3
Keystone species A species with a much larger effect on its ecosystem than its numbers suggest Beaver, gray wolf
Ecological niche An organism's whole role: what it eats, when it is active, how it reproduces, where it lives A great horned owl is a night-time hunter of small mammals
Habitat The place where an organism lives A wetland for a beaver

Retrieval Check — Section 4

  1. Biodiversity is the variety of living things in an area — the number of different species, the genetic variety within them, and the variety of ecosystems.
  2. With more species there are more backups: if one species declines, others can fill its role, so the food web keeps working.
  3. A keystone species has an effect far larger than its numbers suggest. Examples: beaver (creates wetlands), gray wolf (controls elk), grizzly bear (spreads seeds, turns soil).
  4. Habitat is only where an organism lives. Niche is its whole role — what it eats, when it is active, how it reproduces, and how it interacts with other species.
  5. Stretch: It shows a chain of effects (a trophic cascade): removing one keystone predator changed elk numbers and behaviour, which changed the plants, which changed beavers, songbirds, and even the riverbanks. Species are connected through the food web, and a keystone species affects organisms it never eats.

Section 5: Environmental Change

Vocabulary

Term Model definition Example
Natural disturbance A natural event that changes an ecosystem Forest fire, flood, drought
Ecological succession The gradual recovery of an ecosystem, as simple communities are replaced by more complex ones Fireweed, then shrubs, then forest after a fire
Invasive species A species introduced to an ecosystem where it did not evolve, that spreads and harms native species Purple loosestrife, zebra mussels
Habitat loss The destruction or breaking up of the place where a species lives Grassland converted to cropland
Species at risk A species in danger of disappearing from an area Woodland caribou, burrowing owl

they often lack ___ predators natural The key reason populations grow unchecked

Retrieval Check — Section 5

  1. Any two with recovery, e.g., fire — fire-adapted species return and new growth appears within years (succession); drought — drought-adapted plants regrow when rain returns; flood — sediment adds nutrients and plants regrow.
  2. An invasive species has no natural predators, parasites, or diseases in its new ecosystem, so its population grows unchecked and it outcompetes or eats native species.
  3. Small separated patches isolate populations, block migration and movement, reduce breeding between groups, and create more edge habitat. Species that need large continuous areas (such as woodland caribou) cannot survive in fragments.
  4. Any species from the notes table with its risk and a protection measure, e.g., burrowing owl — grassland has been converted to farmland, so it loses nesting burrows and hunting area; protecting remaining native prairie and burrowing mammals is needed.
  5. Stretch: The claim ignores cumulative effects. Many "small" cuts add up and fragment the forest; some species (woodland caribou) need large continuous old forest; ecosystems have tipping points beyond which they do not recover. Strong answers accept that the forest is large but explain why size alone does not prevent harm.

Term Model definition Example

Retrieval Check — Section 6

  1. Any two services with the consequence of replacing them, e.g., water filtration by wetlands — a treatment plant costs millions to build and run; pollination — hand or machine pollination is impractical at farm scale; carbon storage — industrial carbon capture is extremely expensive.
  2. TEK is knowledge built from generations of careful observation of specific ecosystems. It is valuable because it records long-term patterns and relationships in places that Western science has studied for a much shorter time.
  3. Stewardship means taking responsibility for keeping the land and its ecosystems healthy for present and future generations — caring for them, not just using them.
  4. Stretch: Three sentences that name one ecosystem type and include (a) an ecosystem service, (b) a biodiversity principle such as protecting species variety or a keystone species, and (c) Indigenous stewardship or TEK, e.g., "Protect prairie wetlands because they filter water for free. Keep the surrounding native plants so the wetland supports many species and stays resilient. Work with local First Nations knowledge keepers on when and how to manage water levels and burning."

Unit Synthesis

  1. Food web drawing. Must include at least 2 producers (e.g., aspen, grass), 3 consumers at different levels (e.g., snowshoe hare, lynx, great horned owl), and decomposers. Arrows point from the organism eaten to the organism that eats it.
  2. Labels. T1 = producers; T2 = primary consumers; T3 = secondary consumers; T4 = tertiary consumers. The 10% rule matters most on the arrow from producers to primary consumers — the largest amount of energy (about 90% of the biggest total) is lost there, and it limits everything above. Accept another arrow if the reasoning is sound.
  3. Threat explanation (3–4 sentences; threat plus cascading effects):
    • Forest fire: removes producers, so herbivores lose food and shelter and predators follow; nutrients return to the soil and succession rebuilds the web over years.
    • Zebra mussels: filter out the plankton at the base of the web, so native mussels and small fish lose food and larger fish decline; no natural predators, so numbers explode.
    • Removing wolves: elk increase and overgraze streamside willow and aspen, so beavers and songbirds decline and riverbanks erode.
