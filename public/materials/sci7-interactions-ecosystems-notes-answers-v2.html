Section 1: Ecosystems and Their Components

Learning target: I can describe the biotic and abiotic components of an ecosystem and identify Alberta ecosystem types.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Ecosystem All the living and non-living things in an area and the interactions between them A boreal forest; a prairie slough
Biotic factor Any living part of an ecosystem Aspen tree, wolf, mushroom, bacteria
Abiotic factor Any non-living part of an ecosystem Water, temperature, sunlight, soil
Habitat The place where an organism lives A beaver's pond; a burrowing owl's grassland burrow
Population All the individuals of one species living in an area All the wolves in Jasper National Park
Community All the populations of different species living in an area The wolves, elk, willows, and beavers of a river valley

Guided Notes

An ecosystem is all the living things (biotic) and non-living things (abiotic) in an area, and the interactions between them. An ecosystem is more than just a list of species — it includes the energy and nutrients that flow through it.

Biotic factors are all living components of an ecosystem. Examples: plants, animals, fungi, bacteria (yes, bacteria and fungi count).

Abiotic factors are all non-living components. Examples: water, temperature, sunlight, soil, air.

Tip:

Abiotic factors do not just provide a backdrop — they determine which living things can survive in an ecosystem. Water availability, temperature range, and sunlight levels set the limits.

Levels of organization in ecosystems:

Level What it includes Example
Individual one single organism one wolf
Population All the individuals of one species living in an area All the wolves in Jasper National Park
Community All the populations of different species living in an area The wolves, elk, willows, and beavers of a river valley
Ecosystem All the living and non-living things in an area and the interactions between them A boreal forest; a prairie slough

Alberta's major ecosystem types:

Ecosystem Key features Representative species
Boreal forest coniferous forest (spruce, pine) mixed with aspen; long cold winters, short summers; many lakes and wetlands wolves, moose, black bears
Grassland dry, few trees, dominated by grasses; hot summers, cold winters, windy; fertile soil pronghorn, burrowing owls
Wetland land covered or soaked with water for all or part of the year (marshes, bogs, fens); filters and stores water great blue herons, beavers
Mountain conditions change quickly with elevation; cold, thin soil, heavy snow; forest on lower slopes, alpine meadow and bare rock higher up grizzly bears, mountain goats
Parkland (foothills) transition zone between grassland and forest; aspen groves mixed with open grassland highest biodiversity in Alberta

Where do humans fit? Humans are part (members) of the ecosystems we inhabit. We depend on ecosystem services including: clean water, clean air, food, and carbon storage.

Section 2: Energy Flow — Producers, Consumers, and Decomposers

Learning target: I can describe the roles of producers, consumers, and decomposers, and construct and interpret food chains and food webs.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Producer An organism that makes its own food, usually from sunlight Grass, aspen, algae
Consumer An organism that gets energy by eating other organisms Hare, lynx, wolf
Primary consumer A consumer that eats producers (herbivore) Snowshoe hare, moose
Secondary consumer A consumer that eats primary consumers Lynx, fox
Tertiary consumer A consumer that eats secondary consumers Great horned owl, golden eagle
Decomposer An organism that breaks down dead material and returns nutrients to the soil Bacteria, fungi
Food chain A single path showing who eats whom and where the energy goes grass → hare → lynx
Food web Many connected food chains showing all the feeding relationships in an ecosystem Boreal forest food web
Trophic level An organism's feeding position in a food chain Producers are level 1; herbivores are level 2

Guided Notes

How does energy enter an ecosystem?

Energy enters most ecosystems through sunlight (the Sun). Organisms that make their own food using sunlight are called producers (also called autotrophs). Most producers use photosynthesis to convert light energy into chemical energy (food / glucose).

Consumer types:

Consumer level What it eats Also called Alberta example
Primary consumer producers only herbivore snowshoe hare
Secondary consumer primary consumers carnivore / omnivore lynx (also fox, coyote, hawk)
Tertiary consumer secondary consumers top predator / apex predator (carnivore) great horned owl (also wolf, golden eagle, cougar)
MISCONCEPTION

"Decomposers are just garbage collectors — the ecosystem could work without them." Why this is wrong: Decomposers (bacteria, fungi) are not optional cleanup crew. They are the organisms that break down dead material and return nutrients to the soil. Without them, nutrients would be locked permanently in dead matter and producers would run out of the minerals they need to grow. Decomposers complete the nutrient cycle — they are as essential as any producer or consumer.

Food chains show energy flow in a single (linear / straight-line) path:

grass → snowshoe hare → lynx → great horned owl

Arrows mean: "the food (prey) is eaten by the consumer (predator) is eaten by e.g., "grass is eaten by snowshoe hare"" (energy flows in the direction of the arrow)

Food webs are more complex (realistic). In a real ecosystem, most organisms eat several things and are eaten by several things. A food web shows all these connections at once.

Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. What is a producer? Why are producers essential to all other trophic levels?
    Answer A producer makes its own food through photosynthesis. Producers are essential because they are the only way energy enters the food web — every consumer depends on them directly or indirectly.
  2. A wolf eats a deer that ate grass. What trophic level is the wolf?  
    Answer Secondary consumer (third trophic level): grass = producer, deer = primary consumer, wolf = secondary consumer. Correction: the v1 key said "tertiary consumer", which is wrong for this three-link chain.
  3. True or False: A decomposer is a type of consumer. Explain your answer.
    Answer Accept either answer if it is well explained. Strongest answer: decomposers do get their energy from other organisms (so they are consumers in the broad sense), but they are treated as their own group because they feed on dead material and return nutrients to the soil rather than eating living prey.
  4. In the food chain: willow → beaver → wolf → decomposers — what do the arrows represent?
    Answer The arrows show the direction of energy flow — from the organism being eaten to the organism that eats it.
  5. Stretch: If all the beavers in a wetland ecosystem disappeared, predict at least three cascading effects on the food web. (Hint: beavers build dams that create wetland habitat.)
    Answer Stretch: Any three linked effects, e.g., dams fail → ponds drain → wetland habitat is lost → waterfowl, fish, frogs, and muskrats decline; willow and aspen are no longer cut (more food for moose and hares at first); wolves lose a prey species and shift pressure to others; water table drops and streamside plants change.

Section 3: Energy Pyramids and the 10% Rule

Learning target: I can explain why energy decreases up the food chain and interpret an energy pyramid diagram.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Energy pyramid A diagram showing how much energy is available at each trophic level Wide producer base, tiny top-predator tip
Trophic level A feeding position in a food chain Producers = level 1; top predators = level 4
Biomass The total mass of living things at a level or in an area All the grass in a field

Guided Notes

The problem with energy transfer:

When energy moves from one trophic level to the next, approximately 90% of the energy is lost. Only about 10% of the energy at one level is available to the level above it.

Where does the lost energy go? heat (given off as the organism uses energy for life processes) (used for movement, growth, body heat, digestion).

The Energy Pyramid:

An energy pyramid shows the amount of energy available at each trophic level. The base is widest / largest (the producers) and each level above is smaller / narrower (less energy).

Fig. — Energy pyramid. Fill in the energy amounts for each level using the 10% rule.

Label the pyramid above. Add approximate energy amounts if producers have 10,000 units.

MISCONCEPTION

"Top predators like wolves and eagles have the most energy because they eat everything below them." Why this is wrong: At each level, about 90% of the energy is lost. By the time you reach the top predator, only a tiny fraction of the original energy is available. A field of grass may contain 10,000 units of energy → the deer/hare feeding on it can access ~1,000 → the wolf/eagle feeding on them can access ~100. Top predators are energy-poor compared to producers. This is why top predators need large territories — there simply isn't much energy available at the top.

Why are there fewer organisms at the top of a food web?

Because of the 10% rule, there is less energy available at each higher level. Supporting a population of wolves requires far more land (habitat) area than supporting a population of herbivores (hares, deer).

Connection to biodiversity:

If a species at a critical trophic level is removed, the entire web can collapse (be disrupted). This is why keystone species — organisms with a disproportionately large effect on their ecosystem — are so important.

Example of a keystone species in Alberta: beaver (also wolf, grizzly bear) — because its dams create wetland habitat that many other species depend on

    Answer Primary consumers: 5,000 units. Secondary consumers: 500 units.

    Answer Pyramid, widest at the bottom: grass 10,000 → grasshoppers/ground squirrels 1,000 → meadowlarks/foxes 100 → hawks/coyotes 10. Accept any correct Alberta grassland organisms.

  4. Explain why top predators require large territories compared to herbivores.
    Answer So little energy reaches the top level that a predator must hunt over a large area to find enough prey.
  5. Stretch: A disease kills most of the grasshoppers in an Alberta grassland. Using your knowledge of energy pyramids and food webs, predict what will happen to (a) the meadowlarks that eat grasshoppers and (b) the coyotes that eat meadowlarks. Will all coyotes be equally affected? Explain.

Section 4: Biodiversity

Learning target: I can explain what biodiversity is, why it matters, and describe the concept of a keystone species.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Biodiversity The variety of living things in an area A wild meadow with dozens of plant and insect species
Species richness The number of different species in an area A pond with 12 fish species is richer than one with 3
Keystone species A species with a much larger effect on its ecosystem than its numbers suggest Beaver, gray wolf
Ecological niche An organism's whole role: what it eats, when it is active, how it reproduces, where it lives A great horned owl is a night-time hunter of small mammals
Habitat The place where an organism lives A wetland for a beaver

Guided Notes

What is biodiversity?

Biodiversity refers to the variety of living things in an area — including the number of different species, the genetic variation within each species, and the variety of ecosystem (habitat) types.

Why does biodiversity matter?

A more biodiverse ecosystem is generally more resilient (stable / resistant) to disturbance. If one species disappears, other species can fill its role. An ecosystem with only one or two species at each trophic level is fragile — removing one species can collapse the food web.

Connection:

Think about a single-crop farm. A monoculture is the opposite of biodiversity — every plant is genetically identical. A single pest or disease can wipe out the entire crop. A wild meadow with many plant species is far more resistant to the same pest.

Keystone species have a disproportionately large (very large) effect on their ecosystem relative to their population size. Examples:

Keystone species Ecosystem Why they are critical
Grizzly bear Mountain/boreal Disperse seeds, turn soil, regulate prey populations
Beaver Wetland/boreal dams create wetlands that provide habitat for many species, store water, and slow floods
Gray wolf Boreal/grassland control elk and deer numbers and movement, which lets streamside plants recover

Ecological niches: No two species occupy exactly the same niche. A niche is not just where an organism lives (its habitat) — it is everything about how it lives: what it eats, when it's active, how it reproduces. When two species compete for the same niche, one will eventually outcompete the other (the other dies out or moves away).

Alberta biodiversity highlights:

  • Alberta has over about 1,700 native vascular plants species of plants, about 590 (roughly 400 birds, 95 mammals, 65 fish, 10 amphibians, 8 reptiles) species of vertebrates
  • The parkland/foothills transition zone has the highest biodiversity in the province
  • Alberta has supply the current number from the Alberta or federal species-at-risk list species listed as at risk at the federal or provincial level
Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. Define biodiversity in your own words.  
    Answer Biodiversity is the variety of living things in an area — the number of different species, the genetic variety within them, and the variety of ecosystems.
  2. Why is a biodiverse ecosystem more stable than a low-diversity one?
    Answer With more species there are more backups: if one species declines, others can fill its role, so the food web keeps working.
  3. What is a keystone species? Name one from Alberta and explain why it qualifies.
    Answer A keystone species has an effect far larger than its numbers suggest. Examples: beaver (creates wetlands), gray wolf (controls elk), grizzly bear (spreads seeds, turns soil).
  4. A student says "habitat and niche mean the same thing." Correct this misunderstanding.
    Answer Habitat is only where an organism lives. Niche is its whole role — what it eats, when it is active, how it reproduces, and how it interacts with other species.
  5. Stretch: The gray wolf was eliminated from Alberta's national parks for much of the 20th century. Elk populations grew dramatically. Willows and aspens along riverbanks were heavily grazed. Beavers declined because their food source declined. Riverbanks eroded without willow roots. When wolves were reintroduced, elk returned to moving more and avoiding riverbanks — plants recovered. What does this sequence illustrate about keystone species and food web connections?
    Answer Stretch: It shows a chain of effects (a trophic cascade): removing one keystone predator changed elk numbers and behaviour, which changed the plants, which changed beavers, songbirds, and even the riverbanks. Species are connected through the food web, and a keystone species affects organisms it never eats.

Section 5: Environmental Change

Learning target: I can compare natural and human-caused environmental changes and describe their effects on ecosystems.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Natural disturbance A natural event that changes an ecosystem Forest fire, flood, drought
Ecological succession The gradual recovery of an ecosystem, as simple communities are replaced by more complex ones Fireweed, then shrubs, then forest after a fire
Invasive species A species introduced to an ecosystem where it did not evolve, that spreads and harms native species Purple loosestrife, zebra mussels
Habitat loss The destruction or breaking up of the place where a species lives Grassland converted to cropland
Species at risk A species in danger of disappearing from an area Woodland caribou, burrowing owl

Guided Notes

Natural disturbances are part of the normal cycle of an ecosystem. Examples:

Disturbance Effect on ecosystem Recovery
Forest fire burns vegetation, returns nutrients to the soil, opens the canopy to sunlight new growth within years; fire-adapted species return
Flood drowns plants, erodes banks, moves or kills animals, leaves a layer of sediment nutrient-rich sediment helps plants regrow; recovery within a season to a few years
Drought plants die back, ponds and streams shrink, animals leave or die plants regrow from roots and seeds when rain returns; many prairie species are drought-adapted
Disease outbreak a sharp drop in one species' population, which affects its predators and prey resistant survivors reproduce and the population rebuilds over generations

After a natural disturbance, ecosystems typically undergo ecological succession: a gradual process of recovery where simpler communities are replaced by progressively more complex ones over time.

Human-caused changes are often more rapid (severe / widespread / permanent) and more difficult to reverse:

Human impact Mechanism Example in Alberta
Habitat loss/fragmentation land is cleared or split into small patches, so species lose living space and cannot move between patches boreal forest cleared for agriculture or oil sands
Pollution harmful substances enter the air, water, or soil and poison organisms or build up in the food chain tailings ponds, agricultural runoff
Invasive species introduced species outcompete or prey on native species, with no natural predators to control them zebra mussels (threat), European starlings (established)
Overharvesting organisms are removed faster than the population can reproduce over-trapping, commercial fishing
Climate change temperature and precipitation patterns shift faster than species can adapt or move permafrost thaw, earlier springs, shifted migration
MISCONCEPTION

"Nature always recovers — it's resilient." Why this is wrong: Ecosystems have tipping points — thresholds beyond which recovery does not happen. Examples: once a coral reef bleaches repeatedly, the structural community collapses and does not rebuild on human timescales; once grassland is broken by cultivation, returning it to native prairie takes decades of active restoration; once a species goes extinct, it is gone permanently. Resilience is real but limited.

Invasive species are organisms introduced to an ecosystem where they did not evolve. Because they often lack natural predators, their populations can grow unchecked and outcompete or prey on native species.

Alberta at-risk invasive species:

  • Zebra mussels — not yet established; prevention program at borders; would devastate native bivalves and clog water infrastructure
  • Purple loosestrife — wetland plant; creates dense monocultures that eliminate native vegetation and waterfowl nesting habitat

Alberta species at risk:

Species Risk reason Status
Woodland caribou Habitat fragmentation from roads and industrial activity Threatened
Burrowing owl Grassland conversion to agriculture Endangered
Swift fox Hunting + habitat loss; reintroduction program Threatened
Bull trout Water temperature, sedimentation Threatened
Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. Name two natural disturbances and explain how ecosystems recover from them.
    Answer Any two with recovery, e.g., fire — fire-adapted species return and new growth appears within years (succession); drought — drought-adapted plants regrow when rain returns; flood — sediment adds nutrients and plants regrow.
  2. What makes an invasive species so harmful compared to a native species?
    Answer An invasive species has no natural predators, parasites, or diseases in its new ecosystem, so its population grows unchecked and it outcompetes or eats native species.
  3. Why might habitat fragmentation be harmful even if the total amount of habitat stays the same?
    Answer Small separated patches isolate populations, block migration and movement, reduce breeding between groups, and create more edge habitat. Species that need large continuous areas (such as woodland caribou) cannot survive in fragments.
  4. Choose one Alberta species at risk. In 2–3 sentences, explain why it is at risk and what is needed to protect it.
    Answer Any species from the notes table with its risk and a protection measure, e.g., burrowing owl — grassland has been converted to farmland, so it loses nesting burrows and hunting area; protecting remaining native prairie and burrowing mammals is needed.
  5. Stretch: "Alberta's boreal forest is so large that logging in one area doesn't matter — it's a drop in the bucket." Evaluate this claim using your knowledge of ecosystems, tipping points, and species at risk.
    Answer Stretch: The claim ignores cumulative effects. Many "small" cuts add up and fragment the forest; some species (woodland caribou) need large continuous old forest; ecosystems have tipping points beyond which they do not recover. Strong answers accept that the forest is large but explain why size alone does not prevent harm.

Section 6: Sustainability and Stewardship

Learning target: I can describe what ecosystem services are and explain different approaches to sustainable stewardship of ecosystems.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Ecosystem services The benefits healthy ecosystems provide to people for free Wetlands filtering water; bees pollinating crops
Stewardship Taking responsibility for caring for the land so it stays healthy Protecting a wetland; restoring native prairie
Sustainability Meeting today's needs without reducing the ability of future generations to meet theirs Harvesting trees no faster than they regrow
Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) Knowledge of specific ecosystems built up by Indigenous peoples over many generations of observation Blackfoot burning practices for grassland health

Guided Notes

Ecosystem services are the benefits that functioning ecosystems provide to humans — for free. They include:

Service type Examples
Provisioning (supplying materials) food, fresh water, timber
Regulating (controlling processes) clean air, water filtration, climate regulation (also flood control, pollination, carbon storage)
Cultural (non-material value) recreation, spiritual value
Supporting (underlying processes) nutrient cycling, soil formation

When ecosystems are damaged, these services become costly or impossible to replace. Example: wetlands filter water for free; replacing that filtration with a treatment plant costs millions of dollars.

Approaches to stewardship:

Indigenous land stewardship traditions are grounded in the understanding that people (humans) are responsible for maintaining the health of the land they depend on. Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) is built from generations (thousands of years) of careful observation of specific ecosystems.

TEK and Western science are complementary — they ask different questions and observe at different scales. Where they overlap, their combined knowledge is stronger than either alone. Example: Blackfoot burning practices for grassland health — intentional burning maintained the open grassland that bison needed; contemporary fire management draws on this knowledge.

Sustainability means meeting present (current) needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. For ecosystems, this means harvesting, disturbing, or changing at a rate that the ecosystem can recover from (sustain / replace).

Connection to your pre-knowledge check:

Go back and read your answer to Question 4 — "Do you think humans are part of an ecosystem?" How has your thinking changed?

Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. List two examples of ecosystem services. For each, explain what would happen if we tried to replace the service technologically.
    Answer Any two services with the consequence of replacing them, e.g., water filtration by wetlands — a treatment plant costs millions to build and run; pollination — hand or machine pollination is impractical at farm scale; carbon storage — industrial carbon capture is extremely expensive.
  2. What is Traditional Ecological Knowledge and why is it valuable to modern environmental science?
    Answer TEK is knowledge built from generations of careful observation of specific ecosystems. It is valuable because it records long-term patterns and relationships in places that Western science has studied for a much shorter time.
  3. In your own words: what does it mean for humans to be stewards of an ecosystem?
    Answer Stewardship means taking responsibility for keeping the land and its ecosystems healthy for present and future generations — caring for them, not just using them.
  4. Stretch: Design a 3-sentence policy recommendation for managing one Alberta ecosystem type. Your recommendation must reference at least one ecosystem service, one biodiversity principle, and one element of Indigenous stewardship or TEK.
    Answer Stretch: Three sentences that name one ecosystem type and include (a) an ecosystem service, (b) a biodiversity principle such as protecting species variety or a keystone species, and (c) Indigenous stewardship or TEK, e.g., "Protect prairie wetlands because they filter water for free. Keep the surrounding native plants so the wetland supports many species and stays resilient. Work with local First Nations knowledge keepers on when and how to manage water levels and burning."

  • Food web drawing. Must include at least 2 producers (e.g., aspen, grass), 3 consumers at different levels (e.g., snowshoe hare, lynx, great horned owl), and decomposers. Arrows point from the organism eaten to the organism that eats it.
  • Labels. T1 = producers; T2 = primary consumers; T3 = secondary consumers; T4 = tertiary consumers. The 10% rule matters most on the arrow from producers to primary consumers — the largest amount of energy (about 90% of the biggest total) is lost there, and it limits everything above. Accept another arrow if the reasoning is sound.
  • Threat explanation (3–4 sentences; threat plus cascading effects):
    • Forest fire: removes producers, so herbivores lose food and shelter and predators follow; nutrients return to the soil and succession rebuilds the web over years.
    • Zebra mussels: filter out the plankton at the base of the web, so native mussels and small fish lose food and larger fish decline; no natural predators, so numbers explode.
    • Removing wolves: elk increase and overgraze streamside willow and aspen, so beavers and songbirds decline and riverbanks erode.
