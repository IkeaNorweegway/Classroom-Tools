Teacher notes

Squares & Cubes

Grade 7 Mathematics · Number · 4 lessons + quiz · Outcomes 7N3.1, 7N3.2 · Codes SC1–SC4

Overview

Big idea: a perfect square is a number of tiles that makes a square, and a perfect cube is a number of small cubes that makes a cube. The power notation comes from the picture: 5² is a 5 by 5 square (two 5s multiplied), 4³ is a 4 by 4 by 4 cube (three 4s). Roots read the picture backwards: √49 is the side of a square of 49 tiles, ∛64 is the edge of a cube of 64 small cubes. The problems in Section 4 are the same pictures with units: the fence (perimeter, m), the grass (area, m²) and the ice (volume, m³).

Sequence: perfect squares as square regions, base and exponent, recall to 12² (SC1) → square roots as the side length, checking by squaring, recall to √144 (SC2) → perfect cubes as cubic regions built in layers, cube roots as the edge, recall to 5³ (SC3) → perimeter and area of squares, volume of cubes, working forwards and backwards (SC4).

Materials: square tiles (at least 36 per pair; 144 for a class demonstration), linking or centimetre cubes (at least 27 per pair; 64 or 125 for a demonstration), grid paper, a Rubik's cube if you have one, a metre stick or tape for a 1 m² square on the floor.

Depth ceiling: natural numbers only. Perfect squares to 144 (12²), perfect cubes to 125 (5³), and their principal roots. Identifying that a number is not a perfect square (20 is between 16 and 25) is in; estimating √20 or using a calculator for non-perfect roots is Grade 8. No negative bases in powers (that is exponent work for Grades 8 and 9), although (−4)(−4) appears in retrieval as integer review.

Reading level: student pages are written at about Grade 3–4. The math words (perfect square, squared, power, base, exponent, square root, perfect cube, cubed, cube root, edge, perimeter, area, volume, square unit, cubic unit) are the learning; teach each with the tiles or cubes in hand.

Lessons

Lesson 1 — Perfect squares · 60 min
DOPre-knowledge check (5 min). Look at Q3–4 (area vs perimeter of a rectangle) and Q6 (counting cubes). These predict Section 4.
SHOWHand out tiles. "Make a square with 9 tiles. With 12 tiles. With 16." Record which numbers work.
SAY"A number of tiles that makes a square is a perfect square. A 5 by 5 square is 5 × 5. We write it 5² and say five squared."
DOThe base/exponent key box, then Worked Example 1 (Rubik's cube face, 3²).
WATCH5² = 10. Use the misconception box right after Worked Example 1, with tiles.
DOWorked Example 2 (36 hay bales, a 6 by 6 layer) and Worked Example 3 (20 is not a perfect square: it sits between 16 and 25).
CUEFill the 1–12 table together. Students say the squares aloud in order; it is the recall target for the year.
DOYour turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 1.
Lesson 2 — Square roots · 60 min
DOWarm-up interleaved: 8², 11², "is 50 a perfect square?", (−5)(−5).
SHOWA square of 49 tiles with the side hidden. "How long is one side?"
SAY"The side is the square root. √49 asks: what number times itself makes 49? Squaring goes side to area; the root goes area to side."
DOThe flow chart (side → square → area → root → side), then Worked Example 1 (64 hay bales).
WATCH√16 = 8. Use the misconception box before Worked Example 2. "Check by squaring" every time.
DOWorked Example 2 (factor pairs of 36: the equal pair is the root) and Worked Example 3 (√(9²) = 9). Fill the roots table.
DOYour turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 2. Quiz next class (SC1–SC2).
Lesson 3 — Perfect cubes and cube roots · 60 min
DOMid-unit quiz (25 min) first. Then the lesson.
SHOWLinking cubes: build 1, 2 by 2 by 2 and 3 by 3 by 3 cubes. "How many small cubes in each?"
SAY"A cube has the same length, width and height. 3 × 3 × 3 is 3³, three cubed. Build it as layers: one layer is a square, 3 × 3 = 9. Three layers make 27."
DOWorked Example 1 (Rubik's cube: one face is 3² = 9 stickers, the whole looks like 3³ = 27 cubes).
WATCH2³ = 6. Use the misconception box with a row of 6 cubes beside the 2 by 2 by 2 cube.
DOWorked Example 2 (Ice on Whyte, a 4 by 4 by 4 stack = 64 blocks), then cube roots: ∛125 = 5.
WATCH∛27 = 9. Lift a 9-cube layer off the 3 by 3 by 3 cube.
CUE"Square: two of them. Cube: three of them." Compare 4² = 16 and 4³ = 64 in the table.
DOYour turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 3.
Lesson 4 — Area, perimeter and volume problems · 60 min
DOWarm-up interleaved: √81, 5³, ∛8, "a rectangle 6 by 2: area and perimeter?"
SHOWA 1 m by 1 m square taped on the floor. Walk the edge (perimeter, 4 m). Fill it with students' feet or paper (area, 1 m²).
SAY"The fence is the perimeter, a length in m. The grass is the area, squares in m². The ice is the volume, cubes in m³."
WATCHArea and perimeter mixed up. Use the 4 cm misconception box (both are 16, different units).
DOWorked Example 1 (garden area 49 m² → side 7 m → fence 28 m) and Worked Example 2 (fence 32 m → side 8 m → area 64 m²). Both go "backwards" first.
DOWorked Example 3 (2 m ice block: 8 m³), the "edge × 3" misconception, and Worked Example 4 (125 cm³ box → edge 5 cm).
CUEThe five-step flow chart: fence, inside or solid? Then square, root, cube or cube root? Then the units.
DOCore worksheet; Challenge for early finishers. Exit ticket Section 4.

Assessment

Mid-unit quiz (22 marks)

SC1 (Q1–4), SC2 (Q5–9). Given at the start of Lesson 3, after squares and square roots. Q9 is the explain-your-thinking item. Mark the reasoning in Q4 and Q8: a student who says "20 is not a perfect square" without naming 16 and 25 has the fact but not the reason.

Worksheets and exits

Core (★) covers SC1–SC4 with a tile, cube or garden picture on most questions. Challenge (★★) adds numbers that are both squares and cubes (1, 64), area–perimeter comparisons, two-step backwards problems (fence → area, volume → edge → face area) and error analysis. Each misconception has at least one targeted question; the key names it.

Data and contexts used

  • Rubik's cube: a standard cube is 3 by 3 by 3 with 9 stickers per face. It looks like 27 small cubes; the real puzzle has 26 visible pieces and a central core. The notes say "looks like" on purpose.
  • Ice on Whyte is a real winter festival on Whyte Avenue in Edmonton with ice carving. The 4 by 4 by 4 stack of blocks and the 2 m cube-shaped block are made up for the math. Real carving blocks are usually rectangular.
  • Hay bales: small "square" bales are really rectangular boxes. The problems use them only for square layers (counts), never for volume.
  • The Lethbridge community garden plot (49 m²), the goat pen (32 m of fence) and the 125 cm³ gift box are made up.

Connections

  • Integers (last topic): (−4)(−4) = 16 and (−2)(−2)(−2) = −8 appear in retrieval; negative bases in powers are not taught here.
  • Fractions & Decimals: order of operations includes squares and cubes (FD4).
  • Circles: A = πr² uses squaring; finding r from an area is a ★★ stretch with perfect squares.
  • Volume: a cube is a rectangular prism with equal edges; V = Bh gives e² × e = e³.
  • Grade 8: square roots of non-perfect squares (estimating between perfect squares) and the Pythagorean theorem.
