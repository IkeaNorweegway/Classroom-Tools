Teacher notes

Lines, Angles & Congruence

Grade 7 Mathematics · Geometry & Measurement · 4 lessons + quiz · Outcomes 7G1.1–7G1.3 · Codes LA1–LA4

Overview

Big idea: points, lines, rays, segments, angles and polygons are abstract objects; a pencil drawing is only a model of them, and the symbols say exactly which object is meant (the endpoint of a ray first, the vertex of an angle in the middle). Marks carry relationships: a small square for a right angle, matching arrowheads for parallel lines, hash marks for equal sides and arcs for equal angles. Where two lines cross, opposite angles are congruent and adjacent angles on a line are supplementary. Where a transversal crosses parallel lines, corresponding angles are congruent, and measuring a pair of corresponding angles is how students check whether two lines really are parallel. Congruent polygons have matching (corresponding) vertices, sides and angles, and the letter order in a statement like △ABC ≅ △RST records the match.

Sequence: objects and naming, with the symbol notation drawn over the letters; polygon or not (gap, crossing sides) (LA1) → parallel and perpendicular from railway rails, window panes and Edmonton's street grid; paper folding; reading arrowheads and right-angle squares; ∥ and ⊥ (LA2) → two crossing lines with tracing paper; opposite and adjacent angles; the transversal, its 8 angles and the corresponding pairs; using corresponding angles to check for parallel lines, including a pair that looks parallel and is not (LA3) → congruent polygons by tracing, hash marks and arcs, congruence statements, missing sides and angles in quilt pieces and roof trusses (LA4).

Materials: rulers, protractors, plain paper for folding, tracing paper (or patty paper), coloured pencils for marking corresponding angles, mini whiteboards. Optional: GeoGebra (geogebra.org/geometry) to drag a transversal and watch corresponding angles stay equal only while the lines stay parallel, and to drag a triangle onto its congruent copy.

Depth ceiling: 7G1.2 is limited to opposite (vertically opposite) angles, adjacent angles on a line (supplementary) and corresponding angles. Alternate angles, co-interior (same-side interior) angles, the triangle angle sum (8G2) and similarity are not taught; questions never need them. All angle answers are whole degrees, found by reasoning or by measuring. The congruence work names corresponding parts and solves for missing measures; there are no congruence conditions (SSS, SAS, ASA) and no formal proof.

Reading level: student pages are written at about Grade 3–4. The math words (point, line, ray, segment, endpoint, angle, vertex, polygon, intersect, parallel, perpendicular, right angle, arrowheads, opposite angles, adjacent angles, supplementary, congruent, transversal, corresponding angles, corresponding vertices and sides, hash marks, arcs) are the learning; teach each with a picture. Note that "corresponding" appears twice: matching angles on a transversal (LA3) and matching parts of congruent shapes (LA4). The notes flag this.

Notation on the page: line CD, ray AC and segment AB are written in words in the text, with the ↔, → and bar symbols drawn over the letters in the diagrams. Students should write the symbols over the letters by hand.

Watch forWhat to do
"A ray goes both ways" / "a line has end points"Count arrowheads: line two, ray one, segment none. A flashlight beam (ray) vs. a railway track between two stations (segment).
Ray BA written for ray ABThe endpoint goes first. Ray AB and ray BA point opposite ways.
The vertex in the wrong place (∠ABC for the angle at A)Trace the two rays with a finger; the letter where they meet goes in the middle.
"Lines that look parallel are parallel"Measure the gap at both ends of the near-rectangle in the notes (2.6 cm and 3 cm). Only arrowheads or a measurement settle it.
"Opposite angles add to 180°"Tracing paper: turn it halfway; ∠1 lands on ∠3. Then point at the straight line under ∠1 and ∠2: that pair makes 180°.
Mixing up which angles are correspondingColour the four regions at the top crossing, then the same four positions at the bottom crossing: "same spot, same colour".
"Corresponding angles are always equal"Show the non-parallel picture: 60° and 55°. Equal corresponding angles are a test for parallel lines, not a rule for every pair.
"The letter order in △ABC ≅ △RST doesn't matter"Write the statement, then draw arrows A → R, B → S, C → T. Check each arrow against the arcs and ticks.
Saying shapes with equal angles are congruentTwo squares, 3 cm and 4 cm: same angles, different sizes. Congruent needs equal sides too.

Lessons

Lesson 1 — Lines, rays, segments, angles, polygons · 60 min
DOPre-knowledge check (5 min). Q1 and Q3 recall angle types, Q2 and Q5 preview 180° subtraction, Q4 and Q6 polygon vocabulary.
SHOWA line, a ray and a segment through the same two points. Ask "where does each one stop?"
SAY"A drawing is a model. The real line goes on forever. The arrowheads say 'keeps going'."
DOThe naming key box. Students write each symbol over the letters on a whiteboard: ↔ CD, → AC, bar over AB, ∠CAB, △ABC.
WATCH"A ray goes both ways." Misconception box: count the arrowheads.
CUEWorked Example 1 (name each object), Worked Example 2 (three names for one angle), Worked Example 3 (polygon or not: a gap, crossing sides, a pentagon), Worked Example 4 (a roof truss as △ABC).
DOYour turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 1.
Lesson 2 — Parallel and perpendicular · 60 min
DOWarm-up interleaved: name a ray from a picture; 180 − 47; solve x + 70 = 180; 20% of 45.
SHOWRailway rails (parallel) and a window pane (perpendicular). Draw the arrowheads and the small square.
DOThe paper fold: two folds give perpendicular creases. Students check the four corners against a page corner.
SAY"Matching arrowheads mean parallel. A small square means a right angle. We write m ∥ n and m ⊥ n."
WATCH"Lines that look parallel are parallel." Misconception box: the gaps are 2.6 cm and 3 cm.
CUEWorked Example 1 (window pane: AB ∥ DC, AD ∥ BC, AB ⊥ BC), Worked Example 2 (Jasper Ave ∥ 102 Ave; 109 St ⊥ both), Worked Example 3 (a quilt trapezoid with one parallel pair).
DOYour turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 2.
Lesson 3 — Angles where lines cross · 60 min
DOWarm-up interleaved: write "p is perpendicular to q"; name the vertex of ∠XYZ; −15 ÷ 3; solve 3x = x + 40.
DOTracing-paper investigation: draw two crossing lines, trace, turn halfway around a pencil point. ∠1 lands on ∠3.
SAY"Opposite angles are congruent. Angles side by side on a line are supplementary: they add to 180°."
CUEWorked Example 1 (find three angles from 40°), Worked Example 2 (Peace Bridge X, given 115°).
WATCH"Opposite angles add to 180°." Misconception box.
SHOWA transversal across two parallel lines. Number the 8 angles; colour the corresponding pairs. Optional: GeoGebra, drag one line off parallel and watch ∠2 and ∠6 split.
CUEWorked Example 3 (a diagonal road across parallel avenues), Worked Example 4 (are the rails parallel? 70° and 70° vs. 75° and 70°).
WATCH"Corresponding angles are always equal." Misconception box: 60° and 55° on lines that are not parallel.
DOYour turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 3. Quiz next class (LA1–LA3).
Lesson 4 — Congruent polygons · 60 min
DOMid-unit quiz (25 min) first. Then the lesson.
DOTrace △ABC; slide, turn or flip it onto △RST. It fits, so they are congruent.
SAY"Hash marks match sides. Arcs match angles. The letter order in the statement records the match."
CUEWorked Example 1 (read the marks: △ABC ≅ △YXZ), misconception box (letter order), Worked Example 2 (flipped quilt pieces: missing sides and angles), Worked Example 3 (are they congruent? a turned rectangle vs. a longer one), Worked Example 4 (roof trusses).
WATCH"The letter order doesn't matter." Have students draw the match arrows A → Y, B → X, C → Z before writing the statement.
DOCore worksheet; Challenge for early finishers. Exit ticket Section 4.

Assessment

Mid-unit quiz (24 marks)

LA1 (Q1–4), LA2 (Q5–6), LA3 (Q7–10). Given at the start of Lesson 4, before congruent polygons (LA4). Q10 is the explain-your-thinking item: full marks need "no" and a reason that opposite angles are equal (a numeric example such as 50° and 50° is enough), ideally contrasted with adjacent angles on a line. On Q1 a ray named with the endpoint second loses the mark. On Q3 accept freehand drawings if the arrowhead and endpoints are clear. On Q9 accept "not parallel" only with the unequal corresponding angles named.

Worksheets and exits

Core (★) covers LA1–LA4 with a diagram on most questions: naming objects and angles, polygon or not, window-pane symbols, a near-parallel pair with no arrowheads, two crossing lines, a transversal on parallel avenues, a non-parallel check, matching parts from a congruence statement, flipped quilt pieces, and fixing a congruence statement from marks. Challenge (★★) adds rays on one line (ray AB = ray AC, ray BA ≠ ray BC), crossing angles x and 3x (an equation from topic 6), a corresponding angle written as (2x + 10)°, three avenues where only two are parallel, a truss perimeter, a statement starting with △CAB, and two squares with equal angles that are not congruent. Each misconception has at least one targeted question; the key names it. Exit tickets: one per section, each ending with an "explain how you know" item.

Data and contexts used

  • Edmonton street grid: in downtown Edmonton avenues run east–west and streets north–south; Jasper Avenue is 101 Avenue, so it is parallel to 102 Avenue, and 109 Street crosses both at right angles. The "diagonal road" is generic; roads such as Kingsway cut across the grid diagonally, but no real angle is claimed.
  • Calgary's Peace Bridge (over the Bow River) has a red steel lattice whose bars cross in X shapes. The 115° and 65° angles are made up for the example.
  • Railway rails as parallel lines, window panes, ladders, quilt blocks and roof trusses are generic. Truss sizes (8 m span, 35° pitch) and quilt pieces (6 cm, 4 cm, 3 cm, 5 cm) are made up but plausible.
  • Angle measures in diagrams match the drawings to within a degree; the non-parallel pictures differ by about 5°, so students who measure with a protractor should see the difference.

Connections

  • Linear Equations (topic 6): angle problems become equations, like x + 3x = 180 or 2x + 10 = 70.
  • Circles (topic 9): a full turn is 360°; the four angles at a crossing add to 360°.
  • Volume (topic 10): prisms have parallel, congruent bases and lateral edges perpendicular to the bases (right prisms).
  • Grade 8: the triangle angle sum (8G2), and similar shapes, which keep angles but not side lengths.
