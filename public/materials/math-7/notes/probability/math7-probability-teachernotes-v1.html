Teacher notes

Probability

Grade 7 Mathematics · Statistics & Probability · 4 lessons + quiz · Outcomes 7PR1.1, 7PR1.2 · Codes PB1–PB4

Overview

Big idea: an experiment has outcomes; an event is the set of outcomes we are looking for, and the outcomes that match it are favourable. Probability is a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). When the outcomes are equally likely, theoretical probability is favourable ÷ all, written as a ratio (favourable : all), a fraction, a decimal or a percent, and the probabilities across a sample space add to 1. Experimental probability is what actually happened ÷ the number of trials. A few trials can land far from the theory; many trials (or pooled class data) land close to it. Trials don't affect each other. Probability then supports decisions: is a game fair, which bag gives the better chance, what a 70% forecast means.

Sequence: outcomes, events, favourable outcomes, the 0-to-1 likelihood line, more/less likely, not-equally-likely situations (unequal spinner, a bag with more red, a bent coin) (PB1) → sample space as a list, P(event) = favourable ÷ all in four forms, splitting an unequal spinner into equal pieces, probabilities add to 1, P(not A) = 1 − P(A) (PB2) → trials, experimental probability from coin, spinner and bag data (with replacement), the coin has no memory, simulations with a coin and a die (PB3) → predicting counts from theory, few vs. many trials, pooling class data, fair games, choosing a bag, weather forecasts (PB4).

Materials: coins (one per student), dice (one per pair), paper-clip spinners on printed 4-part and unequal spinners, opaque bags with coloured counters (3 red + 1 blue, and 3 red + 2 blue per group), a class tally chart on the board or a shared spreadsheet for pooled data, calculators, mini whiteboards.

Depth ceiling: one experiment at a time (coin, die, spinner, bag of counters, simple number cards). Sample space as a list. P(event) as ratio, fraction, decimal and percent; P(not A). Experiments and simulations with many trials; pooled class data. Two-event (compound) probability, tree diagrams and outcome tables for two experiments are Grade 8: if a student asks about "a coin and a die together", say that is next year. Drawing without replacement is not used (it makes trials dependent). Odds are not taught.

Ratio wording: the draft asks for probability "as a ratio". These materials write it as favourable : all (4:5 for 4 red of 5 counters), which is a part-to-whole ratio. Students met part-to-part ratios in Ratios & Percents (topic 5), where "2:3 is not 2 out of 3" was a misconception. Say "favourable to all" every time you write a probability ratio so the two uses don't collide.

Reading level: student pages are written at about Grade 3–4. The math words (experiment, outcome, event, favourable outcome, simple event, equally likely, probability, impossible, certain, sample space, theoretical probability, trial, experimental probability, with replacement, simulation, predict, pool, fair game) are the learning; each has a picture or example beside it.

Diagrams: five new diagram types support this topic: `dice` (a row of die faces, some shaded), `bag` (a bag of coloured counters marked R/B/Y, with one drawn out), `spin` (a spinner with equal or unequal parts; `guides=1` splits a big part into equal pieces), `flips` (a row of coins or cards, with a dashed "?" for the next trial) and `trials` (experimental probability after more and more trials, with a dashed theory line). Results of trials use the existing `bar` type; the likelihood scale uses `numberline`.

Watch forWhat to do
"A probability can be 3 (or 120%)"Back to the meaning: favourable ÷ all. The favourable outcomes are part of all the outcomes, so the fraction can't be more than 1. Show the 0-to-1 line with nothing beyond 1.
Calling 0.5 "likely" or 0.2 "even"Keep the likelihood line on the wall. Even chance is exactly 1/2; likely is between 1/2 and 1.
"It either happens or it doesn't, so it's 50-50"Ask "how many faces are a 6? how many are not?" List the sample space and count. Two possibilities are not two equally likely outcomes.
Using favourable ÷ all on an unequal spinner (P(red) = 1/3 because there are 3 colours)Split the spinner into equal pieces first (the `guides` picture). Count pieces, not colours.
Writing the ratio as favourable : unfavourable (3:1 for 3 red, 1 blue)That is odds, not probability. Probability is favourable : all, 3:4.
Probabilities that don't add to 1Make a table of every outcome and add the column. If it isn't 1 (or 100%), something was missed or counted twice.
"After 5 heads, tails is due"The coin has no memory. Flip live: record what follows a run of 3 heads in the class data. It is about half and half.
Not putting the counter backWithout replacement the bag changes and each draw depends on the last. Always put it back and shake.
"If P = 1/2, 10 flips give exactly 5 heads"Every pair flips 10 coins and reports heads: the class sees 3s, 4s, 6s, 7s. Then pool: the total is close to half.
"Our result isn't the theory, so we did it wrong"Different is normal for a few trials. Compare the spread of group results with the pooled result.
"More red counters means a better chance"Compare the fractions, not the counts: 3 of 5 (60%) beats 5 of 10 (50%).
"It stayed dry, so the 70% forecast was wrong"70% is likely, not certain. On 3 days in 10 with that forecast, it stays dry.

Lessons

Lesson 1 — Outcomes, events and likelihood · 60 min
DOPre-knowledge check (5 min). Q1–Q3 fractions, decimals and percents (the four forms of a probability), Q4 1 − 0.4 (the complement), Q5 a fraction of a number (predicting), Q6 reading a bar graph (experimental results).
SHOWA die and a coin. "What can happen?" List the outcomes on the board.
SAY"Each possible result is an outcome. An event is what we are looking for. The outcomes that match it are favourable. A probability is a number from 0 to 1: 0 is impossible, 1 is certain."
DOStudents place events on a 0-to-1 line taped to the board (roll a 7, the sun rises tomorrow, a coin lands heads, roll a number less than 5).
CUEWorked Example 1 (greater than 4 on a die), Worked Example 2 (a bag of only red counters: certain and impossible), Worked Example 3 (the unequal Stampede spinner: more and less likely).
WATCH"A probability can be 3 (or 120%)." Misconception box with the number line from 0 to 1.5.
DOYour turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 1.
Lesson 2 — Theoretical probability · 60 min
DOWarm-up interleaved: favourable outcomes for "roll an odd number"; place "draw red from 2 red, 6 blue" on the line; 3/4 as a decimal and percent; the mean of 4, 6 and 8.
SHOWThe sample space table: coin {H, T}, die {1, …, 6}, 4-part spinner.
SAY"When the outcomes are equally likely, the probability is favourable ÷ all. We can write it four ways. All the probabilities in the sample space add to 1."
CUEWorked Example 1 (4 red, 1 blue: 4:5, 4/5, 0.8, 80%), Worked Example 2 (P(roll a 6) = 1/6 ≈ 0.17 ≈ 17%).
WATCH"It either happens or it doesn't, so it's 50-50." Misconception box (five faces are not a 6).
CUEWorked Example 3 (split red into 2 equal pieces: 2/4 = 1/2), Worked Example 4 (3 red, 2 blue, 5 yellow add to 1; P(not red) = 0.7), the 10 number cards.
DOYour turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 2. Quiz next class (PB1–PB2).
Lesson 3 — Experimental probability · 60 min
DOMid-unit quiz (25 min) first. Then the lesson.
SHOW"Theory says P(heads) = 1/2. What will happen if we flip 20 times?" Every student flips 20 times and records heads.
SAY"Each flip is a trial. Experimental probability is the number of times it happened ÷ the number of trials. It is what really happened."
CUEWorked Example 1 (40 spins: 12/40 = 0.3 vs. 0.25), Worked Example 2 (3 red, 1 blue with replacement: 72% vs. 75%). Groups run the bag experiment (20 draws each, put it back every time).
WATCH"After 5 heads, tails is due." Misconception box (the dashed 6th coin).
CUESimulations: Worked Example 3 (a coin for a 50-50 true/false guess), Worked Example 4 (a die for 6 equally likely Stampede toys: 20% vs. about 17%).
DOYour turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 3. Keep every group's coin and bag results for Lesson 4.
Lesson 4 — Comparing and deciding · 60 min
DOWarm-up interleaved: experimental P from 18 heads in 30 flips; P(not rain) for 0.4; is heads due after 4 tails?; 15% of 60.
SHOWThe Lesson 3 coin results from each group, then the pooled total. "Which is closest to 1/2?"
SAY"Theory predicts about what to expect: probability × trials. A few trials can be far off. Many trials, or everyone's data pooled, get close."
CUEWorked Example 1 (predict 3s in 120 rolls: about 20), the `trials` graph, Worked Example 2 (pool 5 groups: 53 heads in 100 flips).
WATCH"If P = 1/2, 10 flips give exactly 5 heads." Misconception box (10 flips, 7 heads).
CUEWorked Example 3 (a dice game that isn't fair, and the fix), Worked Example 4 (Bag A 60% beats Bag B 50%), Worked Example 5 (70% chance of rain in Calgary).
DOCore worksheet; Challenge for early finishers. Exit ticket Section 4.

Assessment

Mid-unit quiz (22 marks)

PB1 (Q1–4), PB2 (Q5–9). Given at the start of Lesson 3, before experiments (PB3). Calculators allowed. Q5 is the explain-your-thinking item: full marks need the count of equally likely outcomes (1 of 6) and the reason "happens / doesn't happen" are not equally likely. Q6: one mark per form; accept 3:8 as the ratio only if it is written favourable : all. Q8: P(red) on the unequal spinner needs the equal pieces; no marks for 1/3 (one of three colours). Q9: accept 0.7 or 70% or 7/10.

Worksheets and exits

Core (★) covers PB1–PB4 with a diagram on most questions: favourable outcomes, the likelihood line, a 130% chance of rain, P in four forms from a bag, P(roll a 5) and the 50-50 slip, an unequal spinner, probabilities that add to 1, experimental P from a spinner, the gambler's fallacy, predicting from theory, a fair game, and a forecast. Challenge (★★) adds a missing count from a probability, building a bag to match given probabilities, a die simulation of 3 equal choices, judging a "broken" coin from few vs. many trials, pooling data, changing a game to make it fair, and choosing between two bags. Each misconception has at least one targeted question; the key names it. Exit tickets: one per section, each ending with an "explain how you know" item.

Data and contexts used

  • The Calgary Stampede booth spinners, plush-toy prizes and "Spin to Win" game are invented for the math; real midway games are not this simple and their odds are not published here.
  • The 70% chance of rain in Calgary and 20% chance of snow in Edmonton are typical forecast wording from Environment and Climate Change Canada ("POP", probability of precipitation), not a real forecast.
  • All coin, die, spinner and bag results (12 of 40, 36 of 50, 53 of 100, Kai's 500 flips, and so on) are made up but plausible: they are within the normal spread for that many trials.
