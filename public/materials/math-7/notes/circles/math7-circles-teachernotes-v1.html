Teacher notes

Circles

Grade 7 Mathematics · Geometry & Measurement · 4 lessons + quiz · Outcome 7M1 · Codes CI1–CI4

Overview

Big idea: a circle is every point at one fixed distance (the radius) from a centre, so the radius alone sets its size; the diameter is two radii. Measuring real circles shows that C ÷ d is the same for every circle, a little over 3, which gives π as a measured ratio before it is a symbol. C = πd (or 2πr) follows directly, and run backwards gives d = C ÷ π. Cutting a circle into equal sectors and laying them tip up, tip down makes a near-parallelogram whose height is r and whose base is half the circumference, πr; so A = πr × r = πr². Problems then come down to two decisions: radius or diameter, and edge (a length) or inside (square units).

Sequence: parts of a circle, d = 2r, drawing from a radius with a compass or GeoGebra, comparing circles by radius (CI1) → measure C and d of real objects, C ÷ d ≈ 3.14, the rolling wheel, C = πd and C = 2πr, d from C (CI2) → r-squares (the circle covers a bit over 3 of them), cut-and-rearrange with 8, 12 and 16 sectors, A = πr², area from r or d (CI3) → edge or inside, centre-pivot irrigation (area in m² and hectares, distance the wheels travel), a running track's semicircular end, doubling the radius (4 times the area) (CI4).

Materials: compasses, rulers, string or thin ribbon, tape measures, calculators, a collection of round objects (jar lids, a hockey puck, paper plates, a hula hoop, a bike wheel if possible), paper circles about 16 cm across (two per student), scissors, glue sticks, chart paper, mini whiteboards. Optional: GeoGebra (geogebra.org/geometry) for the "Circle with Centre and Radius" tool and for dragging the radius while C and A update.

Depth ceiling: π ≈ 3.14 for every calculation; answers rounded to one decimal place unless the question says otherwise. Given r or d, find C and A; given C, find d. Finding r from an area is a ★ stretch only, and always lands on a perfect square (r² = 9, 16, 36, 100). No arc length or sector area beyond the area derivation; the running-track semicircle is "half of C", not an arc formula. No composite figures beyond a circle in a square or a lap of a track in the Challenge set. No 22/7 and no π key: if students use the π key, answers differ in the second decimal place, which is fine to discuss but not to mark.

Reading level: student pages are written at about Grade 3–4. The math words (circle, centre, radius, radii, diameter, circumference, compass, pi, π, ≈, formula, area, sector, r², parallelogram, square units, semicircle, hectare, centre pivot) are the learning; each has a picture beside it.

Notation: the notes write the formulas both ways, C = π × d and C = πd, and A = π × r × r and A = πr². Students should write the multiplication out when they substitute: A ≈ 3.14 × 4.5 × 4.5. The ≈ sign appears whenever 3.14 is used.

Watch forWhat to do
"The radius and the diameter are the same"Fold a paper circle in half: the fold is a diameter. Fold again: the new crease from the centre to the edge is a radius. Two radii lie along the first fold.
Opening the compass to the diameterBefore drawing, say the radius out loud: "8 cm across, so 4 cm on the compass." Check by measuring across the finished circle.
Using r in C = πdEstimate first: C is a bit more than 3 diameters. If 3.14 × r gives an answer smaller than 3 × d, it only went half way around.
"π is exactly 3.14" or "C ÷ d should come out to exactly 3.14"Measurement error. Pool the class data; the mean lands close to 3.14. π keeps going (3.14159…); 3.14 is a rounded value.
Using d in A = πr²Ask "radius or diameter?" before any calculation. Using d makes the area 4 times too big; compare 2826 cm² and 706.5 cm² for the same 30 cm pizza.
Squaring as × 2 (4.5² = 9)Write r² as r × r every time in this unit. Topic 3 (Squares & Cubes) misconception returns here.
"Circumference is in square units"String test: lay a string around the edge, then straighten it. It is a length. Area counts squares.
"Double the radius, double the area"Draw squares of side 2 and side 4 on grid paper first (4 and 16 squares). Then compute both circle areas: 12.56 m² and 50.24 m².
Rounding too earlyKeep all digits until the last step: 3.14 × 20.25 = 63.585, then 63.6. Rounding 20.25 to 20 first gives 62.8.

Lessons

Lesson 1 — Parts of a circle · 60 min
DOPre-knowledge check (5 min). Q1 previews halving, Q2 multiplying by 3.14, Q3 square units, Q4 perimeter (a length), Q5 area of a parallelogram (needed in Lesson 3), Q6 rounding to one decimal place.
SHOWA bike wheel (or the spokes picture). "Every spoke is the same length. What would happen if one were longer?"
SAY"A circle is all the points the same distance from the centre. That distance is the radius. The diameter goes across through the centre. It is two radii."
DOPaper fold: fold a circle in half (diameter) and in half again (radius). Measure both.
WATCH"The radius and the diameter are the same." Misconception box.
CUEWorked Example 1 (face-off circle, r = 4.5 m → d = 9 m), Worked Example 2 (pizza, d = 30 cm → r = 15 cm).
DOCompass practice on plain paper: radius 3 cm, then a circle 8 cm across (Worked Example 3: set to 4 cm). Students check by measuring across. Optional: GeoGebra "Circle with Centre and Radius".
CUEWorked Example 4 (Ben r = 2 cm vs. Tess d = 5 cm: compare radii).
DOYour turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 1.
Lesson 2 — Circumference and π · 60 min
DOWarm-up interleaved: find r from d = 14 cm; 3.14 × 2.5; 10²; 25% of 80.
DOMeasuring stations (20 min). Pairs measure C (string or tape) and d of 4–5 round objects and record C ÷ d. Pool the class data on chart paper.
SAY"Small or big, C ÷ d comes out a bit more than 3. Every circle. That number is pi, π. We use 3.14."
SHOWThe rolling wheel: mark a point on a lid or wheel, roll one turn along a metre stick, mark diameters along the track. Three diameters and a bit.
CUEWorked Example 1 (puck, d = 7.6 cm → C ≈ 23.9 cm, estimate 22.8 first), Worked Example 2 (bike wheel r = 33 cm → d = 66 cm → C ≈ 207.2 cm; the 2πr version).
WATCHUsing r in C = πd. Misconception box: 103.62 is only half way around.
CUEWorked Example 3 (poplar tree, C = 157 cm → d = 50 cm).
DOYour turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 2. Quiz next class (CI1–CI3 after Lesson 3, or CI1–CI2 if the class needs it sooner; see Assessment).
Lesson 3 — Area of a circle · 60 min
DOWarm-up interleaved: C for d = 10 cm; d for r = 2.5 m; 1.2 × 1.2; solve 3x + 4 = 19.
SHOWThe r-square picture. "How many of these squares cover the circle? More than 2? More than 4?" Agree on "a bit more than 3".
DOCut and rearrange (20 min). Each student cuts one paper circle into 8 sectors and one into 16, and glues them tip up, tip down. Label the height r and the base "half of C".
SAY"The height is the radius. The base is half the crust: half of 2πr is πr. Base times height: πr × r = πr²."
CUEWorked Example 1 (r = 10 cm: base 31.4 cm, A = 314 cm², then the rule), Worked Example 2 (face-off circle, r = 4.5 m → 63.6 m²).
WATCHUsing d in A = πr². Misconception box: the 30 cm pizza, 2826 cm² vs. 706.5 cm².
CUEWorked Example 3 (round hay bale end, d = 1.8 m → 2.5 m²).
DOYour turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 3.
Lesson 4 — Circle problems · 60 min
DOMid-unit quiz (25 min) first. Then the lesson.
SHOWThe pizza: crust = circumference (cm), cheese = area (cm²). Then the edge-or-inside table.
WATCH"Circumference is in square units." Misconception box.
CUEWorked Example 1 (centre pivot, 400 m arm → 502 400 m² = 50.24 ha), Worked Example 2 (the pivot's wheels: 2512 m per turn), Worked Example 3 (track curve, d = 73 m → 114.6 m).

WATCH"Double the radius, double the area." Misconception box: 12.56 m² vs. 50.24 m².

Assessment

Mid-unit quiz (24 marks)

CI1 (Q1–3), CI2 (Q4–7), CI3 (Q8–10). Given at the start of Lesson 4, before mixed problems (CI4). Calculators allowed; π ≈ 3.14. Accept answers from the π key if the working is shown (they differ in the second decimal). Q6: no mark for 3.14 × 35 as the final step (radius in C = πd). Q9: using the diameter (1808.64 cm²) earns only the first mark if r was found and then not used. Q10 is the explain-your-thinking item: full marks need half the crust on top and half on the bottom, and the link base × height = πr × r.

Worksheets and exits

Core (★) covers CI1–CI4 with a diagram on most questions: d from r, a radius mistaken for a diameter, setting a compass from a diameter, C from d and from r (rolling wheel), d from C, a radius–diameter–circumference table, A from r, a pizza area using the diameter, the sector parallelogram with numbers, a 300 m pivot, and C and A of one circle with units. Challenge (★★) adds a full track lap (two semicircles plus straights), doubling a crop circle's radius, the unwatered corners of a pivot field, tire turns per kilometre, r from an area (perfect square), a hay bale's diameter and end area from its circumference, and a circle vs. a square with the same perimeter. Each misconception has at least one targeted question; the key names it. Exit tickets: one per section, each ending with an "explain how you know" item.

Data and contexts used

  • Hockey: the puck is 3 inches (7.6 cm) across; NHL face-off circles and the centre circle have a 15 ft radius (4.57 m); the materials round it to 4.5 m.
  • Centre-pivot irrigation is common in southern Alberta (canola, potatoes, wheat). A quarter section is half a mile (about 805 m) on a side, so a pivot of about 400 m fits inside; the 800 m by 800 m field is rounded. A 400 m arm waters about 50 ha, which matches typical quarter-section pivots. The 300 m and 200 m arms are plausible shorter systems.
  • Running track: a standard 400 m track has curves with an inside radius of 36.5 m, so the 73 m diameter and 114.6 m curve are close to real. The Challenge track (70 m curves, 100 m straights, 419.8 m lap) is made up.
  • Round hay bales are commonly 5–6 ft across; 1.8 m and 1.6 m are realistic. Net wrap goes around the curved side.
  • The measuring table (puck, lid, plate, bike wheel, hula hoop) uses realistic sizes; the C values are rounded "student measurements" so C ÷ d varies from 3.13 to 3.16. A 26-inch bike wheel is about 66 cm across with its tire.
  • The poplar tree (C = 157 cm), pizza sizes, garden, sprinkler, tablecloth and crop circles are made up.

Connections

  • Squares & Cubes (topic 3): r² is a perfect square in most early problems; finding r from an area uses square roots of perfect squares.
  • Fractions & Decimals (topic 4): every calculation multiplies or divides decimals; rounding to one decimal place.
  • Lines, Angles & Congruence (topic 8): the diameter is a segment, the centre a point; a full turn is 360°.
  • Volume (topic 10): the area of a circle is the base area B of a cylinder: V = πr² × h.
  • Grade 8: surface area of cylinders uses both C (the label) and A (the two ends).
