Teacher notes

Fractions & Decimals (× and ÷)

Grade 7 Mathematics · Number · 4 lessons + quiz · Outcomes 7N4.1–7N4.4 · Codes FD1–FD4

Overview

Big idea: multiplying by a fraction takes part of something, so a product can be smaller than the number you started with. Dividing by a fraction counts how many fraction-sized groups fit, so a quotient can be bigger. Decimals are fractions with denominators of 10 and 100, so the same two pictures work for them: the area model for × (0.3 × 0.2 is 3 tenths of 2 tenths, 6 of 100 squares) and "how many fit" for ÷ (6 ÷ 0.5 is twelve halves). The order of operations then pulls integers, squares and cubes, fractions and decimals into one expression.

Sequence: fraction × whole number and fraction × fraction on an area model, the rule a/b × c/d = ac/bd, simplest and equivalent forms, the product of two proper fractions is less than each factor, mixed numbers in recipes (FD1) → whole ÷ fraction and fraction ÷ fraction as "how many groups", fraction ÷ whole as sharing, common denominators (a/b ÷ c/b = a ÷ c), reciprocals and × the reciprocal (FD2) → decimal × whole, decimal × decimal on area models and by counting decimal places, decimal ÷ whole as sharing, whole ÷ decimal and decimal ÷ decimal by making an equivalent division (× 10 or × 100), calculator for long division with an estimate check (FD3) → order of operations with integers, squares and cubes, fractions and decimals (FD4).

Materials: fraction strips or fraction bars (halves to twelfths), square paper or transparent grid overlays for area models, two colours of pencil crayon, base-ten blocks or hundred grids, play coins (quarters, dimes, 50¢ is a nice prop even though it no longer circulates widely), a 6 m rope or skipping rope and a metre stick for the cutting demo, calculators for FD3.

Depth ceiling: positive fractions and decimals only (signed fractions and decimals are Grade 8). Mixed numbers appear in problems and are changed to improper fractions first. Multi-digit decimal division uses a calculator; by hand, keep divisors to one digit after the equivalent-division step. Order of operations uses brackets, squares and cubes, × ÷ and + −; no other exponents and no nested brackets beyond one level.

Reading level: student pages are written at about Grade 3–4. The math words (product, quotient, divisor, reciprocal, area model, proper fraction, simplest form, tenth, hundredth, decimal place, estimate, equivalent division, expression, evaluate, order of operations, brackets) are the learning; teach each with a picture on the board.

Lessons

Lesson 1 — Multiplying fractions · 60 min
DOPre-knowledge check (5 min). Q2–3 (simplest form, mixed numbers) predict FD1 fluency. Q5 (decimals × whole numbers) and Q6 (6 + 4 × 2) preview FD3 and FD4.
SHOWFraction strips: three groups of 2/5. "How many fifths? How many wholes?" Then 8 pieces with half shaded: 1/2 of 8.
SAY"Of means times. Half of 8 is 1/2 × 8. Two thirds of three quarters is 2/3 × 3/4."
DOThe area model for 1/3 × 1/2 on square paper. Columns in blue for the second factor, rows in yellow for the first; the overlap is the product. Count the parts: rows × columns.
CUE"How many parts in the whole square? How many in the overlap?" The bottom is rows × columns; the top is shaded rows × shaded columns. The rule comes from the picture.
DOWorked Example 1 (half of 3/4 cup of milk). Then the "multiplying makes bigger" misconception box with the number line.
WATCHCommon denominators. Use the second misconception box: 1/2 × 1/3 with the area model shows one sixth, not a whole.
DOWorked Example 2 (simplify first or last, both give 1/3) and Worked Example 3 (11/2 batches of pancakes, partial area model). Your turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 1.
Lesson 2 — Dividing fractions · 60 min
DOWarm-up interleaved: 2/3 × 3/4, 1/2 × 10, 5 × 3/10, (−4) × 3.
SHOWThree paper strips. "How many quarter-strips can I cut?" Cut and count 12. Then a 6 m rope cut into 3/4 m pieces (or a skipping rope marked in quarters).
SAY"3 ÷ 1/4 asks how many quarters fit in 3. Dividing by a small piece gives a big count."
WATCH"Dividing makes smaller." Use the misconception box right after the opening picture; ask for a prediction (more or less than 3?) before each division.
DOWorked Example 1 (rope, 8 pieces). Then sharing: 1/2 pan of bannock among 3 on an area model (1/2 ÷ 3 = 1/3 of 1/2), and Worked Example 2 (3/4 pizza for 3 people).
DOSame denominators: 3/4 ÷ 1/8 = 6/8 ÷ 1/8 = 6. Worked Example 3 (shelves from a 11/2 m board: 12/8 ÷ 3/8 = 4).
SAY"How many quarters make one whole? Four. 1/4 × 4 = 1. Four is the reciprocal of a quarter." Fill the reciprocal table.
CUEKeep, change, flip the divisor. Show it agrees with the groups picture: 3 ÷ 1/4 = 3 × 4 = 12.
WATCHFlipping the first fraction. Misconception box, then check with the groups picture.
DOWorked Example 4 (2/3 ÷ 1/6 = 4, by reciprocal and by picture). Your turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 2. Quiz next class (FD1–FD2).
Lesson 3 — Multiplying and dividing decimals · 60 min
DOMid-unit quiz (25 min) first. Then the lesson.
SHOWThe ribbon receipt (3 × $1.25). Then a 10 × 10 grid: shade 3 columns (0.3) and 2 rows (0.2). "How many small squares in the overlap? What is one small square worth?"
SAY"0.3 is 3/10 and 0.2 is 2/10. 3/10 × 2/10 = 6/100 = 0.06. Count the decimal places: one plus one is two."
WATCH0.3 × 0.2 = 0.6. Use the misconception box with the hundred grid.
DOWorked Example 1 (30 L of gas at $1.45: estimate first) and Worked Example 2 (2.4 m × 1.5 m rug, four-part area model, checked with 24 × 15).
SHOW$4.50 shared by 3 (tape diagram). Then "how many halves in 6?" with jumps of 0.5 on a number line.
WATCH6 ÷ 0.5 = 3. Misconception box: count the 12 jumps.
SAY"Multiply both numbers by 10 and the answer stays the same. $6 in 50-cent coins is 600 cents ÷ 50 cents."
DOWorked Example 3 (4.8 m of ribbon, 0.6 m bows: 48 ÷ 6 = 8) and Worked Example 4 (quarters in $3: 300 ÷ 25 = 12, the same as 3 ÷ 1/4 from Lesson 2).
CUECalculators are fine for long divisions like 52.20 ÷ 36, but an estimate must come first.
DOYour turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 3.
Lesson 4 — Order of operations · 60 min
DOWarm-up interleaved: 0.4 × 0.2, 3 ÷ 1/2, 2/3 × 3/4, 5².
SHOWThe Stampede receipt. Ask half the class to work 2 + 3 × 4 left to right and half to multiply first. Compare 20 and 14; the receipt decides.
SAY"Brackets first. Then squares and cubes. Then × and ÷ from left to right. Then + and − from left to right."
WATCHStrict left to right, and "× before ÷". Both misconception boxes. Have students say "× and ÷ are a team."
DOWorked Example 1 (integers: −3 + 4 × (−2) = −11) and Worked Example 2 ((5 − 2)² × 2 = 18 vs 5 − 2² × 2 = −3).
DOWorked Example 3 (fractions: 1/2 + 1/2 × 1/4 = 5/8) and Worked Example 4 (West Edmonton Mall change: 30 − 2 × 12.50 = 5).
CUEStudents rewrite the whole expression after each step (the flow-chart pictures model this). Skipping the rewrite is where sign and order errors creep in.
DOCore worksheet; Challenge for early finishers. Exit ticket Section 4.

Assessment

Mid-unit quiz (24 marks)

FD1 (Q1–5), FD2 (Q6–10). Given at the start of Lesson 3, after multiplying and dividing fractions. Q10 is the explain-your-thinking item: full marks need the "how many groups fit" reasoning or a correct reciprocal step, not just "dividing makes it bigger." On Q4 accept any correct simplest-form route; a student who simplifies first and one who simplifies last should both get full marks.

Worksheets and exits

Core (★) covers FD1–FD4 with an area model, groups bar, hundred grid, receipt or flow chart on most questions. Challenge (★★) adds mixed-number recipes, multi-step sharing and cutting problems, money problems with estimates, equivalent division with × 100, and order-of-operations error analysis and bracket placement. Each misconception has at least one targeted question; the key names it.

Data and contexts used

  • Bannock and pancake recipes: the amounts (3/4 cup of milk, 2/3 cup of milk, 3/4 cup of flour) are made up and kept simple. Recipes vary widely by family and community; if students bring their own family recipes, use those amounts instead.
  • Gas at $1.45 per litre is a made-up but plausible Alberta pump price (prices in 2025–26 have mostly been in the $1.30–$1.60 range). Update it to today's local price if you like; 30 L and 40 L keep the arithmetic clean.
  • Ribbon at $1.25 per metre, the 2.4 m by 1.5 m rug, the 6 m rope and the 11/2 m shelf board are made up.
  • Calgary Stampede food truck ($2 drink, $4 hot dogs) and West Edmonton Mall shirts ($12.50) are made-up prices in real places. Real Stampede food costs more.
  • Quarters ($0.25) and the 50¢ coin are real Canadian coins; the 50¢ coin is rarely seen, so present it as "50 cents" if students don't know it.

Connections

  • Integers (topic 2): sign rules for × and ÷ return in FD4 (−3 + 4 × (−2)). Integer retrieval appears in each section.
  • Squares & Cubes (topic 3): squares and cubes are step 2 of the order of operations; (5 − 2)² vs 5 − 2² shows why brackets matter.
  • Positive & Negative Numbers (topic 1): fraction–decimal conversion (0.5 = 1/2, 0.25 = 1/4) links 6 ÷ 0.5 to 6 ÷ 1/2 and 3 ÷ 0.25 to 3 ÷ 1/4.
  • Ratios & Percents (next topic): scaling a recipe is multiplying by a fraction; unit rates (price per litre) are decimal division.
  • Circles and Volume: π ≈ 3.14 means decimal × decimal (A = 3.14 × r²), and V = Bh with decimal dimensions.
  • Grade 8: operations with negative fractions and decimals (rational numbers).
