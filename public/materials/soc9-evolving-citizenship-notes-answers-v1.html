Section 1: Defining Canadian Citizenship

Learning target: I can explore the different categories of citizenship status in Canada and explain why the Canadian Citizenship Act (1947) mattered.

Vocabulary

Guided Notes

Prior to the Canadian Citizenship Act (1947), people in Canada were considered British subjects — there was no separate legal category of "Canadian citizen." The Act defined who could hold or obtain Canadian citizenship, in response to increased international recognition and the increasingly diverse population of Canada after the Second World War.

Permanent residents and refugees in Canada are non-citizens with some of the same rights as citizens and some restrictions. Legislation has been amended over time to reflect Canadian pluralism and immigration from non-European countries — including the ability to pass on citizenship from one generation to the next, and inclusive point systems and designations such as economic, family, refugee, and humanitarian immigration classes.

Section 3: The Charter of Rights and Freedoms

Learning target: I can evaluate the impact the Constitution Act (1982) had on Canadian identity, and discuss historical and contemporary limits on rights and freedoms.

Guided Notes

Canada gained sovereign control over its own constitution through gradual patriation, culminating in the Constitution Act (1982), which entrenched the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms. The Charter defines individual and collective rights, including:

  • fundamental freedoms
  • democratic rights
  • mobility rights
  • legal rights
  • equality rights
  • official language rights
  • minority language educational rights
  • Canada's multicultural heritage
  • Indigenous peoples' rights

Section 1 of the Charter recognizes that reasonable limits may be placed on rights and freedoms — for example, censorship of hate speech such as racism, antisemitism, or Islamophobia. Changes to the Constitution Act and the Charter can be made through the amending formula, which allows the federal government to make additions, or the notwithstanding clause, which allows provincial governments to exempt certain sections. Attempts to amend the Constitution to encourage national unity include the 1987 Meech Lake Accord and the Charlottetown Accord.

Section 4: Rights and Responsibilities

Learning target: I can discuss the relationship between the rights and responsibilities afforded to Canadian citizens.

Guided Notes

Rights afforded to Canadian citizens come with responsibilities, including:

  • obeying the law
  • taking responsibility for oneself and one's family
  • serving on a jury
  • voting in elections
  • helping others in the community
  • protecting Canadian identity and the environment
