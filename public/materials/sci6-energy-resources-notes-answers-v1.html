Section 1: What Are Energy Resources?

Learning target: I can define energy resource, distinguish between renewable and non-renewable resources, and give examples of each in an Alberta context.

Notes

An energy resource is anything we use to do work, produce heat, or generate electricity.

Energy resources come in two categories:

Renewable resources are replenished naturally — they are replaced by natural processes fast enough that we do not run out of them.

Non-renewable resources were formed over millions of years. Once we use them, they cannot be replaced in a human lifetime.

Alberta context

Alberta has historically relied on oil / coal and natural gas — fossil fuels found in large amounts beneath the province. The oil sands in northern Alberta are one of the largest deposits of oil in the world.

Alberta is also growing its solar and wind energy sectors. Large wind farms operate in southern Alberta where wind speeds are high and consistent.

In 2018, Alberta began a coal phase-out — a plan to phase out coal-fired electricity generation. By 2023, coal is no longer used to generate electricity in Alberta.

Connection prompt

Think about where your home gets its heat and electricity. If you are not sure, ask someone at home or look it up. What energy resources are used to heat your home and power your lights?

Misconception — "Renewable = Clean"

Renewable means the resource is naturally naturally and will not run out. It does NOT mean the resource is environmentally harmless. Large hydroelectric dams flood entire river ecosystems. Wind turbines require land and affect bird migration. Solar panel manufacturing uses toxic materials. Renewable and clean are separate ideas — a resource can be one without being the other.

Section 2: Before and After Processing

Learning target: I can explain the difference between using an energy resource directly and using it after processing, and I can identify examples of each — including resources that are used both ways.

Vocabulary

TermDefinition in your own wordsOne example
Before processing  
After processing  
Conversion  

Notes

Some energy resources can be used directly in their natural form — before any processing or conversion happens.

Some energy resources must be converted into another form first — after processing — before we can use the energy in them.

This does NOT map cleanly onto renewable versus non-renewable. A resource can be renewable AND used before processing, or renewable AND used after processing. The two categories are independent.

Wind — the key example

Wind is a renewable resource that is used both before and after processing:

Before processingAfter processing
Wind A traditional windmill uses wind directly to turn a millstone (grinding grain) or pump water A modern wind turbine converts wind into electricity — a different form of energy

The windmill and the wind turbine both use wind, but in very different ways. The turbine adds a conversion step.

ResourceBefore processingAfter processing
WoodBurning logs directly to heat a roomConverting wood to charcoal (charcoal), which burns hotter and cleaner

Crude oil— (not used directly in natural form)Refined into gasoline / petrol, diesel, and other fuels
Moving waterA water wheel uses moving water directly to turn a millstoneA hydroelectric dam generates electricity
Biomass (grain/sugar)Eaten directly as food — the body extracts energyFermented and distilled into ethanol / biofuel fuel
Misconception — "Before processing = renewable, after processing = non-renewable"

This is not true. The before/after processing categories and the renewable/non-renewable categories are completely independent. Look at the examples:

  • Wind (renewable — renewable) can be used before processing (windmill) or after (turbine)
  • Wood (can be renewable) can be used before processing (burning logs) or after (charcoal)
  • Crude oil (non-renewable) is only used after processing — but that is not because it is non-renewable. It is because oil in its raw form is not useful as a fuel.

Do not mix these two classification systems. They answer different questions.

Section 3: Selecting Energy Resources

Learning target: I can name and explain the five factors used to select an energy resource, apply all five to a real Alberta decision, and explain why selecting an energy resource involves trade-offs and evidence.

Vocabulary

TermDefinition in your own wordsOne example
Availability  
Societal impact  
Economic impact  
Environmental impact  
Responsible management  

Notes

Choosing which energy resource to use is not simple. Many factors must be considered. Scientists, engineers, governments, and communities all weigh these factors when making energy decisions.

There are five selection factors:

Factor 1: Availability

Is the resource present in sufficient quantity at the location?

  • A region with consistent wind can use wind power effectively.
  • A region with little sunlight in winter may not get enough energy from solar panels year-round.
  • A community without a nearby river cannot use hydroelectric power.

Availability is about geography and season — does the resource exist in usable amounts, where and when it is needed?

Factor 2: Societal Impact

How does using this resource affect people and communities? Societal impact includes:

  • employment / jobs — does extracting or generating this energy create employment?
  • health effects — does the resource benefit or harm nearby communities?
  • Public acceptance — do people support or oppose this energy project in their community?
  • reliability — can this energy source deliver power consistently, or does it only work when the sun shines or wind blows?

Indigenous land stewardship principles align closely with this factor. First Nations and Métis communities in Alberta understand resources as being held in trust for future generations — not simply extracted for present-day benefit. Many First Nations and Métis communities have been significantly affected by oil sands development on or near their territories. Today, many of the same communities are leaders in renewable energy development on their lands, bringing both economic benefit and stewardship values to the work.

Factor 3: Economic Impact

What does it cost? Economic impact includes the cost to:

  • install the system (building a wind farm, installing solar panels, drilling for natural gas)
  • maintain the system (ongoing upkeep and repairs)
  • operate the system day-to-day (running costs)

A resource that is cheap to extract may be expensive to process. A resource that is expensive to install (e.g., solar) may cost almost nothing to operate once built. Economic decisions look at the total / lifetime cost over the full life of the project.

Factor 4: Environmental Impact

How does using this resource affect the environment? Environmental impact includes:

  • air quality — releasing greenhouse gases or particulates into the air
  • land use — how much land is disturbed or taken up?
  • ecosystem disruption — does extraction or infrastructure harm wildlife or ecosystems?
  • Water effects / quality / use — does the extraction process affect rivers, groundwater, or lakes?
Connection to Computer Science

Technology is designed to solve problems, but it always has unintended consequences. Smart grids, electric vehicles, and renewable energy infrastructure are computational artifacts — they are designed systems, built to reduce emissions and improve energy efficiency. But they have their own unintended consequences: mining rare earth metals for batteries and solar panels disturbs ecosystems; rapid expansion of electric vehicles creates e-waste; connecting more renewable sources to the electrical grid can cause grid instability when supply and demand are mismatched. Every technological solution creates new trade-offs that must be monitored and managed.

Factor 5: Responsible Management

How do we use the resource wisely over the long term? Responsible management includes:

  • conservation — using less energy overall, reducing waste
  • Long-term supply / planning — will this resource still be available and affordable in 50 years?
  • ongoing monitoring — using data to track the impacts of energy use and adjust as needed
  • Leaving options open for future generations — not locking in decisions that cannot be reversed

This factor connects directly to scientific methods: responsible management requires data collection (how much energy is being used?), evidence-based decisions (what do the data show about environmental impact?), and a willingness to update / revise / change decisions when evidence changes.

This factor also aligns with Indigenous land stewardship — the idea that the land, water, and resources we use today are held in trust, not owned. What we take now affects what is available to those who come after us.
