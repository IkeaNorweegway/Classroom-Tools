Part A: Cell Theory and Plant Cells (20 marks)

Q1 — Cell theory tenets: (3 marks)

  1. All living things are made of one or more cells.
  2. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in all living things.
  3. All cells come from pre-existing cells.

Q2 — Diagram labels: (5 marks)

  • a = cell wall (thick outer boundary)
  • b = cell membrane (thin inner boundary)
  • c = nucleus (dark oval in centre)
  • d = chloroplasts (green ovals)
  • e = central vacuole (large clear area)

Q3 — Two differences: (4 marks — 2 per difference)

Any two of:

  1. Plant cell has a cell wall; animal cell does not.
  2. Plant cell has chloroplasts; animal cell does not.
  3. Plant cell has a large central vacuole; animal cell has small or no vacuole.
  4. Plant cell is regular in shape, like a rectangle. Animal cell is irregular in shape.

Q4 — Cell identification: (3 marks)

Animal cell. Here is why: there is no cell wall. Plant cells always have a rigid cell wall. There are no chloroplasts. That fits an animal cell. The vacuole is small. Animal cells have small, uneven vacuoles, not the large central vacuole of a plant cell. All three clues point to an animal cell.

Q5 — Importance of cell theory: (5 marks)

Cell theory matters for many reasons. It gives biology one shared framework: everything alive is made of cells. It guides medicine, since disease often means a cell is not working right. It guides genetics, since DNA lives inside cells. It guides ecology, since every organism is built from cells. Now imagine tenet 3 was false. Imagine cells could form from non-living matter. Biology would have to explain how life just appears from nothing. We could not understand reproduction, inheritance, or infection. Doctors could not track how cells turn cancerous. They could not track how bacteria spread. Germs could pop up anywhere, instead of coming from existing cells.

Part B: Plant Systems and Vascular Tissue (20 marks)

Q6 — Vascular tissue table: (6 marks)

Tissue What it carries Direction of flow System
Xylem Water and dissolved minerals Upward (roots → leaves) Root, stem, leaf
Phloem Sugars (glucose) Downward (leaves → rest of plant) Root, stem, leaf

Q7 — Dye experiment: (3 marks)

Xylem. Xylem carries water, and anything dissolved in it, from the roots up to the leaves. This includes the dye. The rings you see in the stem's cross-section are xylem bundles. They turned red because the dye moved with the water through the xylem.

Q8 — Root functions: (3 marks)

Any three: (1) soak up water and dissolved minerals from soil. (2) Anchor the plant in the ground. (3) Store food and energy, like a carrot or potato. (4) Give the plant stability against wind and gravity.

Q9 — Vascular tissue and height: (4 marks)

Non-vascular plants must soak up water straight through their surface. This limits them to small sizes near water. Vascular tissue (xylem) works like plumbing. It pulls water from the roots all the way up to the leaves. This water column inside the xylem can stretch over 100 metres. Without this transport system, the distance is too far. Diffusion alone could not move water fast enough to keep up with photosynthesis.

Q10 — Phloem disease: (4 marks)

The roots will slowly starve and die. Then the whole tree will die too. Leaves make glucose through photosynthesis. But they need phloem to send that glucose down to the roots. Roots have no chloroplasts, so they cannot make their own food. With the phloem destroyed, leaves keep making sugar, but it cannot reach the roots. The roots run out of energy. They stop growing, then die. Without working roots, the plant cannot absorb water or minerals. So the leaves die too. The tree dies from the bottom up, even though the leaves look healthy at first.

Part C: Photosynthesis (20 marks)

Q11 — Photosynthesis equation: (6 marks)

Carbon dioxide + water + light energy → glucose + oxygen

  • (a) CO₂ from: air, entering through stomata (pores on leaf surface)
  • (b) Water from: soil, absorbed by roots, transported up via xylem
  • (c) Glucose goes to: plant cells, for energy and growth. Extra glucose is stored as starch. Some is sent to other plant parts through the phloem.
  • (d) Oxygen released: through stomata into the atmosphere

Q12 — Why leaves are green: (2 marks)

Chlorophyll is the pigment inside chloroplasts. It absorbs red and blue light for photosynthesis. But it reflects green light. That reflected green light reaches our eyes. This is why we see leaves as green.

Q13 — Photosynthesis and ecosystems: (4 marks)

Photosynthesis turns sunlight into chemical energy, stored as glucose, inside producers. Primary consumers, like deer and caterpillars, eat producers to get that stored energy. Secondary consumers, like wolves and birds of prey, eat the primary consumers. Without photosynthesis, there would be no producers. Food webs would have no energy coming in. Every consumer, at every level, would run out of food. The whole ecosystem would collapse. Photosynthesis also makes the oxygen in our air. Every organism that breathes air needs that oxygen. This includes humans. They need it for cellular respiration.

Q14 — Refuting "plants eat soil": (4 marks)

Plants do not eat soil. They make their own food through photosynthesis, using CO₂ from the air and water taken up by their roots. Jan Baptista van Helmont grew a willow tree for 5 years. The soil lost only 57 grams. But the tree gained 74 kg. That much mass could not have come from the soil. The mass came from CO₂ in the air (carbon forms the backbone of every glucose molecule) and from hydrogen in water. Soil only supplies a small amount of dissolved minerals.

Q15 — Sealed container O₂ prediction: (4 marks)

The O₂ level rises. The plant photosynthesizes, using light from outside the clear container, CO₂ from the air inside, and water from the soil. It releases O₂ as a by-product. The plant also does cellular respiration, which uses O₂ and releases CO₂. But during the day, photosynthesis happens faster than respiration. So overall, O₂ builds up. Over two weeks, with enough light and water, O₂ keeps collecting inside the sealed container. Note: CO₂ will drop as the plant uses it up. Eventually, low CO₂ could slow photosynthesis down.

Part D: Reproduction (20 marks)

Q16 — Comparison table:

Sexual reproduction Asexual reproduction
How many parents Two (male + female) One
Genetic variation Yes — offspring differ from both parents No — offspring are genetically identical clones
Alberta example Canola (pollen + ovule → seed) Strawberry runners; potato tubers
Key advantage Genetic variation → adaptability to changing conditions Fast; no pollinator needed; all offspring robust

Q17 — Flower parts:

  • Petal: attracts pollinators (insects, birds) with colour and/or scent
  • a = stamen (anther + filament): makes pollen, the male reproductive cells
  • b = pistil/carpel: catches pollen. Holds the ovule. It is the female part of the flower.
  • c = stem/pedicel: holds up the flower so pollinators can reach it
  • d = receptacle/ovary base: the base of the pistil. Holds the ovules that grow into seeds.

Accept reasonable variants. Check that the function is correct for each part named.

Q18 — Strawberry runner: Asexual reproduction. The new plant is a clone. It is genetically identical to the parent plant. No genes were mixed. The runner just stretched out, and a new root system grew from it.

Q19 — Seed dispersal: Spreading seeds out stops the parent plant and its offspring from competing for the same light, water, and nutrients. It also lets the offspring reach and settle new habitats.

Two strategies:

  1. Wind dispersal (dandelion, maple keys): the seeds have wings or parachutes that catch the wind. They travel long distances, and the plant spends no extra energy.
  2. Animal dispersal (berries, hooks like burdock): animals eat the fruit, and the seeds pass through their gut and land somewhere new. Or the seeds hook onto fur or clothing and get carried away. This reaches places wind cannot.

Q21 — Selective breeding vs. GMO:

Selective breeding: farmers pick individuals with the trait they want, and let only those breed. Then they pick the best offspring again. Over many generations, the trait becomes more common. It is slower, but needs no lab work. GMO (genetic modification): scientists find a specific gene. They take it from any organism. They insert it directly into the plant's DNA, in a lab. This change is precise and fast — years instead of decades. Key differences: GMOs can use genes from a totally different species. Selective breeding only works within one species or close relatives. Selective breeding is less precise, since whole genomes mix together. GMOs change one specific gene.

Q22 — Herbicide resistance canola:

  • Benefit: farmers can spray a broad herbicide that kills all the weeds but leaves the canola unharmed. This means less competition, a higher yield, and maybe fewer herbicide sprays overall.
  • Concern: the herbicide-resistant genes could cross-pollinate into wild mustard relatives. That could create herbicide-resistant "superweeds." If every canola plant is the same variety, genetic diversity is lost. Also, weeds may build up their own resistance over time.

Q23 — Drought-tolerant wheat comparison:

Selective breeding: start with wheat plants that are already drought-tolerant. Breed only those, for many generations. This can take 10 to 20 years of field seasons. It is imprecise, since many genes change along with drought tolerance. Risk: it can lower genetic diversity. But ecological risk is low, since no foreign genes are added. GMO: find the exact gene, or genes, that control drought tolerance. It might come from another drought-adapted plant. Insert it into the wheat genome. This can show results in 3 to 5 years. It is highly precise, since only the target gene changes. Risk: the gene could spread to wild relatives. It also needs regulatory approval. Canadians may be slow to accept it. Faster: GMO. More precise: GMO. Risks: GMO carries ecological risk (gene spread) and regulatory risk. Selective breeding carries time and precision risks.

Part F: Synthesis (★) — answer-only

Q24:

a. Light, full spectrum and long day length. CO₂ supply, can add more. Enough water. The right temperature, about 20–25°C for most food crops. Nutrients in the growth medium.

b. Vascular system (phloem + xylem) must stay intact. Watch leaf colour (yellow = a nutrient or chlorophyll problem), stem firmness (wilting = not enough water), and root health.

c. Asexual (cuttings, tissue culture): a uniform crop, a faster growing cycle, no wait for seeds, and every plant has the wanted traits. Sexual reproduction adds variation. This is good for breeding programs, but not for production.

d. Accept any answer with good reasons. GMO works for one exact trait, like disease resistance or a longer shelf life. Selective breeding works for slow, steady gains with no regulatory issues. Organic methods work for a pesticide-free operation. Key: the reason must fit the greenhouse setting.
