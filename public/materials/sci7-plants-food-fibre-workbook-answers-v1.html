(b) A student says: "This is an animal cell — it has the same basic parts as any cell." Judge this claim. Name at least two structures in Figure 1 that make a plant cell different from an animal cell. Explain why each one matters.

Your judgment — be specific. Which structures are only found in plant cells, and what is each one's job?

(c) State one of the three tenets of cell theory. Explain how Figure 1 provides evidence for it.

Answer

(a)

  • Structure 1: cell membrane. A thin layer just inside the cell wall that controls what enters and leaves the cell.
  • Structure 2: large central vacuole. A large sac that stores water and dissolved materials; when it is full, it presses outward and keeps the cell firm.

(b) The claim is wrong: this is a plant cell. Two structures found only in plant cells:

  • Cell wall: a rigid outer layer that supports the cell and gives the plant its shape.
  • Chloroplasts: they capture light energy and carry out photosynthesis to make the plant's food.

(The large central vacuole also counts.) Plant and animal cells share a nucleus, a cell membrane, and cytoplasm, but these extra parts show the cell belongs to a plant.

(c) Any one tenet:

  • All living things are made of cells. The diagram shows a plant built from cells.
  • The cell is the basic unit of life. This one cell carries out life processes: it makes food in its chloroplasts, stores materials in its vacuole, and is controlled by its nucleus.
  • All cells come from pre-existing cells. The nucleus holds the information the cell uses to divide and make new cells.
Q2 ★★ Extended Evaluate

Cluster 4 — Reproduction · Evaluate

A greenhouse grower is thinking about two ways to grow more of her strawberry crop:

  • Strategy A: Grow new plants from seeds (sexual reproduction)
  • Strategy B: Grow new plants from runners (asexual reproduction)

(a) Explain how each strategy works. What is a “runner,” structurally, and how does it grow into a new plant? What genetic material does each new plant get?

Explain how both methods work. Include what genetic material each new plant gets.

(b) The grower wants new plants just like her best producer: same size, same disease resistance, same yield. Which strategy should she use? Back up your answer. Use what you know about genetic variation in sexual versus asexual reproduction.

(c) Judge this student claim:

Claim to judge

"Asexual reproduction in plants is something scientists made up in labs — it doesn't happen in nature."

Name two natural asexual reproduction strategies found in Alberta plants. Explain how each one works. Then say whether the student’s claim is correct, and why.

Answer

(a)

  • Strategy A, seeds (sexual): pollen (male) fertilizes an egg (female) in the flower, and a seed develops. Each new plant gets half its genetic material from each parent, so it is a new mix.
  • Strategy B, runners (asexual): a runner (stolon) is a stem that grows sideways along the ground. Where it touches soil it grows roots and leaves, forming a new plant that can be cut off. The new plant gets an exact copy of the parent's genetic material (a clone).

(b) Runners (Strategy B). Asexual reproduction makes plants genetically identical to the parent, so they keep its size, disease resistance and yield. Seeds mix genes from two parents, so their offspring vary and might lose the good traits.

(c) The claim is incorrect. Asexual reproduction happens naturally all the time. Examples:

  • Aspen sends up new trunks from its roots (suckers); a whole aspen grove can be one clone.
  • Strawberries spread by runners.
  • Potatoes grow from tubers.
  • Dandelions and Canada thistle regrow from root pieces.
Q3 ★ Core Historical experiment

Cluster 3 — Photosynthesis · Apply

Figure 2 — Van Helmont’s willow experiment (about 1650). A 2.3 kg sapling was planted in 90.8 kg of dried soil. For 5 years, it was watered with only rainwater.

(a) The tree gained about 75 kg of mass. The soil lost only 0.1 kg. Where did the tree’s mass come from? Explain using your knowledge of photosynthesis inputs.

(b) Van Helmont concluded: “The 77 kg of wood, bark, and root arose out of water only.” Was he correct? What important input did he fail to account for?

(c) Use Van Helmont’s data and what you know about photosynthesis. Prove this student claim wrong:

Student claim to refute

"Plants get their food from the soil — that’s what roots are for."

Use the data AND the photosynthesis inputs and outputs to build your argument. Point to specific numbers.
Answer

(a) Mostly from carbon dioxide in the air, plus water. In photosynthesis, the plant uses light energy to combine CO₂ and water into glucose, then builds wood, bark and roots from it. The soil supplied only a tiny amount of minerals (0.1 kg).

(b) Partly correct. Water was an input, but he missed carbon dioxide from the air, which supplies most of the carbon, the main part of a tree's dry mass.

(c) If plants got their food from the soil, the soil should have lost about 75 kg. It lost only 0.1 kg, while the tree gained 75.2 kg. So almost none of the tree's mass came from the soil. Photosynthesis shows where it came from: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen, using sunlight. Plants make their own food from air and water; roots take in water and a small amount of minerals, not food.

Q4 ★★ Extended

Cluster 2 — Plant systems and vascular tissue · Analyze

A Grade 7 class places a celery stalk in red food colouring mixed with water. After 2 hours, they cut the stalk crosswise. They find a ring of small red dots near the outer edge. The dots are not spread evenly through the whole stalk.

(a) Which type of vascular tissue moves the coloured water upward? Explain what this tissue does. Which way do materials travel in it?

(b) Phloem is not stained red in this experiment. Why not? What does phloem transport, and in which direction does it move materials?

(c) Judge this student claim:

Claim to judge

"The celery drinks the red water like a straw. It goes everywhere evenly through the whole stem."

Is transport in a plant stem spread evenly, or organized into set channels? What does the ring of red dots tell you? What does this pattern show about how plant transport systems evolved to fit plant needs?

Your judgment — refer to the structure of vascular bundles and the two tissue types.
Answer

(a) Xylem. Xylem tubes carry water and dissolved minerals from the roots up through the stem to the leaves. The flow moves one way: upward.

(b) Phloem carries sugars (food made in the leaves), not water from the cup, so the dye does not travel in it. Phloem moves food from the leaves to the rest of the plant (roots, growing tips, fruit), up or down as needed.

(c) The claim is wrong. Transport is organized into separate channels: the red dots are the vascular bundles, where the xylem sits. Water moves only in those tubes, not through the whole stem. This shows plants evolved specialized tissues: xylem moves water up, phloem moves food around, so each job is done efficiently in its own pathway.

(b) A plant is placed in a dark closet for 5 days. Its leaves start to turn yellow, and the plant eventually dies. Explain what happened inside its cells. Which structure stopped working, and why does no light cause this breakdown?

(c) Judge this student claim:

Claim to judge

"Plants breathe out carbon dioxide just like we do."

Is this always true? Describe a case where a plant releases CO₂, and a case where it does not. Explain what decides which process wins out.

Answer

(a) Missing input: water (H₂O). Missing output: glucose (sugar).

(b) Without light, the chloroplasts cannot carry out photosynthesis, so the plant makes no new food. It uses up its stored sugar and starch. Chlorophyll is also broken down and not replaced, so the leaves turn yellow. When its food runs out, the plant dies.

(c) Not always. Plants respire all the time (day and night), releasing CO₂, just like us. In the dark, they release CO₂, because they respire but cannot photosynthesize. In bright light, photosynthesis uses more CO₂ than respiration releases, so the plant takes in CO₂ overall and gives off oxygen. The amount of light decides which process wins.

Q6 ★★ Extended Evaluate

Cluster 5 — Plants for food and fibre · Evaluate

Alberta grows about 40% of Canada’s canola crop. Canola is used for cooking oil, biodiesel, and animal feed. About 95% of Alberta’s canola is genetically modified for herbicide resistance.

(a) Explain what “genetically modified for herbicide resistance” means, in simple biological terms. What was changed in the plant? What real problem does this solve for the farmer?

(b) Judge these two positions. For each one, say what is backed by science and what is exaggerated or not backed by evidence:

  • Organic farmer: “GMO canola is dangerous — scientists are playing God with our food supply.”
  • Biotechnology company: “GMO canola is totally safe and gives bigger yields.”
Tell apart claims about food safety, effects on ecosystems, and farming benefits. Use what the evidence actually says.

(c) Say a GMO crop is safe to eat. What worry might an ecologist still have about growing one herbicide-resistant variety across huge areas of Alberta? Explain one clear risk.

Answer

(a) Scientists added a gene to the canola that lets it survive a specific weedkiller (herbicide). The farmer can spray the field, and the weeds die while the canola does not. This makes weed control easier and cheaper, and it can mean less tilling.

(b)

  • Organic farmer: some concerns are fair, such as weeds becoming herbicide-resistant and GM genes spreading to other plants. But "dangerous to eat" and "playing God" are not supported by evidence: approved GM canola oil has been found safe to eat.
  • Biotech company: "safe to eat" is supported by safety testing. "Totally safe" overstates it: it ignores possible ecosystem effects. "Bigger yields" is partly true; the trait mainly makes weed control easier, and yields depend on many factors.

Food safety, ecosystem effects, and farm benefits are separate questions.

(c) Any one clear risk:

  • Herbicide-resistant weeds: spraying the same chemical everywhere selects for weeds that survive it.
  • Low genetic diversity: one variety over a huge area could be wiped out by a single disease or pest.
  • Gene flow: the resistance gene could spread to wild relatives or volunteer plants.

Q7 ★ Core

Cluster 2 — Plant systems and vascular tissue · Apply

A pine tree in Jasper National Park is girdled. This means a full ring of bark is stripped from around the trunk. It cuts through the phloem, but not the xylem. When this happens, the tree still has its needles (leaves) and healthy roots.

(a) At first, the needles keep photosynthesizing normally. Will the roots survive long-term? Explain, using what you know about what phloem carries and which way it moves.

(b) After several months, the needles start to turn yellow and die. This happens even though the xylem is intact and water is still reaching the leaves. Explain why the leaves eventually fail when only the phloem was cut.

HintThink about what roots need to stay alive, and where that comes from.

(c) What does this girdling example show about how the root system and shoot system depend on each other? Explain the relationship in your own words.

Answer

(a) No, not long-term. Phloem carries sugar from the needles down to the roots. With the phloem cut, no food reaches the roots, so they slowly starve and die once their stored food runs out.

(b) Roots need sugar from the leaves to stay alive and keep absorbing water. When the roots weaken and die from starvation, they stop taking up water, so the xylem has no water to carry up. The needles then dry out, turn yellow and die.

(c) The two systems depend on each other. The shoot system (needles) makes food for the roots. The root system takes in water and minerals for the shoots. If either one is cut off, the whole tree dies.

Q8 ★★ Extended Evaluate

Cluster 6 — Biotechnology · Evaluate

A group of Alberta students are talking about the Three Sisters companion planting system. Many Indigenous peoples across North America use it. They grow corn, beans, and squash together in the same plot. Two students disagree:

  • Student A: “This is just a traditional practice. It has no real science behind it.”
  • Student B: “This is an example of traditional biotechnology. Indigenous peoples have selectively bred these plants together for thousands of years.”

(a) Judge Student A’s claim. Name at least two ecological benefits of the Three Sisters system. Think about: nitrogen fixing by the beans, corn acting as a trellis, and squash covering the ground. Does this system have a scientific basis?

(b) Judge Student B’s claim. Is selective breeding a form of biotechnology? How is it different from GMO technology, at the level of genes?

(c) A modern industrial farmer wants to replace the Three Sisters system. The plan is a monoculture of genetically modified corn. Using what you know about biodiversity and biotechnology, judge the tradeoffs. What is gained? What is lost?

Your judgment — cover yield, soil health, ecological risk, and food security.
Answer

(a) Student A is wrong. Benefits:

  • Beans fix nitrogen: bacteria in their roots add nitrogen to the soil, which feeds the corn.
  • Corn is a trellis: beans climb its stalks toward the light.
  • Squash shades the ground: its large leaves block weeds and keep moisture in the soil.

The system has a clear scientific basis in nutrient cycling and plant interactions.

(b) Mostly correct. Selective breeding is a form of traditional biotechnology: people chose and planted seeds from the best plants over many generations. The difference: selective breeding mixes genes that already exist in the species through normal reproduction, over many years. GMO technology inserts or changes specific genes directly in a lab, sometimes using genes from another species, in one step.

(c)

  • Gained: higher corn yield per hectare, easier machine harvesting, and consistent crops.
  • Lost: soil health (no nitrogen fixing, so more fertilizer is needed; less ground cover, so more erosion); biodiversity (one crop instead of three, so less habitat); food security (one crop and one variety is vulnerable to a single pest, disease, or drought, and the beans and squash are no longer grown).

A good answer weighs both sides.

Q9 ★ Core

Cluster 1 — Cell theory and plant cells · Analyze

A student looks at two slides under a microscope: Slide A — cells from an Elodea (aquatic plant) leaf. Slide B — cells from the inside of a human cheek.

(a) List two structures the student would see in Slide A but NOT in Slide B. For each one, explain its job. Then explain why animal cells do not have it.

(b) Cell theory states: “all cells come from pre-existing cells.” How does this apply to growing a new plant from a cutting? Trace where the new plant’s cells come from. How does a cutting grow into a whole new organism?

(c) Judge this student claim:

Claim to judge

"A single plant cell can grow into a whole plant. So it’s basically the same as a stem cell in humans."

What part of this comparison is true? What part is misleading or different?

Answer

(a)

  • Chloroplasts: carry out photosynthesis. Animals eat food instead of making it, so they do not need them.
  • Cell wall: a rigid layer for support. Animals have skeletons for support, and their cells need to be flexible.

(The large central vacuole also counts: it stores water and keeps the cell firm.)

(b) A cutting contains living cells from the parent plant. Those cells divide to make new cells, which form roots, stems and leaves. Every cell in the new plant comes from the pre-existing cells of the cutting, so the new plant is a copy of the parent.

(c) True: many plant cells can divide and develop into any type of plant cell, even a whole new plant, similar to how stem cells can become different cell types. Misleading: a single adult human stem cell cannot grow into a whole human. Many ordinary plant cells keep this ability throughout life, while most human cells lose it.

Q10 ★★ Extended

Cluster 4 — Reproduction · Analyze

In Alberta, dandelions reproduce two ways. Sexually, they produce seeds carried by wind. Asexually, new plants grow from root fragments when roots are cut or disturbed by mowing. This makes dandelions very hard to get rid of on a lawn.

(a) Explain why dandelions do so well by using both ways to reproduce. What clear edge does each way give that the other cannot?

(b) A homeowner tries to get rid of dandelions by mowing again and again, before any flowers appear. Will this work in the long run? Explain your answer. Use what you know about asexual reproduction in plants.

(c) Many people call dandelions “weeds.” But in Alberta, dandelions are often the first nectar source bees can find after winter. Using what you know about plant reproduction and ecosystems, judge the tradeoff of wiping out dandelions from lawns on a large scale. Who or what benefits? Who or what is harmed?

Answer

(a)

  • Seeds (sexual): carried far by wind to new places, and they produce variety, so some offspring may survive new conditions or diseases.
  • Root fragments (asexual): let the plant regrow quickly in the same spot without flowering, even after damage, and make exact copies of a plant that is already succeeding there.

Together, the plant spreads to new places and holds onto the spot it already has.

(b) No. Mowing stops seeds, but the roots survive underground and keep sending up new leaves. Root pieces broken off can grow into new plants. Mowing removes leaves, not the root system that drives asexual reproduction.

(c)

  • Benefits of wiping them out: homeowners who want uniform lawns.
  • Harmed: bees and other pollinators, which lose an early food source after winter; the plants those pollinators later pollinate, including crops and wildflowers; and the ecosystem, through more herbicide use, which can harm insects and water.

A good answer weighs both and may suggest leaving some dandelions in spring.

Q11 ★ Core

Cluster 5 — Plants for food and fibre · Apply

A family in Red Deer, AB wants to grow their own food in a 10 m × 10 m plot. They are choosing between:

  • Wheat — grain crop, high yield per hectare, but needs milling and processing
  • Beans — a legume, can eat right away, adds nitrogen to soil
  • Potatoes — lots of calories per m², grows asexually from tubers

(a) Potatoes reproduce from tubers (asexual reproduction). Explain what a tuber is. Then explain why this method helps a home gardener. The gardener wants to grow the same variety every year. Compare it to growing from seed.

(b) The family chooses beans partly because “beans make the soil better.” Explain the biological reason for this. (Hint: think about what legume roots can do with nitrogen gas from the air.)

(c) Say the family grows only one crop in the same plot, year after year (monoculture). What problem might they face over time? Use what you know about soil biology and plant health to explain.

Answer

(a) A tuber is a swollen underground stem that stores starch and has "eyes" (buds). Each eye can sprout into a new plant. Because the new plants are clones, the gardener gets the same variety every year, with the same taste and size, and plants that start fast using stored food. Seeds mix genes, so offspring can vary.

(b) Beans are legumes. Their roots have nodules with bacteria that take nitrogen gas from the air and turn it into forms plants can use (nitrogen fixation). This adds nitrogen to the soil, a nutrient plants need to grow.

(c) Growing the same crop every year uses up the same nutrients from the soil, so the soil becomes poorer. Pests and diseases that attack that crop build up in the soil. Yields drop over time unless the family adds fertilizer or rotates crops.

Q12 ★★ Extended Evaluate

Cluster 6 — Biotechnology · Evaluate

A government agency has approved a new genetically modified wheat variety that is drought-resistant. Many Canadian wheat farmers want to grow it. But several European countries have banned imports of GMO wheat.

(a) Explain what “drought-resistant” means, at the level of plant biology. What might scientists have changed? Think about ways to cut water loss, or to help the plant take up more water in dry conditions.

(b) Judge both views below. For each one, say what is backed by science and what is still truly uncertain:

  • Alberta farmer: “This would let me use less water and make my farm more sustainable.”
  • European consumer: “We don’t yet know the long-term effects of GMO wheat on human health or on ecosystems.”
Give a balanced judgment — cover food safety and ecological effects separately. Don’t dismiss either view completely.

(c) Imagine you are advising the Alberta government. Should farmers be allowed to grow this GMO wheat for export? What two exact, science-based rules or safeguards would you require before approving it? Explain the science reason for each one.

Answer

(a) "Drought-resistant" means the plant can survive and produce grain with less water. Possible changes include: deeper or larger roots to reach water; fewer stomata, or stomata that close sooner, to cut water loss; a waxier leaf surface; or the ability to store water or tolerate drying out.

(b)

  • Alberta farmer: supported, because the crop needs less irrigation and survives dry years. Uncertain: how well it performs in real field conditions over many years, and whether it leads to other side effects.
  • European consumer: on food safety, current evidence finds approved GM crops safe to eat, though long-term studies on this specific variety are still limited. On ecological effects, there is real uncertainty about gene flow to other plants, effects on soil organisms, and wider land use.

Neither view should be dismissed completely.

(c) Accept any two science-based safeguards. Examples:

  • Long-term field trials and monitoring of the soil, insects and nearby plants before and after approval, to catch ecosystem effects that short tests miss.
  • Buffer zones and gene-flow controls (separation distances from non-GM wheat), because wheat pollen can carry the gene into other crops.
  • Clear labelling and tracking of GM wheat, so export markets that ban it are protected and any problems can be traced.

S1 Clusters 3 + 1 — Evaluate / Create

Design an experiment to test this: can plants survive with light but no CO₂?

(a) State your hypothesis. Back it up using what you know about photosynthesis. Be specific: what do you predict will happen, and why?

(b) Describe your experiment design. What is your control group? List at least three variables you would control. Explain how you would control each one.

Experiment design — control group, variables controlled, and your method for removing CO₂ from the chamber.

(c) After 4 weeks, the plant in the no-CO₂ chamber has yellowing leaves, but it is still alive. Explain why it hasn’t died yet. What stored resources might it be using? Predict what will happen over the next 4 weeks if conditions stay the same.

Answer

(a) Example hypothesis: "The plant will not be able to make new food and will slowly die, because photosynthesis needs carbon dioxide as well as light. Without CO₂, it cannot make glucose, even in bright light."

(b) Control group: an identical plant in the same kind of chamber with normal air (with CO₂). Variables to control (any three): same species, size and age of plant; same light (brightness and hours); same temperature; same amount of water; same soil or nutrients. Removing CO₂: put a chemical that absorbs CO₂ (such as soda lime or limewater) inside a sealed chamber.

(c) It is living on stored food: starch and sugar in its leaves, stems and roots. It uses this food through respiration while making no new food. Over the next 4 weeks, the stored food will run out, more leaves will yellow and drop, and the plant will most likely die.

S2 Clusters 4 + 6 — Evaluate

Scientists made “seedless” watermelons by giving the plant an extra set of chromosomes. This stops it from forming working seeds. The fruit is grown by crossing these sterile plants with normal plants. So every crop needs new parent plants made in a lab.

(a) Is this technique selective breeding or genetic modification? Explain how the two are different, at the level of genes. Where does the seedless watermelon process fit?

(b) A farmer says: “I can’t save seeds from my crop, so I have to buy new seeds every season — this makes me dependent on the seed company.” Judge what this means for Alberta farming. Think about the effects on nature and on farmers’ money.

(c) Traditional seed-saving means picking and storing seeds from the best plants each season. Indigenous peoples across the Americas have practised this for thousands of years. Compare this practice to the seedless watermelon model. What knowledge or farming skill could be lost if seed-saving traditions disappear?

Answer

(a) It is closest to selective breeding (plant breeding). No new genes are inserted; the plant is given extra copies of its own chromosomes, then crossed with a normal plant. Genetic modification inserts or changes specific genes in the DNA directly. Selective breeding works with the species' existing genes through reproduction. Accept either answer if it is well reasoned, since chromosome doubling is done in a lab.

(b) Economic: farmers must buy seed every year, which raises costs and makes them dependent on a few seed companies and their prices. Ecological: fewer, uniform varieties are grown, so genetic diversity drops and crops are more vulnerable to new diseases or a changing climate.

(c) Seed-saving lets farmers choose and keep the varieties best suited to their own land and climate, generation after generation. If it disappears, we lose: local and traditional varieties (genetic diversity); the knowledge of how to select, store and grow them; and food independence for communities. Indigenous seed-saving keeps both living seeds and cultural knowledge alive.

S3 Clusters 2 + 3 + 5 — Create

You are designing a vertical farm. It will grow leafy greens inside a Calgary office building, year-round. The farm has no soil, no natural sunlight, and no outside air.

(a) Using what you know about plant systems and photosynthesis, name three conditions your farm must provide. For each one, explain which plant process it supports. Then predict what would happen to the plants if that condition were missing.

Three conditions — for each one: name the condition, the biological process it supports, and what fails without it.

(b) Your investor says: “Use a nutrient solution instead of soil — it’s more efficient.” Explain what a nutrient solution replaces in how the plant normally works. What does soil normally give the plant? What must the solution replace it with?

(c) A critic says: “Vertical farming is completely unnatural and bad for people.” Judge this claim using science. What would you need to measure or test? You want to compare the nutrition in vertically farmed greens versus field-grown greens. Name two measurable variables. Explain what each one would tell you.

Answer

(a) Any three:

  • Light (LED grow lights): supports photosynthesis. Without it, no food is made and the plants yellow and die.
  • Carbon dioxide (air circulation): supports photosynthesis. Without it, no glucose is made.
  • Water: supports photosynthesis, transport in the xylem, and firmness of cells. Without it, the plants wilt and die.
  • Nutrients and minerals: support growth (for example, nitrogen for leaves). Without them, growth is stunted and leaves turn yellow.
  • Suitable temperature: keeps processes such as photosynthesis and respiration working. If it is too cold or hot, the plants slow down or die.

(b) Soil normally gives the plant water, dissolved minerals and nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and others), air for the roots, and support for anchoring. The nutrient solution must provide the water and dissolved minerals, and the system must also supply oxygen to the roots and some physical support (racks or growing pads).

(c) "Unnatural" does not mean unhealthy, so the claim must be tested. Measurable variables (any two):

  • Vitamin or mineral content (for example, vitamin C, iron or calcium per 100 g): tells whether the greens are as nutritious.
  • Pesticide or contaminant levels: tells whether they are safer or less safe.
  • Fibre or protein content.
  • Calories or yield per square metre.

Compare the same variety grown both ways.
