Section 1: Types of Political Systems

Blank / PromptExpected Answer
A dictatorship is a system where ___ person holds total controlone
An oligarchy is a system where a small ___ of people hold powergroup
A democracy is a system where citizens elect people to ___ themrepresent
Anarchy means little or no ___ authoritycentral / centralized

Retrieval Check answers:

  • Q1 (order most → least control): Dictatorship → Oligarchy → Monarchy → Democracy (anarchy is the "least control" endpoint, not itself part of this four-item order).
  • Q4 (council of ten wealthy owners, no elections): Oligarchy — small group, not one person, holds power without elections.
  • ★ Stretch (Q5 — constitutional monarchy): This is a constitutional monarchy: a democracy with a ceremonial monarch. It's hard to place in one box because it blends features of monarchy (a king/queen exists) and democracy (elected reps actually make the laws).
High-Risk Vocabulary Flag

"Democracy" and "monarchy" — students often think these are mutually exclusive. Flag that a country (e.g., the UK, Canada) can have both a monarch AND be a functioning democracy at the same time; the two are not opposites once you separate ceremonial role from real political power. Also flag "anarchy" — students confuse it with chaos/violence in casual usage; keep the definition strictly to "absence of centralized government authority."

Retrieval Check answers:

  • Q4 (small town of 800, new park): Direct approach makes sense — small population, single local issue, easy to gather everyone's input.
  • ★ Stretch (Q5 — "not a real democracy" claim): The claim confuses direct and representative democracy. Canada IS a democracy — citizens elect representatives who vote on laws on their behalf. Not voting directly on every law does not make a system undemocratic; it makes it a representative democracy, which is the realistic model for a country of 40+ million people.
High-Risk Vocabulary Flag

"Referendum" — students may confuse with a regular election. Clarify: a referendum is a vote on one specific question or law, not a vote for a person/representative.

Section 3: Parliamentary vs. Presidential Democracies

Blank / PromptExpected Answer
PM elected as leader by their own ___ firstparty / caucus
President elected ___, in a nationwide votedirectly
Comparison table — Leader title (Presidential)President

Retrieval Check answers:

  1. (How PM vs. President are chosen) PM: becomes leader through party leadership, then PM only if their party wins the most seats. President: elected directly, nationwide, by all voters.
  2. (Two parliamentary terms) Any two of: caucus (all elected members of a party), backbencher (MP without a Cabinet post), frontbencher (MP with a Cabinet post), whip (ensures party members vote as directed), Leader of the Opposition (leader of the second-largest party), shadow cabinet (opposition critics assigned to each government minister).
  3. (Two presidential terms) Any two of: Congress (the elected legislature), veto (President's power to reject a bill), elected Senate (unlike Canada's appointed Senate), elected judiciary (unlike Canada's appointed judges).
  4. (Veto/override example) The President rejected the bill using the veto power; Congress then voted again and got enough votes to pass the bill anyway, overriding the President's veto.
  5. ★ Stretch (fixed-term trade-off) Parliamentary: advantage — a PM who loses support can be replaced without waiting years; disadvantage — less stability/predictability. Presidential: advantage — stable, predictable fixed term; disadvantage — a president who loses public support still serves the full term.
High-Risk Vocabulary Flag

This section carries the heaviest vocabulary load in the unit (10 curriculum-mandated terms). Watch for students conflating "cabinet" (used in both systems, different meaning nuance) and "senate" (appointed in Canada vs. elected in the U.S. — this contrast is a common test-item target).

Section 4: Canada's Political System

Blank / PromptExpected Answer
Canada's three branchesExecutive, legislative, judicial
Each riding elects one ___Member of Parliament (MP)
GG's role is mostly ___ceremonial

Retrieval Check answers:

  1. (Three branches + roles) Executive (PM/Cabinet — carries out and enforces laws), legislative (Parliament — debates/passes laws), judicial (courts — interprets laws, settles disputes).
  2. (How someone becomes PM) Wins leadership of a political party, then that party wins the most seats in a federal election — the PM is the leader of the winning party, not directly elected by the whole country.
  3. (What is a riding) An electoral district; each one elects one MP to represent it in the House of Commons.
  4. ("Voting for the PM" classmate) Wrong: you cannot vote for the PM directly. Should say: "I'm voting for my local MP / for the party I want to form government."
  5. ★ Stretch (PM vs. GG real power) The PM holds real day-to-day power because they lead the party with the most elected support and make policy decisions with Cabinet. The GG's role is mostly ceremonial (Royal Assent, opening Parliament) and by convention acts on the PM/Cabinet's advice — the GG looks more official in ceremonies but does not hold independent political power.

[misconception target: PM directly elected like a U.S. president] — appears as a refutation-first callout in this section.

High-Risk Vocabulary Flag

"Riding" and "constituency" are used interchangeably — confirm students know these are the same thing, just different common names. "Senate" here is the APPOINTED Canadian Senate — do not let students merge this with the elected U.S. Senate from Section 3.

Section 5: Political Parties and Party Systems

Blank / PromptExpected Answer
Parties organize to turn ideas into ___law
Opposition's job is to ___ government decisionsquestion / scrutinize / challenge
Party systemExample
One-partyChina
Two-partyUnited States
MultipartyCanada, Alberta, many European countries

Retrieval Check answers:

  1. (Why parties form around ideology) People with similar political beliefs organize together because it is more effective to pool support and resources to get elected than to run alone.
  2. (Government vs. opposition) Government = party with the most seats, forms the executive and sets policy. Opposition = next-largest party (or parties), whose job is to question and challenge government decisions — a required, legitimate role, not disloyalty.
  3. (Three party systems + example) One-party (China), two-party (United States), multiparty (Canada / Alberta / Europe).
  4. (5 parties, no majority) Multiparty system; challenge — no party has enough seats to govern alone, so parties may need to negotiate a coalition or minority government, which can slow decisions.
  5. ★ Stretch (US vs. Canada trade-off response) A strong response acknowledges Canada's multiparty system offers more voter choice, but notes it can also produce minority governments and slower decision-making; the U.S. two-party system decides faster but offers fewer choices. Neither is simply "better."
Misconception Target

[misconception target: more parties = healthier democracy] — appears as a refutation-first callout in this section.

High-Risk Vocabulary Flag

"Ideology" is introduced in the companion Foundations of Ideologies unit at deeper depth — here, keep it at "a shared set of political beliefs," not the left/right spectrum detail (that is out of scope for this unit; see scope boundary note in _courses/grade-8-social/_context.md).

Section 6: Canada's and Alberta's Parties

Retrieval Check answers:

  1. (Name the parties) Federal: Liberal, Conservative, NDP, Green, Bloc Québécois. Alberta: United Conservative Party (UCP), New Democratic Party (NDP).
  2. (What is a platform, why it matters) A document laying out what a party promises to do if elected; it matters because it lets voters compare parties' plans before deciding who to support.
  3. (Two things members vote on) Any two of: local candidate selection, party priorities/policy direction, party leader.
  4. (15-year-old getting involved) Could join a party as a member (most parties don't require voting age to join), volunteer with a local riding association, or attend party events — membership and volunteering are open before voting age.
  5. ★ Stretch (Full path, 3+ terms) Example: A citizen joins a party as a member → votes in their riding association to choose the local candidate → that candidate wins their riding in the general election → the party wins the most seats and forms government → the party's leader becomes Prime Minister. (Accept any answer using 3+ correct unit terms in a logical chain.)

Unit Synthesis — Sample Answers

ClusterSample answer
Types of political systemsPolitical systems sit on a spectrum from total government control (dictatorship) to no central authority (anarchy), with oligarchy, monarchy, and democracy in between.
Models of democracyDemocracy isn't one system — direct democracy has citizens vote on laws themselves; representative democracy has citizens elect people to vote for them, which is more realistic for large populations.
Parliamentary vs. presidentialCanada's parliamentary system chooses its leader (PM) through party leadership and seat count; the U.S. presidential system elects its leader directly, for a fixed term, with executive and legislative branches kept separate.
Canada's political systemCanada has three branches (executive, legislative, judicial); Parliament includes the GG (ceremonial), PM/Cabinet (executive), Senate (appointed), and elected MPs from each riding (House of Commons).
Political parties and party systemsParties form around shared ideology to win seats and make law; government and opposition both play required roles; countries differ in one-party, two-party, or multiparty systems, each with trade-offs.
Canada's/Alberta's parties and membershipCanada's federal parties include Liberal, Conservative, NDP, Green, and Bloc Québécois; Alberta's are UCP and NDP; citizens can join as members and influence candidates, priorities, and leadership through riding associations.
