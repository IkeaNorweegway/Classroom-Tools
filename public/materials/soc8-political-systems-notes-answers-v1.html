Who Gets to Decide?

Every country has to answer one big question: who gets to make the decisions that control people's lives? The answer is different in every country. We call the set of rules and structures that answers this question a political system.

Political systems are not all the same. Some countries give almost all control to one person or one small group. Other countries spread control across many people, or elected representatives. We can line political systems up on a spectrum, from the most government control to the least.

A dictatorship is a system where one person holds total control, usually without free elections.

An oligarchy is a system where a small group of people — not one person, and not the whole population — hold the power.

A monarchy is a system led by a king, queen, emperor, or similar hereditary ruler. Some monarchies today (like the United Kingdom's) are "constitutional" — the monarch is mostly ceremonial, and real power sits with elected leaders. Others, historically, held absolute power. Canada's own head of state is technically the monarch, which we will connect to Canada's system in Section 4.

A democracy is a system where citizens hold the power, either by voting directly on laws or by electing people to represent them.

Anarchy means there is little or no central / centralized authority at all. No government is making and enforcing the rules for the whole society.

A useful way to remember the spectrum: as you move from dictatorship toward anarchy, government control goes down and individual freedom generally goes up — but that does not automatically mean a society runs better. A society with no government at all can be unsafe and unstable too.

Misconception

"Democracy is just one thing — every country with a democracy works the same way."

Why this fails: Picture a town meeting in Ancient Athens. A few thousand citizens could gather in one place and vote directly, by a show of hands, on every law. Now picture Canada — over 40 million people spread across ten provinces and three territories. There is no way to gather everyone in one place to vote on every single law, every single time. A model built for a small city cannot simply be copied onto a country of millions.

Correct understanding: "Democracy" is not one single system — it is a spectrum of models. Two of the most important are direct democracy and representative democracy, described below. Later in this unit you will also see that representative democracies themselves come in different forms (parliamentary and presidential).

A direct democracy is a system where citizens vote directly on laws and decisions themselves, without going through elected representatives.

A representative democracy is a system where citizens elect people to represent them and make decisions on their behalf.

Direct democracyRepresentative democracy
Who votes on laws?All citizens, directlyElected representatives, on behalf of citizens
Best suited toSmall population, local decisionsLarge populations; national-level decisions
Canadian exampleMunicipal plebiscite / referendumFederal and provincial general elections

Most modern countries — including Canada — use representative democracy for national decisions, because voting directly on every law is not realistic for millions of people. However, elements of direct democracy still exist. A referendum is a vote where citizens decide directly on one specific question, rather than voting for a representative. Canada has held referendums before, such as votes on constitutional questions.

Most decisions in a representative democracy are made using majority rule — the option, candidate, or party with the most support usually wins. This does not mean everyone agrees; it means the largest group's choice becomes the decision.

Two Ways to Build a Representative Democracy

Representative democracies are not all built the same way. Two major models are the parliamentary (Westminster) democracy, used by Canada, and the presidential democracy, used by the United States.

In a parliamentary democracy, voters elect local representatives. The leader of the party with the most seats becomes the head of government — usually called the Prime Minister. The Prime Minister is not elected directly by the whole country; they are elected as a leader by their own party / caucus first, then become PM if their party wins.

In a presidential democracy, voters elect the head of government — the President — directly, in a nationwide vote, separately from voting for other representatives.

FeatureParliamentary (Canada)Presidential (United States)
Leader titlePrime MinisterPresident
How the leader is chosenLeader of the party with the most seatsElected directly by voters, nationwide
Typical term lengthNo fixed term — election can be called earlierFixed term (4 years in the U.S.)
Who makes law-making decisionsExecutive (PM/Cabinet) and legislature are closely connectedExecutive and legislature are kept separate (checks and balances)

Parliamentary-system vocabulary in context: All the elected members of a party form its caucus. Members who do not hold a Cabinet position are backbenchers; members who do (like Cabinet ministers) are frontbenchers. Each party has a whip, whose job is to make sure party members show up and vote the way the party wants. The leader of the second-largest party is the Leader of the Opposition, and their team of critics — each one assigned to watch a government minister's file — is the shadow cabinet. Some MPs are also given the title of parliamentary secretary, assisting a minister with their work.

Presidential-system vocabulary in context: The U.S. legislature is called Congress, made up of an elected House of Representatives and an elected Senate — unlike Canada's Senate, which is appointed, not elected. The President can veto (reject) a bill passed by Congress, and Congress can override that veto with enough votes. The U.S. also elects some judges, whereas Canada's judiciary is appointed, not elected.

The core difference to remember: in a parliamentary system, the executive (PM and Cabinet) comes directly out of the legislature and depends on its support to stay in power. In a presidential system, the executive (President) is elected separately and stays in power for a fixed term, independent of the legislature.

Misconception

"Canadians vote directly for the Prime Minister, the same way Americans vote directly for their President."

Why this fails: Look closely at a federal election ballot. It does not have the Prime Minister's name on it anywhere — it only lists the local candidates running in your specific riding. If Canadians directly elected the Prime Minister, every voter across the whole country would see the exact same names on their ballot. They don't. Every riding's ballot is different.

Correct understanding: Canadians vote only for their local Member of Parliament (MP) in their own riding. The party that wins the most seats across the country usually forms the government, and the leader of that party becomes Prime Minister — a position earned through party leadership and seat count, not a nationwide vote.

Canada is a parliamentary democracy. Like most modern governments, Canada's political system is divided into three branches: the   branch, the   branch, and the Executive, legislative, judicial branch.

The executive branch carries out and enforces laws — in Canada, this is the Prime Minister and Cabinet. The legislative branch debates and passes laws — this is Parliament, made up of the elected House of Commons and the appointed Senate. The judicial branch interprets laws and settles disputes — this is Canada's court system. In Canada, the executive and legislative branches are closely connected, because the Prime Minister and Cabinet are themselves elected Members of Parliament.

Parliament's Structure

Canada is divided into electoral districts called ridings (also called constituencies). Each riding elects one Member of Parliament (MP) to represent it in the House of Commons. Canada currently has 338 ridings, so there are 338 MPs.

Misconception

"The Governor General is a powerful decision-maker who could override the Prime Minister whenever they wanted."

Why this fails: If the Governor General held real independent power, we would expect to see them regularly rejecting bills, dismissing prime ministers, or setting their own policies. In practice, this almost never happens — the Governor General acts on the advice of the Prime Minister and Cabinet, following long-standing tradition.

Correct understanding: The Governor General's role is mostly ceremonial. They represent the monarch, give formal approval to bills (Royal Assent), and open each new session of Parliament. Real day-to-day political power belongs to the Prime Minister and Cabinet.

A political party is a group of people who share a similar set of political ideas — an ideology — and organize together with one goal: getting their members elected so they can turn their ideas into law.

Political parties are not a new idea. The very first political parties in the modern sense formed in 18th-century England, called the Whigs and the Tories. Since then, parties around the world have followed the same pattern: they form around shared ideas, and they can also merge with other parties or dissolve completely if they lose support.

In a parliamentary system, the party with the most seats becomes the government, and its leader becomes Prime Minister. The next-largest party becomes the official opposition. The opposition's job is to question / scrutinize / challenge the government's decisions and offer a different point of view — this is not disloyal, it is a required part of how the system works.

Countries also differ in how many parties realistically compete for power. We call this a country's party system:

Party systemWhat it meansExample
One-party systemOnly one party is allowed to hold real power 
Two-party systemTwo major parties realistically compete; smaller parties rarely win 
Multiparty systemThree or more parties realistically compete for power 
Misconception

"The more political parties a country has, the healthier its democracy automatically is."

Why this fails: If more parties always meant a healthier democracy, we would expect multiparty countries to make decisions more easily and effectively than two-party countries. Often the opposite happens — when no single party wins enough seats to govern alone, parties must negotiate and form coalitions, which can slow decisions down or cause instability. Two-party systems can act faster, but voters have fewer choices. Neither design is automatically "healthier."

Correct understanding: The number of parties is a trade-off, not a scorecard. More parties usually means more choice for voters but can mean slower, harder-to-reach decisions. Fewer parties usually means faster decisions but less choice. What matters most is whether a system genuinely represents and serves its citizens.

Connection Prompt

Section 6  ·  Canada's and Alberta's Parties — Joining and Taking Part

Learning target: I can identify Canada's and Alberta's political parties and explain how citizens can join and take part in a party.

Vocabulary

TermDefinition in your own wordsOne example
Party platform  
Party membership  
Riding association  
Board of Directors (riding association)  

Canada's federal Parliament currently includes members from several parties, including the Liberal Party, the Conservative Party, the New Democratic Party (NDP), the Green Party, and the Bloc Québécois. In Alberta, the two major provincial parties are the United Conservative Party (UCP) and the New Democratic Party (NDP).

These parties are named here factually, so you can recognize and study them — not to say which one is "best." Comparing party platforms is part of informed citizenship, not taking sides.

Each party publishes a party platform — a document laying out what it promises to do if it forms government. Comparing platforms is one way citizens decide who to support.

Most major parties in Canada allow open public membership — almost anyone can join, usually for a small fee. Party members are not just supporters from the sidelines. Members can:

  • Vote on which local candidate represents the party in their riding
  • Vote on the party's priorities and policy directions
  • Vote for the party's overall leader

At the local level, each party has a riding association (also called a constituency association) in every riding where it runs a candidate. Each riding association has its own President and Board of Directors, elected by the local party members, who organize local party activities between elections.
