Part A: Types of Structures (16 marks)

Q1 — Classify structures: (6 marks)

a. Bicycle = F (frame)

b. Egg = Sh (shell)

c. Lethbridge Viaduct = F (frame — steel lattice)

d. Brick = S (solid)

e. Car body = Sh (shell — thin outer skin carries load)

f. House = C (combination — frame interior + shell exterior cladding + solid foundation)

Q2 — Why combination structures: (4 marks)

Different parts of a structure carry different loads. No single structure type can handle every job well. Frame parts spread lateral and compression loads through an inner skeleton. But they leave the outside unprotected. Shell surfaces keep out weather and spread surface loads. But they have no inner framework to hold up people or objects. Solid parts make strong foundations. But they would be too heavy and costly if used everywhere. Alberta example: the Glenbow Museum in Calgary. It has a concrete frame — columns handle compression. It has a glass-and-stone shell on the outside. It has solid concrete foundation pads.

Q3 — Cardboard tube principle: (3 marks)

Shape matters as much as material for strength. A flat sheet buckles under compression. The force pushes on one spot, and the sheet bends sideways easily. A tube spreads compression force all the way around it. Any push to buckle one way is stopped. The curved surface resists it from every other side. This is why columns, pipes, tree trunks, and bones are round or hollow tubes. They are not flat slabs.

Q4 — Structure type identification: (3 marks)

a. Igloo = shell. It is a thin, curved ice dome. The dome shape spreads load along the curve. So the ice skin itself carries the load. No framing is needed.

b. Eiffel Tower = frame. It is an open steel lattice skeleton. Most of the structure is open space, held up by rigid triangulated members.

c. Poured concrete dam = solid. The material is the same all the way through, with no gaps inside. Its mass and solid material resist the water pressure.

Part B: Loads (16 marks)

Q5 — Load types table: (9 marks — 3 per row)

Load type Definition Alberta example
Dead load Constant weight of the structure's own materials Weight of bridge steel on the Groat Bridge, Edmonton
Live load Variable weight the structure is designed to carry; gravity, predictable Cars and trucks on an Alberta highway bridge; accumulated snow on a Calgary arena roof
Dynamic load Moving, changing, or impact forces; often unpredictable in direction/magnitude Chinook wind gusts on Calgary buildings; seismic activity in the Rockies foothills; traffic vibration

Q6 — February bridge collapse: (4 marks)

Most likely, this is a dead load failure. Ice or snow may have built up. This could push the permanent load past its design limit. There was no traffic, so it was not a live load. The weather was calm, so it was not a dynamic wind or seismic load. Those causes are ruled out. In a cold Alberta February, ice can build up on bridge parts. This adds real mass to what the structure treats as permanent dead weight. Over time, weak materials or metal fatigue can cause a dead-load failure. This can happen even with no traffic. Or: a flaw or rust weakened a part. It finally gave way under the load already there.

Q7 — Why dynamic loads are more dangerous: (3 marks)

Dynamic loads can change direction and size fast. Structures are usually built for loads from one direction — gravity pulls straight down. But dynamic loads can push sideways or shake back and forth. Earthquakes and wind gusts are examples. Shaking forces can cause resonance. This is when the force matches the structure's own natural shake rate. Each shake grows bigger than the last, until the structure fails. Static loads of the same size are steady and predictable. Engineers can plan for them exactly. Dynamic loads change all the time. They wear materials down over time.

Part C: Internal Forces (20 marks)

Q8 — Internal forces table: (8 marks — 2 per row)

Force What it does Real-world example
Compression Pushes material together; squeezes Column in a building; top surface of a beam under load; arch
Tension Pulls material apart; stretches Cable in a suspension bridge; bottom surface of a beam; elastic band
Shear Two parallel forces acting in opposite directions on adjacent planes Bolt holding two plates being pulled apart; scissors cutting; nailing two boards
Torsion Twisting force along the long axis of a member Drive shaft of a vehicle; bridge deck twisting in wind; wringing a wet cloth

Q9 — Beam under load: (6 marks)

a. Compression: top surface. The load pushes the top fibers together and shortens them.

b. Tension: bottom surface. The load pulls the bottom fibers apart and stretches them.

c. Concrete is strong in compression but very weak in tension. It cannot resist being pulled apart well. The bottom surface, under tension, would crack, and the beam would fail.

d. Reinforced concrete: steel rebar sits inside the bottom part of the beam. Rebar has very high tensile strength. Concrete handles the top compression. Rebar handles the bottom tension. Together, the beam resists both forces.

Q10 — Suspension bridge cables: (3 marks)

Cables must be steel, not concrete. The cables run from the deck up to the towers and anchors. Their job is to hold up the deck's weight by staying pulled taut. They are entirely under tension. Concrete is very weak in tension. It cracks and crumbles when pulled apart. Steel has very high tensile strength. It can take huge pulling forces without breaking. Internal force: tension.

Q11 — Bolt with opposing plates: (3 marks)

Shear. The two plates are pulled sideways in opposite directions. They sit on either side of the bolt. The bolt must resist two parallel forces pushing across it from adjacent planes. This is the definition of shear: forces parallel to the surface, working against each other.

Part D: Stability (16 marks)

Q12 — Stability rule: (4 marks)

A structure is stable as long as its centre of gravity (CoG) stays right above its base of support. If the CoG moves outside the base, the structure topples. Two design choices boost stability. (1) Widen the base of support. A bigger base means the CoG must travel farther before the structure tips. (2) Lower the centre of gravity. Put heavy mass near the base. Then the CoG is less likely to shift outside the base when the structure tilts.

Q13 — Heavy bottom is not always more stable: (4 marks)

Not always true. What matters is where the whole centre of gravity sits, compared to the base of support. It is not just where the heavy parts are. Take a very tall structure with its heavy part at medium height. Its CoG can still sit high above a narrow base. Even a heavy base can topple, if it sits on a narrow, tall structure with a high CoG. For stability, the CoG must stay within the base when the structure tilts. This is true no matter where the mass sits.

Q14 — Triangle vs. square: (4 marks)

A triangle is the only shape that cannot change form without changing the length of a side. Push a triangulated frame sideways. The shape forces the load into compression or tension along its rigid parts. The joints cannot rotate. A square or rectangle is different. It can turn into a slanted shape under a sideways force. No side changes length. It deforms by rotating at the joints. This is why triangulation shows up in roof trusses, bridge trusses, and lattice towers. Adding a diagonal brace to a rectangle turns it into triangles that cannot deform.

Q15 — Construction crane: (4 marks)

The crane risks toppling when the load at the end of its arm shifts the combined centre of gravity — crane plus load — outside the base. The farther out the load is, and the heavier it is, the more the CoG shifts that way. Ways to prevent this: use a large, wide base. Then the CoG must travel farther before it crosses the edge. Add counterweights on the other side of the arm. This pulls the CoG back toward the centre. Also use outrigger legs that reach outward. These make the base much wider.

Part E: Simple Machines (20 marks)

Q16 — Identify simple machine:

a. Wheelbarrow = lever (2nd class — load sits between fulcrum and effort)

b. Flagpole rope = pulley (fixed — changes the direction of force)

c. Jar lid = wheel and axle, or screw, depending on the lid. The rim is the wheel; the threads are the screw.

d. Door wedge = wedge

e. Screwdriver prying = lever (1st class — fulcrum at the paint can rim)

f. Stairs = inclined plane. They spread a rise over more distance, so each step needs less effort.

Q17 — Calculate MA:

a. MA = load / effort = 300 N / 60 N = 5

b. MA = slope length / height = 4 m / 1 m = 4

c. MA = number of rope segments supporting load = 4

Q18 — One-fifth the work? (4 marks)

Not correct. A simple machine with MA 5 lets you use 1/5 the force. But you must move 5 times the distance. Work = force × distance. If we ignore friction, the total work on the load equals the total work you put in. 1/5 force × 5× distance = the same total work. Machines do not cut down work. They just trade force for distance in a more useful way. Energy is always conserved. So no machine can lower the total energy needed to move an object.

Q19 — Lever classes:

1st class: seesaw or crowbar. The fulcrum sits between the effort and the load. It can multiply force or change direction. F — fulcrum — L

2nd class: wheelbarrow. The load sits between the fulcrum and the effort. MA is always greater than 1 — it always multiplies force. F — L — E

3rd class: tweezers or fishing rod. The effort sits between the fulcrum and the load. MA is always less than 1 — it trades force for speed or reach. F — E — L

Part F: Materials and Design Synthesis (12 marks)

Q20 — Match role to material:

Structural role Best material Reason
Column under compression Concrete (or stone) Very strong in compression, low cost, and long-lasting. Takes compression load without changing shape.
Suspension bridge cable High-strength steel cable Very high tensile strength. Flexible, so it can carry tension around a curve. Does not snap under repeated use.
Building frame in earthquake zone Structural steel (or reinforced concrete) Steel is ductile — it bends before it breaks. This soaks up shaking energy. Concrete alone is brittle, so it can fail all at once under sideways force.

Q21 — Banff pedestrian bridge:

a. The bridge's own weight = dead load. Tourists walking = live load. Mountain wind gusts = dynamic load. Snow buildup = live load, though heavy snowfall could turn dynamic during a storm.

b. The railing and lateral bracing should be triangulated. Wind pushes sideways on the bridge. Rectangular parts would bend out of shape under this push. Triangulated bracing cannot deform. It sends sideways force into the rigid parts as plain push and pull.

c. Steel advantage: more strength for its weight on the main span. It also lasts longer in wet mountain conditions. It resists wear better from repeated foot traffic. Wood advantage: it looks more natural in Banff National Park. It is renewable and found nearby. It is lighter too. That makes it easier to install in a remote spot without heavy equipment.

Part G: Challenge (★) — answer-only

Q22 — Quebec Bridge failure:

a. Frame structure — a truss bridge with a triangulated lattice frame

b. Compression. Under mid-span loading, the lower chord of this truss is in compression. This is the opposite of a simple beam's bottom chord. Why? Because the truss shape sits above the deck. The lower chord pushes back against compression.

c. Dead load — the weight of the extra building materials added to the half-built structure

d. Compressive strength — how well it resists buckling under a squeezing force along its length. Also: buckling resistance, and how slender the member is.

e. Any of: make the lower chord thicker so it resists buckling better. Or use a stockier, less slender part. Or add lateral bracing to shorten the length that can buckle. Or get an outside review of the design math before building goes on. Or check the load capacity at each stage of construction.
