Part A — Choose the Answer

Circle the best answer. [1 mark each]   /8

1. You roll a die. What is P(rolling a 5)?

A) 5/6B) 1/6C) 5D) 1/5
Answer B · Why: 1 favourable out of 6 · Common mistakes: A: counted the numbers that are not 5

2. Which number can not be a probability?

A) 0B) 0.5C) 1D) 1.5
Answer D · Why: Probabilities go from 0 to 1 · Common mistakes: A: 0 is allowed (impossible)

3. A bag has 3 green cubes and 7 yellow cubes. What is P(green) as a percent?

A) 30%B) 3%C) 70%D) 37%
Answer A · Why: 3 out of 10 = 30% · Common mistakes: C: found P(yellow)

4. You flip a coin and spin a spinner with 4 equal parts. How many outcomes are there?

A) 6B) 4C) 8D) 2
Answer C · Why: 2 × 4 = 8 · Common mistakes: A: added 2 + 4

5. Two events are independent when

A) one does not change the chances of the otherB) they happen at the same timeC) they have the same probabilityD) they can never both happen
Answer A · Why: One event does not change the other · Common mistakes: B: "at the same time" is not the rule

6. You flip a coin and spin a spinner with 4 equal parts. One part is blue. What is P(heads and blue)?

A) 3/4B) 2/6C) 1/8D) 1/6
Answer C · Why: 1/2 × 1/4 = 1/8 · Common mistakes: A: added. D: 1 out of 2 + 4

7. A and B are independent. P(A) = 1/2 and P(B) = 1/3. What is P(A and B)?

A) 5/6B) 2/5C) 1/5D) 1/6
Answer D · Why: 1/2 × 1/3 = 1/6 · Common mistakes: A: added. B: added tops and bottoms

8. Mo flips a coin 10 times and gets 7 heads. What is the experimental P(heads)?

A) 1/2B) 7/10C) 3/10D) 10/7
Answer B · Why: 7 heads out of 10 flips · Common mistakes: A: gave the theoretical P

[3]12. Ava rolled a 6 three times in a row. Ava says: “6 is my lucky number. I will probably roll it again.” Zoe says: “A 6 can't come up four times in a row, so the next roll won't be a 6.” Explain why both of them are wrong. What is P(6) on the next roll?

Answer Each roll is independent. The die has no memory, so three 6s in a row do not change the next roll. Ava is wrong: 6 is not more likely. Zoe is wrong: 6 is not less likely. P(6) = 1/6.
  • 1 mark: each roll is independent (the die has no memory)
  • 1 mark: explains why both are wrong
  • 1 mark: P(6) = 1/6

13. You flip a coin and roll a die.

[2](a) Use the multiply rule to find P(tails and a number greater than 4).

[1](b) Jake adds instead: 1/2 + 2/6 = 5/6. How can you tell his answer is too big?

Answer (a) P(tails) = 1/2. P(greater than 4) = 2/6 = 1/3 (5 or 6). P(tails and greater than 4) = 1/2 × 1/3 = 1/6.
  • 1 mark: P(greater than 4) = 2/6
  • 1 mark: multiplies to get 1/6

(b) "And" must be less likely than each event on its own. 5/6 is bigger than both 1/2 and 2/6, so it is too big. (Or: a table has 12 outcomes and only 2 are tails with 5 or 6.)

  • 1 mark

14. On any school day, the probability that Bus 12 is late is 1/10. One day does not change the next.

[1](a) Find P(late on Monday and late on Tuesday).

[2](b) Find P(on time on Monday and on time on Tuesday).

Answer (a) 1/10 × 1/10 = 1/100
  • 1 mark

(b) P(on time) = 9/10 each day. 9/10 × 9/10 = 81/100 (81%)

  • 1 mark: P(on time) = 9/10
  • 1 mark: 81/100

16. A game at the Fort McMurray school fair has two steps. First you spin a spinner with 4 equal parts: star, moon, sun and cloud. Then you pick one of 3 cards without looking: 1 prize card and 2 blank cards. You win if you get the star and the prize card.

[2](a) Draw a tree diagram or a table that shows every outcome. How many outcomes are there?

[2](b) Use the multiply rule to find P(win). Check your answer with your tree or table.

[2](c) 120 people play the game. About how many should win? Show how you know.

[2](d) At the fair, 15 of the 120 players won. Does this mean the game is broken? Explain using experimental and theoretical probability.

Answer (a) A tree or table with 4 × 3 = 12 outcomes (each symbol paired with prize, blank, blank). Accept the cards as "prize, blank 1, blank 2".
  • 1 mark: a full tree or table
  • 1 mark: 12 outcomes

(b) P(star) = 1/4, P(prize) = 1/3. P(win) = 1/4 × 1/3 = 1/12. The tree shows 1 winning outcome out of 12.

  • 1 mark: multiply rule
  • 1 mark: checks with the tree or table

(c) 1/12 × 120 = 10 winners.

  • 1 mark: method
  • 1 mark: 10

(d) No. 15/120 = 1/8 is the experimental probability. It is close to the theoretical 1/12, but results vary. 120 games is not a lot of trials, so 15 winners instead of 10 can happen by chance.

  • 1 mark: names experimental vs theoretical
  • 1 mark: explains that small samples vary

17. Theo flips a coin and rolls a die. He writes:

“P(heads and a 6) = 1/2 + 1/6 = 4/6.”

Explain Theo's mistake. Find the right answer, and explain how an outcome table shows that your answer is right.

Answer Model answer: Theo added, but for "and" with independent events you multiply. The right answer is 1/2 × 1/6 = 1/12. An outcome table for a coin and a die has 2 × 6 = 12 cells, and only one cell is heads and 6. So the answer is 1 out of 12. (Adding gives 4/6, which is bigger than P(heads), and that can't be right.)
