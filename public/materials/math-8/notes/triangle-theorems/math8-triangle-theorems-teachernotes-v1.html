Teacher notes

Triangle Theorems

Grade 8 Mathematics · Geometry & Measurement · 4 lessons + quiz · Outcome 8G2 · Codes TT1–TT4

Overview

Big idea: a theorem is a fact proved from facts we already have. Tearing off a triangle's three corners and lining them up fills a straight line, so the angles of every triangle add to 180°, whatever its size; isosceles and equilateral triangles follow (equal base angles, three 60° angles). The triangle inequality says the two shorter sides must add to more than the longest, or the sides lie flat or leave a gap. For right triangles only, the squares on the two legs add to the square on the hypotenuse, a² + b² = c², and the converse lets us test whether a corner is square. Problems come down to one decision: is the missing side the hypotenuse (add the squares) or a leg (subtract)?

Sequence: tear and line up the corners, the angle sum theorem, isosceles and equilateral triangles, a roof truss and a ramp (TT1) → three straws: meet, gap or flat; test the longest side; the range for a missing side; the shortcut home is never longer than the path through school (TT2) → squares on the sides, legs and hypotenuse, the converse as a right-angle test, the 3-4-5 rope, Pythagorean triples (TT3) → add or subtract, a ladder, a soccer field diagonal, a guy-wire, a TV screen, answers to one decimal place (TT4).

Materials: paper triangles (several shapes and sizes, one per student), scissors, glue sticks, protractors, rulers, straws or pipe cleaners cut to 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 10 cm, grid paper for squares on the sides, a rope with 12 equally spaced knots, calculators with a √ key, a metre stick or tape measure. Optional: GeoGebra to drag a vertex while the angle sum and the side squares update.

Depth ceiling: angle sum, isosceles, equilateral, triangle inequality, Pythagorean theorem and its converse. No exterior-angle theorem, no parallel-line angle pairs, no trigonometry. Missing sides of right triangles only, with answers to one decimal place (square roots from topic 2). Triangle-inequality ranges are written as a < x < b with open dots. Proof is informal: tearing corners, counting squares, measuring.

Reading level: student pages are written at about Grade 3–4. The math words (theorem, angle sum, isosceles, base angles, equilateral, right triangle, triangle inequality, longest side, range, legs, hypotenuse, Pythagorean theorem, converse, Pythagorean triple, square root, one decimal place, guy-wire) are the learning; each has a picture beside it.

Notation: ticks mark equal sides and arcs mark equal angles; a small square marks 90°. The hypotenuse is always c, whatever the letters on the picture; students write which side is c before substituting. Squares are written out (6² = 36) and the root step is shown with ≈ when rounded.

Watch forWhat to do
"A bigger triangle has more degrees"Tear the corners of a big and a small triangle with the same shape. Both fill one straight line. The angle sum depends on shape, never size.
Isosceles: halving the top angle instead of finding the base anglesMark which angles are equal with arcs first. The equal angles are across from the equal sides, at the base.
"Any three lengths make a triangle"Hand out 2, 3 and 7 cm straws. Let students try. Then 3, 4 and 7: they lie flat. Equal is not enough: the sum must be MORE.
Testing the wrong pair (adding the longest side to a short one)Always test the two shorter sides against the longest. If that works, every other pair works too.
Range for a missing side: only the upper limitTwo cases: x is the longest (less than the sum) or x is shorter (more than the difference). Draw both with straws.
"c is whatever side is labelled c"Find the right angle first. The side across from it is c, the longest side. Rename if the picture uses other letters.
Converse test with the wrong side as c (5² + 13² vs 12²)Put the longest side alone on one side of the equals sign.
"For a leg, add the squares"Estimate: a leg is shorter than the hypotenuse. If the answer is longer than c, you added. Missing leg: subtract.
Doubling instead of squaring (6² = 12)Write 6² as 6 × 6 every time in this unit. The grid-paper squares make it visible.
Rounding too earlyKeep the full square root until the end: √33.75 = 5.809… ≈ 5.8.

Lessons

Lesson 1 — Angles in a triangle · 60 min
DOPre-knowledge check (5 min). Q1 previews angles on a straight line, Q2 subtracting from 180, Q3 squares (Lesson 3), Q4 a square root (Lesson 4), Q5 solving x + x = 140 (isosceles), Q6 comparing a sum (Lesson 2).
DOTear off the corners (10 min). Every student cuts a different triangle, tears off the three corners, and lines them up on a straight line. Compare across the room.
SAY"Every triangle we tried fills a straight line: 180°. A fact that is proved like this is a theorem. This is the angle sum theorem."
WATCH"A bigger triangle has more degrees." Misconception box: two triangles with the same 40°, 60°, 80° angles.
CUEWorked Example 1 (48° and 75°, x = 57°), Worked Example 2 (isosceles roof truss, top 40°, base angles 70°), Worked Example 3 (base angle 35°, top 110°), Worked Example 4 (a ramp with a right angle, t = 56°).
SHOWTicks and arcs on the isosceles and equilateral triangles. Why must each equilateral angle be 60°?
DOYour turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 1.
Lesson 2 — The triangle inequality · 60 min
DOWarm-up interleaved: an isosceles triangle with top angle 50°; 9² − 5²; is 3 + 5 > 9?; √64.
DOStraw stations (15 min). Pairs try 3, 4, 6; 2, 3, 7; 3, 4, 7 and record "triangle, gap or flat".
SAY"The two shorter sides must add to more than the longest side. If they are equal, the straws lie flat."
WATCH"Any three lengths make a triangle." Misconception box: 2, 3 and 8 cm.
CUEWorked Example 1 (5, 7, 10: yes), Worked Example 2 (4, 5, 9: equal, no triangle).
CUEWorked Example 3 (sides 4 and 9: 5 < x < 13 on a number line). Act out both cases with straws.
CUEWorked Example 4 (home, school and rink: the straight path is never longer than 2.0 km, so not 2.5 km).
DOYour turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 2.
Lesson 3 — The Pythagorean theorem · 60 min
DOWarm-up interleaved: third angle of a right triangle with a 52° angle; can 3, 3, 7 make a triangle?; 8² + 6²; √100.
DOSquares on the sides. On grid paper, draw a 3-4-5 right triangle and a square on each side. Count: 9 + 16 = 25. Repeat with a triangle that is not right (5, 5, 8 roughly) to see it fail.
SAY"In a right triangle, the two small squares fill the big one: a² + b² = c². c is the hypotenuse, across from the right angle."
WATCH"c is whatever side is labelled c." Misconception box.
CUEWorked Example 1 (6, 8, 10: right), Worked Example 2 (7, 8, 11: 113 vs 121, not right).
DOThe 12-knot rope. Three students stretch it into a 3-4-5 triangle and check the corner with a set square or a book corner.
CUEWorked Example 3 (the rope, the converse), Worked Example 4 (5, 12, 13 and doubling to 10, 24, 26).
DOYour turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 3. Quiz at the start of next class.
Lesson 4 — Right-triangle problems · 60 min
DOQuiz (25 min) first. Then the lesson.
SHOWThe two "add or subtract?" triangles. "Which side is missing? Is it c?"
WATCH"For a leg, add the squares." Misconception box: a 3 m by 5 m ladder cannot reach higher than its length.
CUEWorked Example 1 (6.0 m ladder, foot 1.5 m out: h ≈ 5.8 m), Worked Example 2 (soccer field 100 m by 64 m: 118.7 m), Worked Example 3 (guy-wire, 30 m tower, 16 m out: 34 m exactly), Worked Example 4 (48 by 27 inch screen: a "55-inch" TV).
DOMeasure the classroom or a desk and predict its diagonal, then measure it. Compare to one decimal place.
DOCore worksheet; Challenge for early finishers. Exit ticket Section 4. The unit synthesis can be homework or the start of the next class.

Assessment

Quiz (24 marks)

TT1 (Q1–4), TT2 (Q5–7), TT3 (Q8–10). Given at the start of Lesson 4, before right-triangle problems (TT4). Calculators allowed. Q1: the theorem must be named ("angle sum" or "angles add to 180°"). Q2: 2b + 24° = 180° earns the setup mark even with an arithmetic slip; 78°, not 156° or 12°. Q3 needs the reason (three equal angles share 180°). Q6 checks the "equal is not enough" case: 2.5 + 3.5 = 6 is not more than 6. Q7: both limits and open dots for full marks; 5 ≤ x ≤ 21 earns 2 of 3. Q8: full marks need "by the converse" or "it matches, so it is right". Q9: MP, with the reason. Q10 is the explain-your-thinking item: Raj put the longest side in the wrong place; full marks need the corrected test and the conclusion that 5, 12, 13 IS a right triangle.

Worksheets and exits

Core (★) covers TT1–TT4 with a diagram on most questions: a missing angle with the theorem named, an isosceles truss with a 54° top angle, a ramp with a 41° angle, straws of 4, 6 and 9 cm, an "any three lengths work" error, the range for sides 7 and 11, a triangle labelled x, y, z, converse tests (9, 40, 41 and 6, 7, 9), a missing hypotenuse, a missing leg, and a 4.0 m ladder. Challenge (★★) adds angles x, x + 20° and 2x, an isosceles triangle with a 100° angle, whole-number third sides, a deck builder squaring a corner, a 25 m guy-wire, a 65-inch TV's height, the Red Deer field shortcut, and both possible third sides from 6 and 8. Each misconception has at least one targeted question; the key names it. Exit tickets: one per section, each with a diagram and an "explain how you know" item.

Data and contexts used

  • Soccer field 100 m by 64 m: inside the international range (100–110 m by 64–75 m); the standard is 105 m by 68 m.
  • TV sizes are diagonals. A 48 by 27 inch screen (16 : 9) has a diagonal of 55.1 inches, close to real "55-inch" sets (about 47.9 by 27.0 inches). The worksheet's "65-inch" screen 57 inches wide is close to real (about 56.7 inches).
  • Ladder safety: the usual rule puts the foot one quarter of the ladder's length out from the wall (6.0 m ladder, 1.5 m out), which the notes follow. The 5.0 m and 4.0 m ladders in the exits and worksheet are made up.
  • The 12-knot rope: rope stretchers are often linked to ancient Egyptian builders; the history is not certain, so present it as "builders use this trick", which is true today.
  • The guy-wire (30 m tower, 16 m out), the home–school–rink distances, the Red Deer school field (80 m by 50 m), the roof trusses, ramps and the deck are made up.

Connections

  • Square Roots & Irrational Numbers (topic 2): every missing side ends with a square root; most are irrational and rounded to one decimal place.
  • Inequalities (topic 6): the range for a third side is a double inequality on a number line with open dots.
  • Similarity & Dilations (topic 8): similar triangles have equal angles; scaling a Pythagorean triple by k gives another triple.
  • Surface Area (topic 10): the slant height of a pyramid comes from a right triangle (given in Grade 8; found with a² + b² = c² as a stretch).
  • Grade 7: angles on a straight line add to 180°; classifying triangles by sides and angles.
  • Grade 9 and beyond: distance between two points on a grid; trigonometry in high school.
