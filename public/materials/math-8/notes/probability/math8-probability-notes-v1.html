The multiply rule (independent events)

P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)

Worked Example 5 — Heads and a 6

P(heads) = 1/2. P(6) = 1/6.

P(heads and 6) = 1/2 × 1/6 =

Check with the table in Section 2: 1 cell out of 12 ✓

Watch out — a common mistake

"P(heads and 6) = 1/2 + 1/6 = 4/6."

Why it is wrong: "And" means both must happen. That is harder than either one alone, so the answer must be SMALLER. Adding can even give more than 1 — impossible.

What is true: Multiply: 1/2 × 1/6 = 1/12. The table shows 1 cell out of 12.

Explain it Why must P(A and B) be smaller than P(A) and smaller than P(B)?

Section 4 · Testing probability

Learning target: I can compare experiment results with theoretical probability, and explain why they can be different.

Words to know

WordWhat it means (in your own words)Example or picture
theoretical probability
experimental probability
trial
Watch out — a common mistake

"P(heads) = 1/2, so 10 flips will give exactly 5 heads."

Why it is wrong: Theoretical probability tells what happens over MANY trials. A small number of trials can vary a lot.

What is true: In 10 flips you might get 3, 5 or 7 heads. With 1000 flips, the results get closer to 1/2.

Worked Example 8 — A class experiment

Each group flips a coin and rolls a die. They count "heads and a 6." Theory says 1/12 ≈ 8%.
