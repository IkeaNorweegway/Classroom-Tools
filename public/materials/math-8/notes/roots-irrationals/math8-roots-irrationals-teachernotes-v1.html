Teacher notes

Square Roots & Irrational Numbers

Grade 8 Mathematics · Number · 4 lessons + quiz · Outcomes 8N3, 8N1.2 · Codes SR1–SR4

Overview

Big idea: a square root is the side of a square with a given area. Grade 7 stopped at whole-number perfect squares; Grade 8 extends the idea in three directions. First, fractions and decimals can be perfect squares too (9/16 = (3/4)², 0.25 = 0.5²). Second, every positive number has two square roots, because a negative times a negative is positive: √ names the principal (positive) root, −√ the negative one, and x² = a gives x = ±√a; 0 has one root and a negative number has none in the reals. Third, most numbers are not perfect squares, so their roots are estimated between benchmark perfect squares, and those roots turn out to be decimals that never end and never repeat. That is a new kind of number, the irrationals, which together with the rationals make up the real number line.

Sequence: squares as repeated multiplication and powers, perfect squares among fractions and decimals (top and bottom both perfect squares in simplest form), the "half" misconception (SR1) → two numbers square to 25, the mapping picture, why a negative has no real root, principal vs negative root, solving x² = a, rejecting the negative root for a length (SR2) → benchmarks, estimating to one decimal place and checking by squaring, the "halfway" trap, side from area and perimeter of a square (SR3) → √2 between 1.41 and 1.42 (zooming in), π from a rolling wheel, exact vs approximate, rational vs irrational, the real-number boxes, ordering real numbers including negatives (SR4).

Materials: square tiles or grid paper (cut a 7 × 7 square and a 10 × 1 strip), a 10 × 10 grid for decimal squares, calculators with a √ key, number-line strips from 1 to 2 in tenths and 1.4 to 1.5 in hundredths, a can or wheel with a 1 m diameter mark (or a 10 cm lid and string for the π demo), mini whiteboards. Optional: Desmos to zoom in on √2.

Depth ceiling: square roots only; no cube roots in Grade 8. Estimating applies to numbers up to 144 (benchmarks 1, 4, … 144), then the calculator. No simplifying radicals (√50 stays √50, not 5√2), no operations with radicals, no proof that √2 is irrational. "No real square root" is the answer for a negative number; complex numbers are not mentioned. Answers to one decimal place unless asked otherwise; perimeter is found from the unrounded side.

Reading level: student pages are written at about F&P N–P. The math words (square, power, perfect square, non-perfect square, square root, √, principal square root, negative square root, ±, benchmark, estimate, approximate, exact, irrational number, real number) are the learning; each has a picture or example beside it.

Notation: √25 = 5 (one value), −√25 = −5, ±√25 = ±5. The equation x² = 25 has two solutions; the expression √25 has one value. Write ≈ whenever a root is rounded: √50 ≈ 7.07, never √50 = 7.07. The notes use bar notation (0.6); the parent home sheets write 0.4545… because the home renderer has no bar.

Lesson 1 — Perfect and non-perfect squares · 60 min
DOPre-knowledge check (5 min). Q1–Q2 recall Grade 7 squares and roots, Q3 the perfect-square test for whole numbers, Q4 a negative times a negative (needed in Lesson 2), Q5 fractions to decimals (needed in Lesson 4), Q6 area and perimeter of a square (needed in Lesson 3).
SHOWTiles arranged as 2 × 2, 3 × 3, 4 × 4, 5 × 5, then 12 tiles as 2 × 6 and 3 × 4. "Which ones make a square?"
SAY"A perfect square is a number times itself. Now fractions and decimals can be perfect squares too. 3/4 times 3/4 is 9/16."

CUEWorked Example 1 (49/64 = (7/8)²), Worked Example 2 (0.25 yes, 0.09 yes, 0.9 no), Worked Example 3 (an 8-by-8 Lego plate: 8 × 8, 8², 64 studs).
WATCH"The square root is half of it." Misconception box.
CUEWorked Example 4 (8/18: simplify first, then test).

Lesson 2 — Two square roots · 60 min
DOWarm-up interleaved: (−6)²; is 0.16 a perfect square?; 5² and (−5)²; order −0.3, −1/3.
SHOWThe mapping picture: −5 and 5 both arrow to 25. Then −3, 0, 3 → 9, 0, and nothing reaches −9.
SAY"A positive number has two square roots. √25 is the principal root, 5. Minus √25 is −5. 0 has one root. A negative number has no real root."
WATCH"√−9 = −3." Misconception box: check by squaring.
WATCH"√25 = ±5." Misconception box: √ gives one value; the equation gives two.
CUEWorked Example 1 (x² = 49 → x = ±7), Worked Example 2 (x² = 9/16, x² = 0.36, x² = −4), Worked Example 3 (an Edmonton backyard rink with area 144 m² → 12 m; the −12 is rejected).
DOYour turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 2.
Lesson 3 — Estimating square roots · 60 min
DOWarm-up interleaved: √(49/100); solve x² = 0.04; 7.5 × 7.5; the perfect squares from 1 to 144 written as a strip (keep it on the wall).
SHOWThe benchmark picture for √50: two number lines, 49 and 64 on top, 7 and 8 below.
SAY"Find the perfect squares on each side. Their roots are your benchmarks. Decide which one is closer, estimate to one decimal, then check by squaring."
WATCH"√50 ≈ 25" and "√50 ≈ 7.5, halfway." Two misconception boxes.
CUEWorked Example 1 (√30 ≈ 5.5, checked: 5.5² = 30.25), Worked Example 2 (a 50 m² garden near Red Deer: side ≈ 7.1 m, fence ≈ 28.3 m), Worked Example 3 (√110 ≈ 10.5).
SHOWThe flow: side → square it → area; area → square root → side; side × 4 → perimeter.
CUEWorked Example 4 (a 120 m² field in Lethbridge: side ≈ 10.95 m, fence ≈ 43.8 m).
DOYour turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 3. Quiz next class (SR1–SR3).
Lesson 4 — Irrational numbers and the real number line · 60 min
DOQuiz (25 min) first. Then the lesson.
SHOWA square of area 2. "How long is its side?" Zoom: 1.4² = 1.96 and 1.5² = 2.25; then 1.41² = 1.9881 and 1.42² = 2.0164. "Will this ever stop?"
SAY"√2 never ends and never repeats. It can't be written as a fraction. It is irrational. The rational and irrational numbers together are the real numbers."
SHOWThe π wheel: a 1 m wheel rolls one turn, 3 m and a bit (0.14159…).
WATCH"π = 3.14 exactly" / "1.41 is √2." Misconception box with the 1.40–1.42 zoom.
CUEThe real-number boxes. Worked Example 1 (sort √36, √37, 0.6, π, −√4, 22/7), Worked Example 2 (exact and rounded table: √50, π, √8), Worked Example 3 (order √10, 3.1, π, 3.2, 3), Worked Example 4 (−√5 and √5 on a line).

Data and contexts used

  • Lego: an 8 × 8 stud plate is a real Lego size.
  • The Edmonton backyard rink (144 m², 12 m × 12 m) is made up and large for a backyard; most are closer to 6 m × 9 m.
  • The Red Deer garden (50 m²), the Medicine Hat garden (60 m², worksheet and home sheet) and the quiz's 90 m² field are made up.
  • The Lethbridge dog run (120 m²) is a made-up size.
  • The quiz rink (400 m², 20 m × 20 m) is made up.
  • √2 ≈ 1.414 213…, π ≈ 3.141 592…, √10 ≈ 3.162 277… and the other calculator values are exact to the digits shown; the materials round to one or two decimal places.

Connections

  • Grade 7 Squares & Cubes: perfect squares to 144 and √ of a perfect square are assumed.
  • Rational Numbers (topic 1): the nested boxes gain the irrationals; "ends or repeats" is the test for rational.
  • Triangle Theorems (topic 9): the Pythagorean theorem gives c = √(a² + b²), usually a non-perfect square to estimate or round.
  • Surface Area (topic 10): π ≈ 3.14 is the approximate value used for cylinders.
  • Grade 9: x² = a gives ±√a again when solving equations; cube roots and powers with integer exponents.
