Section 1 · Perfect and non-perfect squares

Learning target: I can tell if a fraction or decimal is a perfect square.

Words to know

WordWhat it means (in your own words)Example or picture
perfect squarea number you get by squaring a rational number49 = 7², 9/16 = (3/4)², 0.25 = 0.5²
non-perfect squarea number that is not the square of any rational number20, 2/3, 0.4
squaredtimes itself0.5 squared = 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25
powera short way to write a repeated multiplication(3/4)² means 3/4 × 3/4

What you know from Grade 7

A perfect square is a number of tiles that makes a square.

12 tiles make rectangles, but never a square. So 12 is a non-perfect square.

Now fractions and decimals can be perfect squares too

Cut a square into 4 by 4 small parts. Shade 3/4 of the rows and 3/4 of the columns.

The overlap is a square. It is 9/16 of the whole.

So 9/16 = (3/4)². It is a perfect square.

Perfect square test

Write the fraction in simplest form.

If the top and the bottom are both perfect squares, it is a perfect square.

9/16 = (3/4)², because 9 = 3² and 16 = 4².

For a decimal, write a fraction first. 0.25 = 25/100 = (5/10)² = 0.5².

Worked Example 1 — Is 49/64 a perfect square?

Step 1: Is the fraction in simplest form? Yes.

Step 2: Top: 49 = 7². Bottom: 64 = 8².

Step 3: So 49/64 = (7/8)² = 7/8 × 7/8.

Yes, 49/64 is a perfect square.

Worked Example 2 — Decimals
  1. a) Is 0.25 a perfect square? 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25. Yes: 0.25 = 0.5².
  1. b) Is 0.09 a perfect square? 0.09 = 9/100. 9 = 3² and 100 = 10². Yes: 0.09 = 0.3².
  2. c) Is 0.9 a perfect square? 0.9 = 9/10. 10 is not a perfect square. So no.
Explain it 0.09 is a perfect square, but 0.9 is not. Why?
Answer 0.09 = 9/100, and 9 and 100 are both perfect squares. 0.9 = 9/10, and 10 is not a perfect square.
Worked Example 3 — A Lego plate

A square Lego plate has 8 studs along each side.

Repeated multiplication: 8 × 8. Power: 8². Value: 64 studs.

Watch out — a common mistake

"The square root of a number is half of it. √16 = 8."

Why it is wrong: The square root is the side of a square. Half is something else.

What is true: √16 = 4, because 4 × 4 = 16. Check: 8 × 8 = 64, not 16.

Worked Example 4 — Simplify first

Is 8/18 a perfect square?

Step 1: Simplify. 8/18 = 4/9.

Step 2: 4 = 2² and 9 = 3². So 4/9 = (2/3)².

Step 3: Yes. 8/18 is a perfect square.

Section 2 · Two square roots (±)

Learning target: I can find both square roots of a number.

Words to know

WordWhat it means (in your own words)Example or picture
square roota number that, times itself, gives the number5 and −5 are square roots of 25
principal square rootthe positive square root. The √ sign means this one.√25 = 5
negative square rootthe negative one. Write −√.−√25 = −5
±"plus or minus": both roots at once±√25 = ±5 means 5 and −5

Two numbers square to 25

5 × 5 = 25. Also (−5) × (−5) = 25. Same signs give a positive.

So 25 has two square roots: 5 and −5.

Square roots of a number

A positive number has two square roots, one positive and one negative.

√25 = 5 is the principal square root. −√25 = −5 is the negative square root.

0 has only one square root: 0 × 0 = 0.

A negative number has no real square roots.

Why does a negative have no square root?

Square any number. Look at the sign of the answer.

Number3−30.5−0.50
Squared990.250.250

Every answer is positive or 0. None is negative.

Watch out — a common mistake

"√−9 = −3."

Why it is wrong: Check it. (−3) × (−3) = 9, not −9. Two negatives make a positive.

What is true: No real number times itself is −9. So √−9 has no real answer.

The equation x² = a

x² = 25 asks: "Which numbers squared give 25?"

There are two: x = 5 or x = −5. Write x = ±√25 = ±5.

Watch out — a common mistake

"√25 = ±5. The root sign gives both answers."

Why it is wrong: √ means the principal (positive) root only. The equation x² = 25 is what has two answers.

What is true: √25 = 5. −√25 = −5. x² = 25 gives x = ±5.

Worked Example 1 — Solve x² = 49

Step 1: Which numbers squared give 49? 7² = 49 and (−7)² = 49.

Step 2: x = ±√49 = ±7

Step 3: Check: (−7)² = 49 ✓ and 7² = 49 ✓

Worked Example 2 — Fractions and decimals
  1. a) x² = 9/16. x = ±3/4, because (3/4)² = 9/16 and (−3/4)² = 9/16.
  2. b) x² = 0.36. x = ±0.6
  1. c) x² = −4. No real answer. No number squared is negative.
Worked Example 3 — A square ice rink

A backyard rink in Edmonton is a square. Its area is 144 m². How long is each side?

Step 1: s² = 144, so s = ±√144 = ±12.

Step 2: A length can't be negative. So the side is 12 m.

Explain it In Worked Example 3, why do we keep only the positive root?
Answer A side length is a distance. A distance can't be negative, so s = −12 makes no sense.

  4. 3. Ana says √−49 = −7. Is she right? Explain.

No. (−7)² = 49, not −49. No real number squared is negative.

Section 3 · Estimating square roots

Learning target: I can estimate a square root, then check it.

Words to know

WordWhat it means (in your own words)Example or picture
benchmarka perfect square near the number, to compare with49 and 64 are benchmarks for 50
estimatea close guess that you can explain√50 is about 7.1
≈"is about equal to"√50 ≈ 7.07

Use the perfect squares on each side

50 is not a perfect square. It sits between 49 and 64.

So √50 sits between √49 = 7 and √64 = 8.

Estimate a square root
  1. 1. Find the perfect squares just below and just above the number.
  2. 2. Their roots are your benchmarks. The answer is between them.
  3. 3. Decide which benchmark is closer. Estimate to one decimal place.
  4. 4. Check by squaring your estimate. Then use the √ key on a calculator.
Watch out — a common mistake

"√50 ≈ 25. Half of 50 is 25."

Why it is wrong: 25 × 25 = 625. That is far bigger than 50.

What is true: 7² = 49, so √50 is just over 7. √50 ≈ 7.07.

Watch out — a common mistake

"50 is between 49 and 64, so √50 ≈ 7.5. It's halfway."

Why it is wrong: 50 is not halfway from 49 to 64. It is only 1 past 49.

What is true: 7.5² = 56.25. √50 is close to 7: √50 ≈ 7.07.

Worked Example 1 — Estimate √30

Step 1: Benchmarks: 25 < 30 < 36. So 5 < √30 < 6.

Step 2: 30 is 5 past 25 and 6 below 36. It is near the middle, a bit closer to 25.

Step 3: Try 5.5. Check: 5.5² = 5.5 × 5.5 = 30.25. Very close to 30!

Step 4: Calculator: √30 = 5.477… ≈ 5.5

Worked Example 2 — A square garden

A square garden near Red Deer has an area of 50 m². How long is each side?

Step 1: Side = √50. Benchmarks: 7² = 49 and 8² = 64.

Step 2: Estimate: just over 7.

Step 3: Calculator: √50 = 7.071… ≈ 7.1 m.

How much fence goes around it? 4 × 7.07 ≈ 28.3 m.

Worked Example 3 — Estimate √110

Step 1: Benchmarks: 100 < 110 < 121. So 10 < √110 < 11.

Step 2: 110 is 10 past 100 and 11 below 121. Near the middle.

Step 3: Try 10.5. Check: 10.5² = 110.25. Close!

Step 4: Calculator: √110 = 10.488… ≈ 10.5

Perimeter and area of squares

From the side: area = side², perimeter = 4 × side.

From the area: side = √area.

From the perimeter: side = perimeter ÷ 4.

Worked Example 4 — A square dog run

A square dog run in Lethbridge has an area of 120 m². Find the length of fence around it.

Step 1: Side: s = √120. Benchmarks: 10² = 100, 11² = 121. Very close to 11.

Step 2: Calculator: √120 = 10.954… ≈ 10.95 m

Step 3: Fence: 4 × 10.954… ≈ 43.8 m

Explain it In Worked Example 4, why is 11 a good first estimate for √120?
Answer 11² = 121, which is only 1 more than 120. So √120 is just under 11.

Section 4 · Irrational numbers and the real number line

Learning target: I can sort and order rational and irrational numbers.

Words to know

WordWhat it means (in your own words)Example or picture
irrational numbera number you can't write as a fraction. Its decimal never ends and never repeats.√2, √50, π
real numbersall the rational and all the irrational numbers together−4, 1/3, √2, π
exact valuethe number itself, with nothing rounded√2, π
approximate valuea rounded decimal that is close√2 ≈ 1.41, π ≈ 3.14

√2 never ends

Find √2 on a calculator: 1.414213562… The digits keep going. No group repeats.

Zoom in. Each zoom gives one more digit, forever.

Rational or irrational?

Rational: you can write it as a/b. The decimal ends (0.25) or repeats (0.3).

Irrational: you can't. The decimal never ends and never repeats (1.414213…).

The square root of a perfect square is rational: √49 = 7, √9/16 = 3/4.

The square root of a non-perfect square is irrational: √2, √50, √120.

π is irrational too

Roll a wheel with a diameter of 1 m one full turn. It goes π m: 3 diameters and a bit.

π = 3.14159265… It never ends and never repeats.

Watch out — a common mistake

"π = 3.14 exactly." / "1.41 is √2."

Why it is wrong: Square 1.41: 1.41 × 1.41 = 1.9881. That is not 2. 1.41 is only close to √2.

What is true: √2 and π are exact. 1.41 and 3.14 are approximate: √2 ≈ 1.41 and π ≈ 3.14.

The real numbers

Every rational and every irrational number is a real number.

Each one has its own point on the number line.

Worked Example 1 — Sort the numbers

Sort: √36, √37, 0.6, π, −√4, 22/7

Step 1: √36 = 6. Natural.

Step 2: −√4 = −2. Integer.

Step 3: 0.6 repeats and 22/7 is a fraction. Rational.

Step 4: √37 and π never end or repeat. Irrational.
