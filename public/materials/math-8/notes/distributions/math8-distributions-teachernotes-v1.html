Teacher notes

Distributions & Circle Graphs

Grade 8 Mathematics · Statistics & Probability · 4 lessons + quiz · Outcomes 8ST1.1, 8ST1.2 · Codes DI1–DI4

Overview

Big idea: a distribution is how often each value, or each interval of values, comes up, and its shape only appears once the data is graphed. Categories and counted values go in bar graphs (bars with gaps); measured data goes in a histogram with equal intervals (bars touch, because no value falls between intervals). The shape is read from the outline: the number of peaks (unimodal, bimodal, uniform), whether the halves match (symmetric), and where the long tail points (skewed right or left; the tail, not the bump, names the skew). The shape then chooses the average: the tail pulls the mean toward it while the median barely moves, so symmetric data uses the mean, skewed data the median, and categories the mode. A circle graph is a different job: parts of one whole at one time, with each sector's angle = percent × 360°.

Sequence: distribution and frequency, bar graphs and double bar graphs, measured data in equal intervals, frequency tables, histograms, the effect of interval size (DI1) → counting peaks, symmetric, uniform, skewed right and left, comparing two random samples (DI2) → the tail pulls the mean, house prices, C-Train waits, symmetric July highs, categories and the mode (DI3) → the whole is 100% and 360°, percent to degrees, counts to percents to degrees, solving problems with a circle graph, angle back to a count, circle graph vs. line graph (DI4).

Materials: grid paper, rulers, protractors, compasses (or paper circles with a marked centre), calculators, sticky notes for a live class dot plot or histogram, chart paper. Optional: a spreadsheet or Desmos (desmos.com/calculator, histogram(list, width)) to redraw one data set with intervals of 5 and 10; CODAP (codap.concord.org) to drag data into a graph and watch the mean and median move.

Depth ceiling: histograms with equal intervals; bar and double bar graphs; the shape words unimodal, bimodal, uniform, symmetric, skewed right, skewed left; mean, median and mode (Grade 7 skills) chosen from the shape. Circle graphs from percents or counts, angles to the nearest degree. No box plots, quartiles, IQR or standard deviation (Grade 9 and beyond). "Outlier" is used informally (far from the rest), with no 1.5 × IQR rule.

Reading level: student pages are written at about Grade 3–4 (F&P N–P). The math words (distribution, frequency, bar graph, double bar graph, interval, histogram, peak, unimodal, bimodal, symmetric, uniform, tail, skewed right, skewed left, measure of central tendency, outlier, typical value, circle graph, sector, central angle, whole) are the learning; each has a picture beside it.

Notation: intervals are written with an en dash, 10–19, and a histogram's horizontal axis is labelled with the intervals. Percents are converted to decimals before multiplying (0.40 × 360° = 144°). Students show the check every time: frequencies add to the total, percents to 100%, angles to 360°.

Watch forWhat to do
"A histogram is a bar graph with gaps"Ask "where does 19.5 minutes go?" Put a sticky note on the boundary. Measured data has no gap, so the bars touch.
Unequal intervals (0–9, 10–14, 15–29)Count the values each interval can hold: 10, 5, 15. Unequal widths make the wide bar look more common than it is.
Overlapping intervals (10–20, 20–30)Ask "which bar does 20 go in?" Use 10–19, 20–29.
"Skewed right means the bump is on the right"Trace the outline with a finger from the peak along the long, low part. The finger points the way of the skew. Say "the tail tells".
Calling any lopsided graph "bimodal"Count clear peaks with a dip between them. One tall bar beside a slightly shorter one is still one peak.
"The mean is always the best average"House prices: 4 of 5 houses cost less than the $573 000 mean. Ask "would a buyer call $573 000 typical?"
Finding the median without ordering, or with an even countWrite the data in order first. With an even count, average the middle two (4 and 5 → 4.5).
Multiplying the percent by 100 instead of 360The whole circle is 360°, not 100. Check: the angles must add to 360°.
Using the count as the angle (90 visitors → 90°)Percent first: 90 ÷ 200 = 45%, then 0.45 × 360° = 162°.
Drawing each sector from the original radius, not from the end of the last oneModel with a protractor: draw a radius, mark the first angle, then start the next angle at the new line.
"A circle graph can show change over time"Ask "what is the whole?" Five years of July highs don't add to anything. Use a line graph.

Lessons

Lesson 1 — Bar graphs and histograms · 60 min
DOPre-knowledge check (5 min). Q1–Q3 mean, median and mode from Grade 7 (needed in Lesson 3), Q4 25% of 360 and Q5 90° as a fraction of a turn (preview circle graphs), Q6 reading a bar graph.
SHOWThe "how we get to school" bar graph. "What does each bar count?"
SAY"A distribution shows how often each value comes up. Categories go in a bar graph, with gaps. Measured data goes in a histogram, in equal intervals, and the bars touch."
CUEWorked Example 1 (read a bar graph: bus 20, total 44), Worked Example 2 (double bar graph, Grade 7 vs. Grade 8; biggest gap is car).
DOClass sticky-note histogram: students write their time to get to school (minutes) and place it in 0–9, 10–19, 20–29….
CUEWorked Example 3 (Edmonton commute times: tally, check the total of 20, draw the histogram).
WATCH"A histogram is a bar graph with gaps." Misconception box.
CUEWorked Example 4 (hockey ages, intervals of 10 vs. 5: the totals match, 5 shows more).
DOYour turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 1.
Lesson 2 — Shapes of distributions · 60 min
DOWarm-up interleaved: mean of 3, 5, 10; histogram or bar graph for heights; 20% of 360; median of 2, 8, 5, 9.
SHOWThe five shape sketches. "Count the peaks. Look for a tail."
SAY"One peak is unimodal, two is bimodal, flat is uniform. If the halves match it is symmetric. If there is a long tail, the tail names the skew."
CUEWorked Example 1 (Calgary July highs: symmetric), Worked Example 2 (Sunshine lift waits: skewed right).
WATCH"The bump is on the left, so it is skewed left." Misconception box: the tail tells.
CUEWorked Example 3 (easy quiz: skewed left; family skate: bimodal; die: uniform).
DOPairs predict and sketch shapes before seeing data: ages at a Grade 2 party with parents, wait times at a doctor's office, last digits of phone numbers.
CUEWorked Example 4 (two sleep samples: both symmetric near 9 h).
DOYour turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 2.
Lesson 3 — Choosing mean, median or mode · 60 min
DOWarm-up interleaved: name a shape from a sketch; mean of 10, 20, 60; 15% of 240; bar graph or histogram for favourite sport.
SHOWThe three shape sketches with mean and median marked. "Which way does the tail pull the mean?"
SAY"The mean uses every value, so a long tail pulls it toward the tail. The median is the middle and barely moves. Symmetric: use the mean. Skewed: use the median. Words: use the mode."
CUEWorked Example 1 (house prices: mean 573, median 350).
WATCH"The mean is always the best average." Misconception box: the number line with the big price.
CUEWorked Example 2 (C-Train waits: mean 7, median 4.5, mode 3), Worked Example 3 (symmetric highs: mean = median = 24), Worked Example 4 (way to school: mode).
DOYour turn, retrieval, exit ticket Section 3. Quiz next class.
Lesson 4 — Circle graphs · 60 min
DOMid-unit quiz (25 min) first (DI1–DI3). Then the lesson.
SHOWThe half-quarter-quarter circle graph. "Half the circle is 50%. How many degrees?"
SAY"A circle graph shows parts of one whole. The whole is 100% and 360°. Angle = percent × 360°."
CUEWorked Example 1 (240 students, percent to degrees, angles add to 360°). Model drawing with a protractor: radius, first angle, next angle from the new line.
CUEWorked Example 2 (Banff visitors: count → percent → angle), Worked Example 3 (home energy: 20% of $2000), Worked Example 4 (Stampede: 90° → 25% → 60 students).
WATCH"A circle graph can show change over time." Misconception box: use a line graph.
DOCore worksheet; Challenge for early finishers. Exit ticket Section 4. Unit synthesis (trip-to-school data) as review.

Assessment

Mid-unit quiz (23 marks)

DI1 (Q1–4), DI2 (Q5–7), DI3 (Q8–10). Given at the start of Lesson 4, before circle graphs (DI4). Calculators allowed. Q3: intervals must be the given ones; a histogram with gaps loses the drawing mark. Q4: full marks name the detail the smaller intervals show. Q6: "No: the tail names the skew" is the key idea. Q8: mean = 56 ÷ 7 = 8, median 6, mode 5. Q9 is the explain-your-thinking item: full marks choose the median and say the 21-minute wait pulls the mean up. Q10: mode (categories).

Worksheets and exits

Core (★) covers DI1–DI4 with a diagram on most questions: choosing a graph for four data sets, a double bar graph of Stampede attendance, a homework-time histogram, a histogram drawn with gaps, one interval that hides the shape, naming shapes, the "tallest bars on the left" error, two commute samples, mean/median/mode from the context, clinic wait times, a Stampede circle graph from counts, and an angle back to a count. Challenge (★★) adds redrawing Banff visitor ages with intervals of 10, predicting shapes (seniors' home, phone digits, a Grade 1 picnic), mean vs. median without calculating, the misleading "typical salary", writing data with mean > median, a seasons circle graph, and an energy bill. Each misconception has at least one targeted question; the key names it. Exit tickets: one per section, each ending with an "explain how you know" item.

Home practice

Two parent-friendly sheets: "The shape of data" (Sections 2 and 3) and "Circle graphs" (Section 4, with a which-graph question from Section 1). Each has a worked example, 6–7 questions and a separate answer sheet.

Data and contexts used

  • Household energy shares (space heating 60%, water heating 20%, appliances 15%, lighting 5%) are rounded from Natural Resources Canada averages for Canadian homes; the $2000 and $2400 yearly bills are made up.
  • Calgary's July daily highs average about 23 °C; the histogram of 31 days and the five yearly July highs (2021–2025) are made-up example data, labelled as such on the line graph.
  • Edmonton commute times, hockey player ages, Sunshine Village lift waits, C-Train waits, house prices in a small Alberta town, the shop salaries and all survey counts (way to school, Banff visitors, Stampede events, favourite sports) are made up but realistic.
  • Stampede attendance figures in the practice double bar graph are made up (real daily attendance is roughly 100 000–150 000).
  • Sleep samples are made up; 9 hours sits within the 8–10 hours recommended for teenagers.

Connections

  • Grade 7 Statistics: mean, median, mode and range, and dot plots, are the tools this unit chooses between.
  • Grade 7 Percents: percent of a number and percent from a part and a whole drive every circle-graph calculation.
  • Grade 7 Circles and Angles: a full turn is 360°; drawing angles with a protractor.
  • Proportional Reasoning (topic 3): percent × 360° is a constant of proportionality (k = 3.6° per percent).
  • Grade 9: box plots, quartiles and the interquartile range build on shape and the median.
