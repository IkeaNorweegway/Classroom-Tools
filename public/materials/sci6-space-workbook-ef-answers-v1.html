Stretch Zone — Challenge Questions
S1
If you shrank the solar system so that the Sun was a basketball, how large and how far away would Earth be? Use the scale factor (Sun = 22 cm diameter, real Sun = 1.4 million km) to estimate. Show your thinking.
Answer Scale factor: Sun model (22 cm) ÷ real Sun (1.4 million km) ≈ 1.57 × 10⁻⁸ cm per km. Earth diameter (12,700 km) × scale factor ≈ 0.02 cm ≈ a small pea (about 2 mm). Earth distance from Sun (150 million km) × scale factor ≈ 235 cm ≈ about 2.4 m from the basketball Sun. Award marks for setting up the proportion correctly and showing a reasonable estimate, even if arithmetic varies slightly.
S2
The Voyager 1 spacecraft was launched in 1977. It is now in interstellar space — beyond the solar system. It is about 23 billion km from Earth. Calculate how many AU this is (1 AU = 150 million km). What does this tell you about the scale of space beyond our solar system?
Answer 23 billion km ÷ 150 million km per AU ≈ 153 AU. This tells us: the space beyond our solar system is vastly larger than the solar system itself (Neptune is ~30 AU). Even after 45+ years of travel, Voyager has barely reached the nearest stars — the nearest star is ~268,000 AU away. Award marks for correct division and a meaningful interpretation of the scale.
S3
Scientists recently discovered an exoplanet (a planet outside our solar system) in the "habitable zone" of its star — meaning liquid water might exist there. The planet is 40 light-years away. Even at the speed of light, it would take 40 years to get a signal there. What challenges does this create for exploring or communicating with other star systems?
Answer Challenges: signals take 40 years one way (80 years for a reply) — no real-time communication possible. Travel time at current speeds would be tens of thousands of years. We cannot know what conditions are like there now — we see 40-year-old information. Any mission would last far longer than a human lifetime. Accept any response capturing: the communication delay, the travel time, and the impossibility of real-time interaction.
S4
Many First Nations groups in Canada have traditional knowledge of specific stars and constellations. This knowledge guided travel, predicted seasons, and marked ceremonies. How could collaboration between Indigenous knowledge holders and modern astronomers benefit both groups?
Answer Indigenous astronomers contribute: long-term observations (centuries of records) of star patterns, seasonal cycles, and specific objects — filling gaps in written scientific records. Modern astronomers contribute: instrumentation, mathematical modelling, and global data networks. Benefits for both: Indigenous knowledge adds historical depth and local specificity; modern science provides tools to verify and extend that knowledge. Mutual respect leads to a more complete picture of the sky. Award marks for addressing benefits to both groups.
