No country has enough of everything for everyone. Land, workers, tools, money, and time are all limited. This problem is called  .

Retrieval Check — notes closed
  1. Define scarcity in your own words.
    Answer Define scarcity in your own words. The idea that there are never enough resources (land, workers, tools, money, time) to give everyone everything they want.
  2. What are the three basic economic questions every society must answer?
    Answer What are the three basic economic questions? What to produce, how to produce it, and for whom to produce it.
  3. What is opportunity cost? Give an example from your own life.
    Answer What is opportunity cost? Give an example. The next-best thing you give up when you choose one option over another. Example: choosing to spend an hour on homework means giving up an hour of free time.
  4. Application: A farmer has one field. She can plant wheat or plant corn, but not both. Explain how this shows scarcity forcing a choice.
    Answer Application — the farmer's field. She has a limited resource (the field). Growing wheat means she cannot also grow corn on that same land at the same time — scarcity forces her to choose one use of the field over the other.
  5. ★ Stretch A city has enough money for either more buses or lower bus fares — not both. Using the three economic questions and opportunity cost, explain what the city must decide and what it gives up either way.
    Answer ★ StretchThe city's buses. The city must decide what to produce (more buses vs. lower fares), effectively for whom (riders who need more service vs. riders who need affordability), using its limited budget. Whichever it picks, the other option is the opportunity cost — e.g., if it buys more buses, cheaper fares are what it gives up.

Every country must answer the three economic questions from Section 1. The plan a country uses to answer them is called an  .

Misconception

"Every country is either a total market economy or a total command economy. There is nothing in between."

No real country works this way. The United States is often called a market economy — but it still runs public schools and programs like Social Security. China is a command economy in many ways — but it now allows many private businesses. If you tried to sort every real country into "pure market" or "pure command," almost none would fit cleanly into either box.

Correct understanding: Most real economies are mixed — a blend of individual choice and government control. They sit somewhere along a spectrum, not at one extreme end.

The Economic Spectrum

The economic spectrum is a line. It shows who decides, and how much control the government holds.

  • One end: individuals and businesses decide almost everything. Little government control. This is a   economy.

Knowing what type of economic system a country has is not enough. We also need a way to check how well its economy is actually doing. Economists use three main tools.

Tool 1 — GDP (Gross Domestic Product): the total dollar value of all goods and services a country produces in one year. When GDP is growing, the economy is producing   than before.

Tool 2 — CPI (Consumer Price Index): tracks how the price of a "basket" of common goods and services changes over time. When CPI rises quickly, we say there is   — prices are going up and money buys less than it used to.

Tool 3 — Unemployment rate: the percentage of people who want a job and are looking for one, but do not have one.

Retrieval Check — notes closed
  1. What does GDP measure?
    Answer What does GDP measure? The total dollar value of all goods and services a country produces in one year.
  2. What is the relationship between a rising CPI and inflation?
    Answer Relationship between rising CPI and inflation. A rising CPI (average prices of a basket of goods going up) is what inflation means — money buys less than it used to.
  3. Why do economists look at GDP, CPI, and unemployment together instead of using just one measure?
    Answer Why look at all three together? No single measure tells the whole story — e.g., GDP can grow while unemployment also rises, so looking at all three gives a fuller picture of who is actually benefiting.
  4. Application: A country's GDP is growing quickly, but its unemployment rate is also rising fast. What might this tell you about who is benefiting from that growth?
    Answer Application — GDP up, unemployment also up. This could mean the growth is coming from fewer, more productive workers or automation rather than new jobs — ordinary workers may not be sharing in the benefit.
  5. ★ Stretch A news report says, "Our country's GDP is at an all-time high — the economy has never been better." What questions would you ask before agreeing with that claim?
    Answer ★ StretchQuestioning the GDP headline. Accept answers that ask: Is unemployment also changing? Is CPI/inflation rising too (so is the value of that growth being eaten by higher prices)? Is the GDP growth spread across the population or concentrated among a few?

Misconception

"Taxes mostly just pay for roads. That's the one main thing my tax dollars go to."

Roads are only a small slice of what taxes fund. If taxes only paid for roads, there would be no public schools, hospitals, police, firefighters, or income support for people who need help. All of these depend on tax revenue too.

Correct understanding: Taxes fund a broad basket of services — healthcare, education, infrastructure, social programs, public safety, and more.

Section 5  ·  Surplus, Deficit, and Debt

Learning target: I can explain the difference between a deficit and debt, and describe why governments sometimes choose to run a deficit on purpose.

Vocabulary

TermDefinition in your own wordsOne example
Surplus  
Deficit  
Debt  
Balanced budget  

Every year, a government compares what it collected in revenue to what it spent. Three things can happen:

  • Surplus: revenue is greater than spending — money is left over.
  • Balanced budget: revenue equals spending exactly.
  • Deficit: spending is greater than revenue — there is a shortfall.
Misconception

"A deficit and debt are the same thing."

A deficit is what happens in one single year — when a government spends more than it collects. Debt is the total amount built up from every year the government has spent more than it collected, added together over time. Mixing them up is like confusing "I spent $50 more than I earned this month" with "I owe $2,000 total, added up from every month I've overspent for the last few years."

Correct understanding: A deficit is one year's gap between spending and revenue. Debt is the running total of every past deficit added together (minus any surpluses that helped pay some of it down).

Worked example: A government collects $100 billion and spends $108 billion this year.

This year's deficit = $  billion.

If the government already owed $50 billion from past years, its total debt is now $  billion.

Each year's deficit adds to the total debt. Debt keeps growing unless a surplus pays some of it back down.
Misconception

"Running a deficit always means the government is being careless with money."

Sometimes governments plan a deficit on purpose. During an emergency — a pandemic, a flood, a recession — a government may need to spend more than it collects right away, to help people and businesses survive. Governments also sometimes borrow money to build things like hospitals or transit systems that will help the economy for decades — an investment now that is expected to pay off later. These are deliberate choices, not always mistakes.

Correct understanding: A deficit can be a planned, reasonable choice, depending on why it happens and what the money is used for. It becomes a real problem mainly when debt grows so large, for so long, that it limits what future governments can afford to do.

Retrieval Check — notes closed
  1. Define surplus, deficit, and debt in your own words.
    Answer Define surplus, deficit, and debt. Surplus = revenue greater than spending in one year. Deficit = spending greater than revenue in one year. Debt = the running total of all past deficits (minus surpluses) added together over time.
  2. A government collected $90 billion and spent $95 billion this year. It already owed $40 billion. What is this year's deficit? What is the new total debt?
    Answer Calculation — $90B revenue, $95B spending, $40B prior debt. This year's deficit = $5 billion. New total debt = $45 billion.
  3. A student says: "The government ran a deficit this year, so it must be managing money badly." Is this always true? Explain.
    Answer Error analysis — "a deficit means bad management." Not always true — governments sometimes run deficits on purpose, such as during an emergency or to invest in long-term infrastructure. Whether it is good or bad management depends on the reason and how the money is used, not just the fact that a deficit occurred.
  4. Application: A province borrows money during a major flood to help rebuild homes and roads immediately. Explain why this might be a reasonable use of a deficit.
    Answer Application — flood recovery deficit. This is a reasonable use of a deficit because the spending responds to an emergency that could not be predicted or delayed — rebuilding homes and roads quickly protects people and the local economy, which can be worth the short-term cost.
  5. ★ Stretch A government has run a growing deficit for fifteen years in a row with no plan to change course. Explain, using the idea of interest payments, why this pattern is different from a single emergency-year deficit.
    Answer ★ Stretch15 years of deficits vs. one emergency year. A single emergency-year deficit is a planned, temporary response with a clear reason. Fifteen years of deficits with no plan means debt keeps growing every year, so a larger and larger share of revenue must go to interest payments on that debt — leaving progressively less money for healthcare, education, and other services. The risk is the long, unplanned pattern, not the existence of any one deficit.
