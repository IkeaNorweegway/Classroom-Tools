Q1 ★ Core
Students at Riverside School are building a wooden bridge over a small creek. The main beam is 4 metres long.

When a student walks across the middle of the beam, the top of the beam gets squeezed together. The bottom of the beam gets pulled apart.

(a) What is the name of the internal force acting on the top of the beam?
(b) What is the name of the internal force acting on the bottom of the beam?
(c) What causes these internal forces to appear in the beam?
Answer (a) Compression — the top of the beam is being squeezed together by the load pressing down.
(b) Tension — the bottom of the beam is being pulled apart as it bends.
(c) The external force (the walker's weight pressing down) causes the beam to bend. As it bends, the material inside resists deformation — producing compression at the top and tension at the bottom as internal forces.
Circulation: if students reverse the two, ask "if I squeezed clay from both sides, is the middle being pulled or pushed?"

Q3 ★ Core
Steel railway tracks run through southern Alberta. Steel — like all matter — is made of tiny particles. On a hot summer day, the steel rail feels warm to the touch. Its total length increases slightly.

Use the particle model to explain what is happening inside the steel rail as it heats up.

Sentence starter: When the rail heats up, the particles inside...
Answer When the rail heats up, particles absorb energy and vibrate faster. As they move faster, they push slightly further apart — increasing the spacing between particles. This causes the rail's overall length and volume to increase (thermal expansion).
Key language: particles moving faster · spacing increasing · volume/length increasing.
Redirect students who say "the rail absorbs water" — keep focused on the particle model.
Q4 ★ Core
In the last 30 years, the town of High River, Alberta has had three major floods. A local resident says: "The weather has been getting worse." A climate scientist says: "The climate in this region has changed."

These two people are making different kinds of claims.

(a) What is the difference between weather and climate?
(b) Which statement — the resident's or the scientist's — needs long-term data to support it? Explain why.
Answer (a) Weather = short-term atmospheric conditions in a specific place and time (temperature, precipitation, wind on a given day). Climate = long-term patterns of weather in a region over decades or longer.
(b) Three floods over 30 years is consistent weather variability data — but to claim the climate has changed, you need decades of data showing a consistent trend or shift in the long-term pattern. The scientist is making a claim about the long-term system, not any single event.

Q9 ★ Core
The timber bridge from Question 1 has two types of wooden parts.
  • The walking planks flex slightly when a student steps on them, then spring back to their original shape.
  • One railing post was hit by a bicycle and is now bent out of shape for good.
(a) What property describes the walking planks' behaviour?
(b) What property describes what happened to the railing post?

Answer (a) Elasticity (elastic deformation) — the planks flex and spring back; temporary change.

Q18 ★ Core

A climate scientist has 30 years of temperature data showing an average regional warming of 1.8°C.

Answer Strong answer: The two types of evidence work together because they measure the same phenomenon from different perspectives and timescales. Q19 ★ Core Visual
The diagram below shows a student standing on the timber bridge deck. Arrow A points downward from the student's feet — this is the student's weight pressing on the deck.
(a) What is the scientific name for Arrow A — the downward force the student pushes on the bridge?

(b) Reaction arrow: points upward from bridge deck toward student · labelled 500 N · Normal force (or reaction force / contact force).

Q20 ★★ Extended
Water in the rock cracks near the railway line freezes in winter. Unlike almost every other substance, water is less dense as a solid than as a liquid — which is why ice floats.
(a) Use the particle model to explain why ice is less dense than liquid water. What is unusual about how water particles arrange themselves when they freeze?
(b) Imagine water acted like most substances — that solid water was denser than liquid water. What would happen to a lake in winter? What effect would this have on aquatic life?
Answer (a) In most substances, particles pack more tightly when they freeze (solid = denser than liquid). Water particles, however, arrange into a more open structure when they freeze — with more space between particles than in liquid water. This makes ice less dense than liquid water. (At Grade 6: convey that particle arrangement in ice is more spread out/open than liquid water.)
(b) If ice were denser, it would sink. Lakes would freeze from the bottom up and could freeze solid in Alberta winters — leaving no liquid water layer. The floating ice layer insulates the water below; without it, no protected zone exists for aquatic life to survive winter.

Q31 ★★ Extended

If you look closely at a concrete sidewalk or railway track in Alberta, you will notice small gaps built in at regular intervals. These are called expansion joints.

(a) Use your knowledge of expansion and contraction to explain why these gaps are needed.
(b) What would happen to the railway track over several seasons if the expansion joints were not there? Use particle model language in your answer.
Answer (a) Steel and concrete expand when heated and contract when cooled. In Alberta, the seasonal range is extreme (–30°C to +35°C). Expansion joints allow material to expand in summer without pressing against adjacent sections. Without the gap, expanding sections push against each other, building enormous compressive forces.
(b) Without joints: sections expand and press together. Compressive forces build until the material buckles, curves sideways, or cracks. Particle model: particles move faster and push further apart — but if expansion is blocked, they press against adjacent sections with increasing force, causing structural failure (buckling = compression-driven internal force failure).

S1 ★★ Matter Forces
The railway track near Lethbridge is made of sections of steel rail, each 25 m long. In summer, each section expands by about 1.5 cm. Engineers install small expansion gaps between sections to allow for this.
(a) Use the particle model to explain what causes the steel to expand.
(b) When two sections press against each other at a closed expansion gap, what type of internal force is acting at the contact point between them? How do you know?
(c) If the rails were welded solid with no gap, what would eventually happen to the track? What type of structural failure would occur? Name the internal force involved.
Answer (a) Heat causes steel particles to gain energy, vibrate faster, and push further apart — increasing the overall length of the rail (thermal expansion, particle model).
(b) Compression — the expanding sections press against each other (pushed together). The internal force at the contact point is compressive.
(c) Without expansion gaps, compressive force builds until the rail buckles sideways (thermal buckling). The material exceeds its compressive limit and deforms permanently outward to relieve the force. This is a compression failure.
Cross-unit connection: particle model explains why expansion happens; internal forces concept explains what structural failure results.
S2 ★★ Living Systems Climate
The boreal forest near Fort McMurray is one of the largest intact forest ecosystems on Earth.
(a) Use your knowledge of photosynthesis to explain how a healthy boreal forest slows the buildup of CO₂ in the atmosphere.
(b) When the forest burns in a wildfire, dead trees release large amounts of CO₂. At the same time, there are fewer living trees to absorb CO₂. How does this create a feedback loop that speeds up climate change?
(c) How does this connection between Living Systems and Climate make the boreal forest important beyond just Alberta?
Answer (a) Trees absorb CO₂ during photosynthesis and incorporate the carbon into biomass (wood, leaves). A healthy boreal forest continuously removes large amounts of CO₂ from the atmosphere — acting as a carbon sink.
(b) Feedback loop: wildfire releases stored carbon as CO₂ → more CO₂ in atmosphere → stronger greenhouse effect → warmer temperatures → drier conditions → more fires. The forest that was removing carbon now adds it, and there are fewer trees to absorb CO₂ going forward.
(c) The boreal forest is one of Earth's largest carbon sinks. Its loss affects global CO₂ concentrations — not just Alberta's climate. This connects Alberta's forest health to global climate stability.

S4 ★★ Forces Living Systems
A large spruce tree in the boreal forest bends a lot in a powerful windstorm but does not break. A smaller, dead branch on the same tree does snap.
(a) Name and describe the internal forces acting on the living tree trunk as it bends.
(b) The living trunk returns to its upright position when the wind stops. What property of the living wood makes this possible?
(c) The dead branch snapped for good. What property has been exceeded? What is different about dead wood that makes this more likely?
Answer (a) Windward side (facing wind): tension — being stretched as wind pushes the crown away. Leeward side: compression — being squeezed as trunk bends. The outer surface on the windward side may also experience shear as wood layers slide relative to each other.
(b) Elasticity — living wood returns to original shape when wind stops. This is elastic deformation; the wood is below its elastic limit.
(c) Plasticity — the dead branch has exceeded its elastic limit; the deformation is permanent. Dead wood is typically less flexible (more brittle) because it has lost the moisture and cellular structure that gives living wood its elasticity.
Cross-unit connection: Both bridge (Forces) and tree (Living Systems context) demonstrate the same internal force principles — elasticity and plasticity operate in biological materials, not only engineered ones.
