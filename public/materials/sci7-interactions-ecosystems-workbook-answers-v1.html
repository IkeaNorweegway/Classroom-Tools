Q1 ★ Core Alberta

Cluster 1 — Ecosystems and their components · Apply (LO 7-1-01).

A researcher is studying a beaver pond near Jasper, Alberta. During a single afternoon she records eight observations:

  • Water temperature: 14°C.
  • Three beavers actively chewing aspen branches at the water's edge.
  • A great blue heron standing motionless in the shallows.
  • Dense aquatic vegetation along the eastern bank.
  • Dissolved oxygen level: moderate.
  • Muddy silt bottom.
  • pH: 7.2.
  • Several dragonfly larvae visible under rocks.

(a) Sort each observation into the correct column of the table below.

Items to sort
water temperature dissolved oxygen beavers chewing aspen great blue heron aquatic vegetation muddy silt bottom pH 7.2 dragonfly larvae
Biotic factors (living) Abiotic factors (non-living)

(b) The researcher returns in January. Water temperature has dropped to 2°C, and ice now covers 80% of the pond surface. Predict how this abiotic change would affect at least two biotic factors. Name each organism and describe the change you expect.

(c) A logging company wants to clear the aspen forest around this pond. Besides removing trees, explain two ways this would change the pond's abiotic conditions. Do not just say which animals would leave.

Answer

(a) Biotic: beavers chewing aspen, great blue heron, aquatic vegetation, dragonfly larvae. Abiotic: water temperature, dissolved oxygen, muddy silt bottom, pH 7.2.

(b) Any two, linked to the cold and ice:

  • Aquatic plants: ice and snow block sunlight, so they photosynthesize much less and many die back.
  • Beavers: they stay in their lodge and live off a stored food cache of branches under the ice.
  • Great blue heron: it cannot fish through ice, so it migrates south (it will not be there).
  • Dragonfly larvae: they slow down in the cold water and grow slowly until spring. Oxygen may also drop under the ice.

(c) Any two: more sunlight reaches the pond, so the water gets warmer and holds less oxygen; without roots holding the soil, more erosion washes silt into the pond, making it muddier and shallower; runoff changes water levels and brings in more nutrients; less shade and windbreak means bigger temperature swings.

Error 2:

(b) Imagine all decomposers vanish overnight in a boreal forest in northern Alberta. Trace what happens to the ecosystem over the next ten years. Your answer must cover three things: what happens to dead matter, what happens to soil nutrients, and what happens to producers. Use at least three linked steps.

(c) Sketch a simple four-organism food chain (grass → snowshoe hare → lynx → decomposers). Explain why the arrow from lynx to decomposers points that way. What does the arrow show about energy?

Sketch your food chain here. Label each arrow to show the direction energy flows.
Answer

(a)

  • Error 1: "Decomposers … are not really part of the food web." They are part of it: they get their energy from dead organisms and wastes from every level, so they are the last link in every food chain.
  • Error 2: "You could remove them and the rest of the ecosystem would keep working fine." Without decomposers, nutrients are never returned to the soil, so producers eventually cannot grow and the whole web collapses.

(b) Dead leaves, animals and wastes pile up and do not rot → the nutrients (such as nitrogen and phosphorus) stay locked inside dead matter, so soil nutrients run out → producers (spruce, aspen, grasses) grow poorly and die → herbivores (hare, moose) lose food and decline → predators (lynx, wolves) decline. Over ten years the forest would be smothered in litter and starving for nutrients.

(c) grass → snowshoe hare → lynx → decomposers. Each arrow points toward the organism that receives the energy. When the lynx dies, or leaves wastes, the energy stored in its body passes to the decomposers that break it down.

(b) Coyotes were extirpated (locally wiped out) from this grassland in the early 1900s because of hunting. Use the food web to predict the effect on three other species over the next five years. Q5 ★ Core Alberta

Cluster 5 — Environmental change · trophic cascade · Apply (LO 7-1-04).

In the 1950s, wolves were removed from Banff National Park. Over the next 30 years, scientists observed these changes:

  • Elk populations tripled.
  • Willows, aspens, and sedges along riverbanks were heavily grazed down.
  • Stream banks eroded a lot.
  • Beaver populations declined sharply.
  • Songbird species in riparian zones declined.

(a) Which trophic level was hit first by wolf removal? Name it, and explain which interaction was disrupted.

(b) Removing the wolves set off a chain reaction that eventually reached the beavers. Fill in what happened at each step below, then mark whether that step is a biotic (living thing) or abiotic (non-living thing) change.

Wolves removed from Banff
↓
Elk populations  Biotic or abiotic? 
↓
Riparian plants (willows, aspens, sedges)  Biotic or abiotic? 
↓
Stream banks  Biotic or abiotic? 
↓
Beavers  Biotic or abiotic? 

In one or two sentences, explain how the change at one step causes the next one.

(c) This chain of effects has a name: a trophic cascade. One top predator was removed. That caused changes across many trophic levels. It even changed the physical environment. Does this evidence show that ecosystems are stable or fragile? Take a side. Back it up with evidence from the Banff example.

Answer

(a) The primary consumers (elk) were affected first. The wolf–elk predator–prey interaction was removed, so nothing kept elk numbers in check.

(b)

  • Elk populations: grew (tripled) — biotic.
  • Riparian plants: heavily grazed down, so fewer willows and aspens — biotic.
  • Stream banks: without plant roots holding the soil, they eroded — abiotic.
  • Beavers: declined, because they lost the willows and aspens they eat and build with — biotic.

Each change removes something the next level depends on: more elk eat more plants; fewer plants means fewer roots holding the banks and less food and building material for beavers.

(c) Either side is acceptable with evidence. Fragile: removing one species changed several trophic levels and even the physical shape of the streams. Stable: some argue the system recovers when wolves return (wolves were reintroduced to Banff in the 1980s–90s). The best answers say the evidence shows ecosystems are fragile to the loss of a top predator.

Q6 ★★ Extended Alberta

Cluster 6 — Sustainability and stewardship · Traditional Ecological Knowledge · Evaluate (LO 7-1-04, 7-1-05).

For thousands of years, Blackfoot (Niitsitapi) peoples of southern Alberta used controlled burning to manage grassland ecosystems. They burned patches of prairie on purpose, in rotation. When European settlers arrived in the 1800s, they stopped all fires. They believed fire only caused damage.

(a) Describe two benefits that regular grass fire brings to a prairie ecosystem. Use what you know about nutrient cycling and species diversity to explain each one.

(b) European settlers stopped fires for over 100 years. Predict two long-term effects on the grassland ecosystem. What would happen to species diversity and soil nutrients over time?

(c) A government ecologist claims: "Indigenous burning practices were just traditional customs — they weren't really ecological management." Judge this claim using evidence from parts (a) and (b). Does Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) match the scientific understanding of ecosystem health? Explain your reasoning with evidence.

Answer

(a) Any two: fire recycles nutrients by turning dead grass into ash that fertilizes the soil; it clears dead material so new grass grows and gets sunlight; it stops shrubs and trees from taking over, which keeps open grassland habitat and species diversity; fresh regrowth attracts grazers such as bison.

(b) Dead plant material builds up and nutrients stay locked in it, so the soil becomes poorer; shrubs and trees spread into the prairie, and grassland species diversity drops; the built-up fuel makes later wildfires larger and more destructive.

(c) The claim is wrong. Blackfoot burning did exactly what ecologists now recommend: it cycled nutrients, kept grassland diversity, and reduced dangerous fuel build-up. The evidence from (a) and (b) shows that Traditional Ecological Knowledge matches the scientific understanding of ecosystem health. It was ecological management, based on long-term observation.

Q8 ★★ Extended Alberta

Cluster 1 — Biotic-abiotic interactions · Analyze (LO 7-1-01, 7-1-04).

A pulp mill starts releasing warm water into the North Saskatchewan River near Edmonton. This raises the average water temperature by 3°C, year-round. Over the next five years, scientists monitoring the river record these changes:

  • Dissolved oxygen drops by 25%.
  • Bull trout (a cold-water species) populations decline sharply.
  • Common carp (warm-water tolerant) begin appearing in large numbers.
  • Aquatic insect diversity drops 40%.
  • Algae blooms grow fast each summer.

(a) The mill is changing an abiotic factor. Trace the cause-and-effect chain from the temperature rise to the bull trout decline. Use at least three linked steps. Say exactly which abiotic change harms the trout, and why.

(b) Carp are generalists — they can handle a wide range of temperatures. Bull trout need cold, well-oxygenated water. Carp are now replacing bull trout. What does this tell us about biodiversity in this part of the river?

(c) The mill manager states: "Fish populations are recovering — we can see lots of carp now." Judge this claim. Is species count alone a reliable measure of ecosystem health? What has actually happened to biodiversity, and what is the manager getting wrong?

Answer

(a) Warm water enters the river → water temperature rises 3°C → warm water holds less dissolved oxygen (down 25%) → bull trout, a cold-water species, cannot get enough oxygen and are stressed by the heat → they cannot grow or reproduce well → the population declines. The harmful abiotic change is the drop in dissolved oxygen caused by the warmer water.

(b) Biodiversity is falling. A tolerant generalist (carp) is replacing a sensitive specialist (bull trout), and insect diversity has dropped 40%. Fewer kinds of organisms survive, and the community is becoming simpler.

(c) The claim is misleading. Lots of carp is not recovery: the native, sensitive species are disappearing and insect diversity has crashed. Species count or total fish numbers alone do not show health. You must look at which species are present, their diversity and their balance.

Q9 ★ Core Alberta

Cluster 4 — Biodiversity · keystone species · Apply (LO 7-1-03).

Grizzly bears in Alberta's mountain ecosystems are a keystone species. That means their effect on the ecosystem is much bigger than their small population would suggest. Grizzlies eat berries, ground squirrels, carrion, and salmon (in BC). When they drag salmon carcasses inland, they carry marine nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus) into the forest soil. This fertilizes trees up to 500 m from the stream.

(a) Explain what "keystone species" means, in your own words. The term comes from architecture. A keystone is the center stone in an arch — it holds all the other stones in place. Explain how this compares to a keystone species in an ecosystem.

(b) Grizzly bears are listed as threatened in Alberta. Predict two ecosystem effects if grizzly bears were extirpated (wiped out locally) from their Alberta mountain habitat. For each effect: name it. Say which organisms are most affected. Explain how it happens.

(c) A landowner says: "Grizzlies are dangerous and rare — only a few hundred left. Getting rid of the last ones wouldn't really affect the ecosystem." Using the definition of keystone species, explain exactly why this reasoning is wrong.

Answer

(a) A keystone species has a much larger effect on its ecosystem than its numbers suggest. Like a keystone holding an arch together, removing it makes much of the ecosystem collapse or change.

(b) Any two:

  • Less nutrient transfer: forest soils and trees near streams lose the nitrogen and phosphorus from carcasses, so trees grow more slowly.
  • Prey populations rise: ground squirrels and others are no longer controlled.
  • Fewer seeds spread: grizzlies spread berry seeds in their scat, so berry shrubs spread less.
  • Scavengers lose food: they lose the carrion that grizzlies leave behind.

(c) A keystone species matters because of its role, not its numbers. Even a few hundred grizzlies move nutrients, control prey and spread seeds. Removing the last ones would set off changes across the whole ecosystem.

Answer

Q11 ★ Core Alberta

Cluster 6 — Sustainability · invasive species · ecosystem services · Apply (LO 7-1-04).

Purple loosestrife is an invasive plant from Europe. It was first recorded in Alberta in the mid-1900s. It grows fast in wetlands and along riverbanks, forming thick mats that crowd out native plants like cattails, bulrushes, and sedges. By the early 2000s, it had spread across thousands of hectares of Alberta wetland.

(a) Invasive species are a major threat to biodiversity. Use purple loosestrife as your example. Explain how one invasive plant can lower biodiversity. It does not need to kill any animals directly.

(b) Wetlands provide ecosystem services. These are benefits that ecosystems give to humans and other species. Name two ecosystem services that Alberta wetlands provide. For each one, explain how a purple loosestrife invasion would reduce that service.

(c) Alberta uses a biocontrol program to manage the plant. This means releasing Galerucella beetles, which eat loosestrife back in Europe. What is one ecological risk of bringing in a second non-native species to control the first? How would scientists decide whether this tradeoff is worth it?

Answer

(a) Purple loosestrife grows fast and forms thick mats that crowd out native plants (cattails, bulrushes, sedges). Animals that depend on those plants for food, shelter or nesting lose their habitat and leave or decline. Fewer plant species means fewer animal species too.

(b) Any two:

  • Water filtering / cleaning: native wetland plants trap sediment and absorb pollutants; dense loosestrife mats do this less well.
  • Flood control / water storage: loosestrife clogs channels and changes how water flows and is stored.
  • Habitat for wildlife: ducks, muskrats and amphibians lose food and nesting plants.
  • Recreation: hunting, birdwatching and boating suffer.

(c) Risk: the beetle could start eating native plants, or spread in ways no one expected, and become a new invasive species. To decide: scientists test the beetle in controlled trials to show that it eats only loosestrife, start with small releases, and monitor the results. They compare the risk with the damage loosestrife is already causing.

Q12 ★★ Extended Alberta

Cluster 5 — Environmental change · cumulative effects · habitat fragmentation · Evaluate (LO 7-1-04).

In the Peace River region of northern Alberta, a 200 km2 area of boreal forest is being turned into farmland. This is happening over 15 years. The forest is home to woodland caribou, black bears, wolves, moose, snowshoe hares, and hundreds of bird and plant species. Each year, more sections are cleared.

Developer claim to evaluate

"We're only clearing 5% of the forest this year — that's a small impact. The ecosystem will recover between clearing phases. Nature always bounces back."

(a) The developer's claim ignores cumulative effects. Define cumulative effects. Then explain why clearing 5% per year for 15 years is not the same, ecologically, as one 75% disturbance followed by full recovery time.

(b) Woodland caribou need large, unbroken areas of undisturbed old-growth forest. Clearing creates "edges" — boundaries between forest and open land. Wolves hunt more easily along these edges, because prey are easier to see. Explain how forest fragmentation can harm caribou, even when the total area cleared seems small.

(c) Is the claim "nature always bounces back" backed by science? Name two conditions that decide whether an ecosystem can recover from disturbance. Then explain whether those conditions are likely to be met in this scenario.

Answer

(a) Cumulative effects are the combined impact of many small changes that build up over time. Clearing 5% a year for 15 years removes about 75% of the forest without any recovery time in between, because forest takes decades to regrow. Each new clearing adds to the damage already done, and the remaining forest is broken into ever smaller pieces.

(b) Clearing breaks the forest into fragments with many edges. Wolves travel and hunt easily along edges, so caribou are caught more often. The undisturbed old-growth areas caribou need become too small and too broken up. Even a small cleared area can cut a habitat into pieces.

(c) No. Recovery depends on conditions such as: enough time between disturbances; a large enough area of intact habitat left to recolonize from; the type of disturbance (land turned permanently to farmland never returns to forest); and whether the key species survive. Here, the land is permanently converted and cleared every year, so these conditions are not met.

S1 Stretch

Clusters 3 + 4 — Energy pyramid · Biodiversity · Create / Evaluate.

You are asked to design a self-sustaining 1 km2 nature reserve in central Alberta. Choose organisms for each trophic level so the ecosystem can run on its own for 20 years, with no human help.

(a) Using the 10% rule and the energy pyramid, explain why you cannot stock equal numbers of producers and top predators. What ratio would you expect between trophic levels? Why does the math force this?

(b) You can choose one keystone species to include. Pick an Alberta species and explain its ecological role. Then argue why it helps reserve stability more than simply adding more producers.

(c) You plan to include decomposers (bacteria, fungi, invertebrates). A planning committee member says: "That's a waste of space — decomposers don't add anything useful." Write a formal response. Explain exactly what would happen to the reserve without decomposers. Use nutrient cycling and energy flow as your evidence.

Answer

(a) Only about 10% of the energy passes up each level, so each level needs about 10 times more energy below it. For example, 10,000 kJ of producers supports 1,000 kJ of herbivores, 100 kJ of small predators and only 10 kJ of top predators. There must be far more producers than top predators: the math forces a pyramid.

(b) Example: beaver. It builds dams that create ponds and wetlands, which provide habitat for fish, birds, amphibians and plants, store water, and raise biodiversity. Adding more producers only adds more of the same level. A keystone species creates habitat and connections that support many levels. (Accept wolf or grizzly with a reasoned role.)

(c) "Without decomposers, dead plants and animals would pile up and their nutrients would never return to the soil. Producers would run out of nitrogen and phosphorus, grow less, and capture less energy from the sun. Less energy would flow to every level above them, and the reserve would starve within a few years. Decomposers are essential to nutrient cycling and keep energy flowing."

S2 Stretch Alberta

Clusters 5 + 6 — Environmental change · Sustainability · Evaluate.

Alberta's boreal forest is warming at roughly twice the global average rate. Scientists predict that by 2080, the southern edge of the boreal forest will shift 200–300 km north. Areas that are boreal forest now will become parkland or grassland.

(a) Is this a natural disturbance or a human-caused one? Justify your answer — trace the cause from where it starts to its effect on Alberta's boreal forest.

(b) Woodland caribou can only survive in old-growth boreal forest. If the forest boundary shifts 200 km north over 80 years, what would caribou need to survive that shift? Think about both their biological needs and their habitat needs.

(c) Cree, Dene, and Métis communities of the boreal forest have watched changes for generations. They have seen changes in animal migration, plant timing, and water levels. A scientist says: "We need satellite data and peer-reviewed studies — Traditional Ecological Knowledge alone is not enough." Judge this position. What does TEK offer that satellite data cannot? What can satellite data offer that TEK cannot? How could both work together better?

Answer

(a) Mainly human-caused. Burning fossil fuels adds greenhouse gases → the atmosphere traps more heat → the climate warms (faster in the north) → hotter, drier conditions and more fire and insects → the boreal forest's southern edge cannot survive and turns into parkland or grassland.

(b) Caribou would need unbroken corridors of old-growth forest so they can move north. They also need the forest itself to move fast enough, but trees spread slowly, so old growth may not exist further north in time. They need lichen to eat (it takes decades to grow), low predation, and protection from new roads and clearing.

(c) Both are needed. TEK offers: long-term, local, year-round observation across generations, and changes that satellites miss (animal behaviour, plant timing, ice and water conditions). Satellite data offers: measurements over huge areas, repeatable numbers, and comparisons across regions. Together: knowledge holders and scientists can design studies jointly, use TEK to decide where and what to measure, and check satellite trends against what communities see on the ground.

S3 Stretch Alberta

Clusters 1 + 2 + 4 — Ecosystems · Energy flow · Biodiversity · Evaluate / Create.

Three changes happen at the same time to a southern Alberta wetland:

  1. A drought reduces water levels by 60% (abiotic change).
  2. Purple loosestrife invades and replaces 70% of native aquatic plants (biotic change).
  3. A nearby road is built, increasing road-salt runoff and raising salinity (abiotic change).

(a) For each of the three changes above, predict one effect on food web structure. Which organism is affected first, and through which pathway?

(b) These three stressors are happening at the same time. Explain why three moderate stressors at once can do more damage than one severe stressor of the same total size. Use the idea of ecosystem resilience in your explanation.

(c) You are an ecosystem manager. You have resources to deal with only one of the three stressors. Which one would you deal with first, and why? Use food web structure, the energy pyramid, and biodiversity ideas from this unit. Do not just use common sense.

Answer

(a)

  • Drought: less water reduces aquatic habitat. Aquatic plants and insects are hit first, then the fish, frogs and ducks that eat them.
  • Purple loosestrife: native plants are replaced. Plant-eaters and the insects that depend on native plants are hit first, then the birds and amphibians above them.
  • Road salt: higher salinity harms salt-sensitive organisms first (amphibian eggs, aquatic insects, some plants), then their predators.

(b) Each stressor weakens a different part of the ecosystem at the same time. The ecosystem loses its resilience, its ability to absorb a disturbance and recover, because the species and habitats that would normally help it bounce back are all under attack together. The stressors make each other worse; for example, less water concentrates the salt.

(c) Accept any well-argued choice. Example: remove purple loosestrife first. It attacks the producers, the base of the energy pyramid that every other level depends on, and it lowers biodiversity directly. The drought cannot be controlled, and salt runoff is easier to reduce later. Restoring native plants protects the base of the food web.
