Part 1: The Four Forces of Flight

Goal: Use the vocabulary of the four forces of flight to explain why a design choice affects glide performance.

Every glider or plane in flight is pushed and pulled by four forces at once:

📌 New Word: lift

Pushes the plane up. Created mostly by air moving over the wings.

📌 New Word: weight

Gravity pulling the plane down.

📌 New Word: thrust

What pushes the plane forward. For a glider, that's the launch — not an engine.

📌 New Word: drag

Air resistance slowing the plane down.

Flight isn't about getting rid of any of these forces. It's about balancing them, so lift and weight, and thrust and drag, work together instead of against each other.

Before designing anything, this unit starts with quick tests: a flat sheet of paper versus a folded-wing glider, and a nose-heavy plane versus a balanced one. A flat sheet has almost no shape to make lift efficiently, and a lot of drag. A folded wing changes how air moves over and under it.

  1. Name the four forces of flight and what each one does.
  2. Why does a flat, unfolded sheet of paper fly worse than a folded-wing glider, in terms of these forces?

Part 2: Weight, Balance & the Fold Pattern

Goal: Explain why weight distribution and wing/fold shape are functional choices, not decorative ones.

Common Mistake #1

"Bigger wings always mean better flight."

Why that's wrong: Bigger wings can create more lift, but they also add more drag and more weight. A wing too big for the rest of the design can make a plane harder to control, not better.

Common Mistake #2

"Weight is just good or bad."

Why that's wrong: A glider too light for its wing area can be unstable and get knocked around by air. One too nose-heavy dives. One too tail-heavy stalls. What matters is the balance between lift and weight — not making one number as big or small as possible.

Common Mistake #3

"A paper plane's fold pattern is just decoration — how you make it look like a plane."

Why that's wrong: Every fold changes the wing's shape, surface area, and weight distribution — which changes lift, drag, and stability. The fold pattern is the design, not decoration on top of it.

What's actually true: Every design choice — wing size, fold pattern, nose weight — is a trade-off between the four forces, not a simple "more is better" dial.

📌 New Word: stability

How steady a glider flies, instead of wobbling, diving, or stalling.

Part 4: Full Design Cycle, Driven by Test Data

Goal: Complete a full design cycle, including at least one redesign based on flight-test data. Present your design process to an audience.

This capstone runs the complete design cycle you've used all year: sketch multiple concepts, build, test-fly (logging glide distance/time/stability for each test), redesign at least once based on that data, and share the process with an audience — your research fact, your design concepts, what the flight tests showed, and what you changed.

A peer design review happens before your final test round: reviewers predict your design's biggest weakness (too nose-heavy, too much drag, unstable) — the same predict-then-compare pattern you used with bridges, now applied to flight.
