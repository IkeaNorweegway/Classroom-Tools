Section 1 — Types of Data and Graphs

Learning target: I can identify when to use a scatter plot versus other graph types, and explain what types of questions scatter plots can answer that other graphs cannot.

Vocabulary

TermDefinition in your own wordsOne example
Univariate dataData on one variable per subjectHeights of all students in a class
Bivariate dataTwo variables measured on the same subjectHeight AND weight of each student
Scatter plotGraph showing bivariate data as individual points on an x-y planeTemperature vs. visitors
VariableA measurable characteristic that can changeTemperature, test score
Data pointOne subject's measurements on both variables; a single dot on the scatter plot(25°C, 130 visitors)

Guided Notes

Univariate data: data about one variable. Describes one trait of each subject.

Examples: heights of students, scores on a test, monthly temperatures.

Best graph types for univariate data:

  • Bar graph: categories or groups
  • Line graph: trends over time
  • Histogram: continuous numerical data, grouped into intervals

Bivariate data: data about two variables measured on the same subject.

Examples: height AND shoe size, study time AND test score, temperature AND ice cream sales.

Best graph for bivariate data: scatter plot

Why scatter plots?

A scatter plot lets you see if there is a relationship/correlation between two variables — whether they tend to change together.

A line graph only works for one variable changing over time.
A scatter plot works when you want to know: does knowing one variable help you predict the other?

Reading scatter plot axes:

Each dot = one data point (one subject's measurements on both variables).

Conventions:

  • Independent variable on x axis
  • Dependent variable on y axis

Describing correlation — use precise language:

A good description includes: direction (positive/negative/none) + strength (strong/moderate/weak/none).

"The scatter plot shows a strong positive correlation between temperature and splash pad visitors."

Outlier on a scatter plot:

An outlier is a point that falls far away from the general pattern.

Outliers:

  • May indicate a measurement error
  • May indicate a genuinely unusual case
  • Should be noted but NOT automatically removed

How does an outlier affect the correlation description? It can make the correlation look weaker (or stronger) than the rest of the data shows; mention it, but describe the overall pattern.

Misconception

MISCONCEPTION: Correlation means causation

Wrong idea: "If two variables have a strong positive correlation, one must be causing the other."

Why it fails: Two things can change together because a third factor drives both, or because the relationship is coincidental. Ice cream sales and drowning rates both increase in summer — but ice cream does not cause drowning. Both are caused by the third variable: hot weather.

Before claiming cause, you must be able to explain a direct mechanism, rule out third variables (confounders), and ideally run a controlled experiment.

Strong correlation is necessary but not sufficient for causation.

Connection Prompt

You have seen linear relations graphed as lines. How is a scatter plot different from a line graph of a linear relation? What is the same about how you read a trend in both?

In both you look at the overall direction of change, not at single points. A line graph shows one variable over time; a scatter plot compares two variables.

Section 3 — Line of Best Fit

Learning target: I can draw a line of best fit by inspection, write its equation, and use it to make predictions.

Vocabulary

TermDefinition in your own wordsOne example
Line of best fitStraight line best representing the trend in a scatter ploty = 10t − 115 for splash pad data
By inspectionDrawing based on visual judgment — equal points above and belowHuman placement of trend line
Trend lineAnother name for line of best fitThe same line
Equation of the line of best fity = mx + b derived from two points ON the linev = 10t − 115

Guided Notes

The line of best fit (trend line) is a straight line drawn to best represent the trend/overall pattern of points in a scatter plot.

It is used when there is a positive or negative correlation (not for no correlation).

Rules for drawing a line of best fit by inspection:

  1. The line passes through the middle of the data (not necessarily through any specific point)
  2. Approximately equal numbers of points above and below the line
  3. The line should follow the general direction/trend of the data
  4. The line does NOT need to pass through the origin or any particular point

Worked Example — Drawing and finding the equation:

Using the temperature vs. visitors data from Section 1:

Step 1: Draw the line of best fit through the middle of the data.

Step 2: Choose two points ON the line (not necessarily data points):
Point 1: (20, 85)    Point 2: (34, 225)

Step 3: Calculate the slope:
m = y₂ − y₁/x₂ − x₁ = (225 − 85) / (34 − 20) = 140/14 ≈ 10

Step 4: Find the y-intercept using y = mx + b:
85 = 10(20) + b
b = −115

Step 5: Write the equation:
v = 10t + − 115   (v = visitors, t = temperature)

Explain Step 2: Why do we choose points ON the line rather than actual data points to find the equation?

Points on the line follow the trend exactly. A data point may sit above or below the line, so using it would give a different, less accurate equation.
Misconception

MISCONCEPTION: The line of best fit must pass through the most points

Wrong idea: "A good line of best fit connects as many dots as possible."

Why it fails: The line of best fit is a model of the trend, not a connect-the-dots. It minimizes the total distance from all points to the line — this means most points will NOT be exactly on it. A line that passes through many points but ignores the overall direction is a worse fit than a line that misses all points but captures the trend.

The goal: equal numbers of points above and below, following the general direction.

The line of best fit in other representations (Rule of Four):

FormWhat it shows
GraphThe visual trend line through scattered data
Equationy = mx + b, where m is the rate of change and b is the starting value
TablePoints that lie exactly on the line (not data points — predicted values)
Verbal"For each degree increase in temperature, approximately 10 more visitors are expected"

Complete the verbal description for your line: For each 1°C rise in temperature, about 10 more visitors come to the splash pad.

Retrieval Check — Section 3 — Close Your Notes
  1. List the four rules for drawing a line of best fit by inspection.
    Answer (1) Through the middle. (2) Equal points above and below. (3) Follows general direction. (4) No requirement to pass through origin or any data point.
  2. On a scatter plot, two students draw different lines of best fit. Student A passes through the origin; Student B's line has approximately equal numbers of points above and below. Whose line is more likely correct? Why?
    Answer Student B is more likely correct. Requiring the line to pass through the origin is an unwarranted constraint not justified by the data.
  3. From your line of best fit equation: if temperature = 29°C, how many visitors would you predict?
    Answer Using v≈10t−115: v=10(29)−115=175 visitors (varies by student's line)
  4. What does the slope of the line of best fit represent in the temperature/visitors context?
    Answer For each 1°C increase in temperature, approximately 10 more visitors are expected — it is the rate of change.
  5. (Stretch) A line of best fit has equation y = −3x + 95. What correlation does the scatter plot likely show? What does the slope mean in context?
    Answer Negative correlation (slope is negative). Slope means: for each unit increase in x, y decreases by 3.

Section 4 — Interpolation and Extrapolation

Learning target: I can use a line of best fit to interpolate and extrapolate values, and explain why extrapolation is less reliable.

Vocabulary

TermDefinition in your own wordsOne example
InterpolationPredicting y for an x-value WITHIN the observed data rangePredicting at 26°C when data spans 20–34°C
ExtrapolationPredicting y for an x-value OUTSIDE the observed data rangePredicting at 40°C when data only goes to 34°C
ReliabilityHow trustworthy/accurate a prediction is likely to beInterpolation is more reliable than extrapolation
Domain of the dataThe range of x-values actually observed20°C to 34°C

Guided Notes

Interpolation means predicting a y-value for an x-value inside/within the range of the data.

Extrapolation means predicting a y-value for an x-value outside the range of the data.

Example — using the temperature/visitors line:

Data range: temperatures 20°C to 34°C.

Predictionx-valueInside or outside data range?Interpolation or Extrapolation?Reliability
Visitors at 26°C26
Visitors at 22°C22
Visitors at 40°C40
Visitors at 18°C18

Worked Example — Interpolation:

Using the equation v = 10t + (your b-value), predict visitors at 26°C.

v = 10(26) + (−115) = 145 visitors

This is interpolation because 26 is inside the data range (20 to 34).

Worked Example — Extrapolation:

Predict visitors at 40°C.

v = 10(40) + (−115) = 285 visitors

This is extrapolation because 40 is outside the data range.

Why extrapolation is less reliable:

The trend seen within the data might not continue outside it. At very high temperatures (45°C), the real relationship might change — perhaps fewer people go out because it's dangerously hot, or the park closes. The line of best fit is a model of what was observed, not a law of nature.

Misconception

MISCONCEPTION: Extrapolation is as reliable as interpolation

Wrong idea: "The equation works for data we measured, so it works just as well beyond those boundaries."

Why it fails: The relationship may only hold within the observed range. Beyond the range, additional factors come into play that we did not observe and cannot model from our data. A linear model predicting visitors at −10°C (using our splash pad equation) would give a nonsensical negative number — the linear trend clearly does not extend to freezing temperatures.

Rule of thumb: interpolation is valid as long as the line of best fit fits the data well. Extrapolation should always be noted as "less certain" and the further from the data range, the less reliable.

Evaluating a prediction:

When you make a prediction, always state:

  1. Whether it is interpolation or extrapolation
  2. How confident you are and why
  3. Any conditions that might make the prediction unreliable

Alberta context — Canola yield:

A farmer collects data on fertilizer applied (kg/hectare) and canola yield (tonnes/hectare). The data ranges from 0–200 kg/hectare. The line of best fit is: yield = 0.015 × fertilizer + 1.2.

a) Predict yield for 150 kg/hectare: 0.015(150) + 1.2 = 3.45 t/ha    Is this interpolation or extrapolation? Interpolation (inside 0–200)

b) Predict yield for 400 kg/hectare: 0.015(400) + 1.2 = 7.2 t/ha    Is this interpolation or extrapolation? Extrapolation (outside 0–200)

c) Why might the extrapolation be unreliable? (Hint: think about what happens if you apply too much fertilizer.)

Too much fertilizer can harm the crop, so the yield levels off or drops. The line assumes the trend keeps rising, but the data never went past 200 kg/hectare.
