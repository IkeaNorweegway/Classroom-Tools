Section 1 — Perfect Squares and Principal Square Roots

Learning target: I can identify perfect squares, evaluate principal square roots, and explain what the principal square root means.

Vocabulary

TermDefinition in your own wordsOne example
SquareThe result of multiplying a number by itself72 = 49
Perfect squareAny number that is the square of a positive integer49, 100, 144
Square rootA number that, when squared, gives the radicand√49 = 7 (since 72 = 49)
Principal square rootThe positive square root (the radical symbol always means this)√25 = 5 (not ±5)
Radical symbol (√)The symbol indicating principal (positive) square root√36 = 6
RadicandThe number under the radical symbolIn √36, radicand = 36

Guided Notes

Squaring and taking the square root are inverse operations.

If 52 = 25, then √25 = 5

If 92 = 81, then √81 = 9

A perfect square is any number that is the square of a positive (or whole) integer.

The principal square root is the positive root.

Both 5 and −5, when squared, equal 25. But √25 = 5 only only, because the radical symbol √ means the positive root root.

Misconception

Wrong idea: "The square root of 9 is plus or minus 3, because both 32 and (−3)2 equal 9."

Why it fails: While it is true that both 3 and −3 are square roots of 9, the principal square root — written √9 — is defined as the non-negative root. The symbol √ gives exactly one answer. If a problem intends both roots, it writes ±√9.

Correct: √9 = 3 (positive only). If asked for all square roots of 9: ±3.

Which expression gives −3? Write it: −√9

Structure of a radical expression:

The radicand of √36 is 36. The principal square root of 36 is 6.

Can you take the square root of a negative number? No

Why: A negative radicand has no real square root because no real number squared gives a negative result.

Square roots of fractions:

√(a/b) = √a / √b (when a ≥ 0 and b > 0)

√(9/25) = √9/√25 = 3/5 = 3/5

Section 2 — Non-Perfect Squares and Irrational Numbers

Learning target: I can estimate the square root of a non-perfect square, locate it on a number line, and explain why it is irrational.

Vocabulary

TermDefinition in your own wordsOne example
Irrational numberA real number that cannot be written as a/b; decimal never terminates or repeats√2 = 1.41421..., π
Non-perfect squareA positive integer that is not the square of any integer2, 3, 5, 7, 50
EstimateAn approximate value (not exact)√47 ≈ 6.9
Real numberAny number on the number line — rational or irrational−3, 1/2, √5, π

Guided Notes

Most numbers are NOT perfect squares.

√3, √7, √10 are not integers. They are not fractions either. They are irrational numbers — they cannot be written as a/b where a and b are integers.

What does irrational mean?

  • The decimal expansion never terminates and never repeats
  • √2 = 1.41421356... (goes on forever with no repeating block)
  • It exists on the number line — it has a location — but cannot be expressed as a ratio
Misconception

Wrong idea: "√2 must equal some fraction like 139/98 or something — we just haven't found it yet."

Why it fails: It has been proven (since ancient Greece) that √2 cannot equal any fraction a/b. If you assume it does and simplify, you reach a contradiction. This is not a matter of precision — it is literally impossible to write √2 as a fraction, no matter how many decimal places you use.

Correct: √2 is irrational. 1.41421... is just the decimal approximation, not the exact value. The only exact form is √2.

Estimating square roots — the squeeze technique:

To estimate √47:

Step 1: Find the perfect squares on either side.
36 < 47 < 49 so √36 < √47 < √49
6 < √47 < 7

Step 2: Is 47 closer to 36 or 49? 49 (distance 2 vs. 11)

Step 3: Better estimate: √47 ≈ √47 ≈ 6.9

Section 3 — The Pythagorean Theorem

Learning target: I can apply the Pythagorean theorem to find unknown side lengths of right triangles, including cases where the answer is irrational.

Vocabulary

TermDefinition in your own wordsOne example
Right triangleTriangle with exactly one 90° angle3-4-5 triangle
HypotenuseSide opposite the right angle; always the longest sidec in a2 + b2 = c2
LegEither of the two shorter sides forming the right anglea and b in a2 + b2 = c2
Pythagorean theoremIn a right triangle: a2 + b2 = c232 + 42 = 52 ✓
Pythagorean tripleThree positive integers satisfying a2 + b2 = c2(3,4,5), (5,12,13)

Guided Notes

The Pythagorean theorem:

In any right triangle, the square of the hypotenuse (c) equals the sum of the squares of the two legs (a and b).

a2 + b2 = c2

Label the parts of this triangle:

The hypotenuse is always the side opposite the right angle and is always the longest side.

Worked Example 1 — Find the hypotenuse:

A right triangle has legs of 6 cm and 8 cm. Find the hypotenuse.

a = 6, b = 8, c = ?

a2 + b2 = c2

62 + 82 = c2

36 + 64 = c2

c2 = 100

c = √100 = 10 cm

Explain the final step: Why do we take the square root at the end? Why do we use the principal square root (positive only)?

c² = 100 tells us c squared, so we undo the square with a square root.A length cannot be negative, so we use only the positive root: c = 10 cm.

Worked Example 2 — Find a leg (irrational answer):

A right triangle has hypotenuse 10 m and one leg of 4 m. Find the other leg.

a = 4, c = 10, b = ?

a2 + b2 = c2

42 + b2 = 102

16 + b2 = 100

b2 = 100 − 16 = 84

b = √84 ≈ 9.2 m (to one decimal)

Explain Step 4: Why did we subtract 16 from both sides instead of taking the square root at that step?

We must get b² alone first. The square root of 16 + b² is not 4 + b, so we subtract 16, then take the root.
Misconception

Wrong idea: "I can use a2 + b2 = c2 on any triangle."

Why it fails: The theorem only holds for right triangles — those with exactly one 90° angle. For other triangles, a2 + b2 ≠ c2 in general. If you are not told a triangle has a right angle, you cannot use this theorem.

Test it: Does a triangle with sides 3, 4, 6 follow the theorem? Check: 32 + 42 = 25 and 62 = 36. Does it work? No (25 ≠ 36) Is this a right triangle? No

Section 4 — Square Roots of Rational Numbers

Learning target: I can find the square root of a positive rational number, and connect square roots to fractional exponents.

Vocabulary

TermDefinition in your own wordsOne example
Square root of a fraction√(a/b) = √a/√b (when a ≥ 0, b > 0)√(9/16) = 3/4
Square root of a decimalConvert to fraction or recognize decimal pattern√0.49 = 0.7

Guided Notes

Square roots of fractions:

√(a/b) = √a / √b

This works because: (√a/√b)2 = (√a)2/(√b)2 = a/b ✓

√(9/16) = √9/√16 = 3/4

Square roots of decimals — two approaches:

Method 1: Convert to fraction, then take root.
√0.49 = √(49/100) = √49/√100 = 7/10 = 0.7

Method 2: Think of a decimal that squares to 0.49.
0.7 × 0.7 = 0.49, so √0.49 = 0.7

Practise:

ExpressionMethodAnswer
√0.25Convert: √(25/100) = 5/100.5
√(4/9)√4 / √92/3
√1.44Convert: √(144/100) = 12/101.2
√(0.01)Convert: √(1/100) = 1/100.1

Connecting square roots to exponents:

Pattern from the power laws:

x(1/2) × x(1/2) = x(1/2 + 1/2) = x1 = x

But also: √x × √x = x

So x(1/2) = √x (the square root of x)

This means: √x = x(1/2)

Verify: 9(1/2) = 3 and √9 = 3. They match ✓

Misconception

Wrong idea: √(9 + 16) = √9 + √16 = 3 + 4 = 7

Why it fails: Evaluate the left side: √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5. That is not 7. The radical sign applies to the entire sum inside, not to each term separately. Square roots do not distribute over addition.

Verify: √(a + b) = √a + √b?
Let a = 16, b = 9: Left side = √25 = 5   Right side = √16 + √9 = 4 + 3 = 7. Equal? No

What DOES work: √(a × b) = √a × √b (roots distribute over multiplication, not addition).

Table — what distributes under a radical?

ExpressionDistributes?Example
√(a × b)Yes√(4 × 9) = √4 × √9 = 2 × 3 = 6 ✓ (√36 = 6)
√(a ÷ b)Yes√(36/4) = √36/√4 = 6 ÷ 2 = 3 ✓ (√9 = 3)
√(a + b)No√(9 + 16) ≠ 3 + 4
√(a − b)No√(25 − 9) ≠ 5 − 3
