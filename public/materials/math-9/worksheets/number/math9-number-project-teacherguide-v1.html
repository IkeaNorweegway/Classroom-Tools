Math 9 — Number Inquiry Project

Teacher Guide

Companion to the student brief (math9-number-project-v1) and the Project Journal (math9-number-project-journal-v1). Teaching reference, not a script.

How to use this document

Label What it contains
SHOW What students see: board, projector or handout.
SAY Key phrases and questions. Answers in [brackets].
CUE The student signal: ★ Write / ✎ Draw / ⊡ Think / ↩︎ Check
Red flag A misconception that will cost marks if it isn’t addressed.
Amber flag A place where projects stall, with a time target.
Green box Expected answers for the option.

Why this project is built the way it is. research/project-based-learning-research.md: projects raise achievement only when they are structured. That means the driving question comes early, short explicit lessons happen inside the project, there’s a critique-and-revise cycle, teams are 4–5 with roles, and the work goes to a real audience. Every period below has a mini-lesson or a protocol for that reason.

The strand thread. Each option needs a number nobody can measure directly: a stack as tall as the distance to the Moon, a diagonal you can’t lay a ruler along, a temperature swing nobody saw. Both mini-lessons teach one habit across all three options: estimate first, then make the calculation checkable.

Where it sits in the strand. Rational Numbers → Powers & Exponents → Square Roots. Run Periods 1–5 after Square Roots Section 3. Option 3 uses only Rational Numbers, so a class that picks only Option 3 can run it earlier.

Flag — Pacing Five periods is tight. The points that run over are Period 1 (measuring the ream, the lockers or the weather data takes a while) and Period 4 (revision that turns into starting over). If a team misses a milestone, they finish it at the start of the next period. Don’t let them skip ahead to the next one.

Prep and Materials

All options: printed brief (one per student), Project Journal (one per student), sticky notes in two colours, chart paper for the gallery walk, rubric copies, calculators.

Option 1 — Fold to the Moon: an unopened or full ream of copy paper per team (or one shared), rulers, scrap paper for folding.

Option 2 — Will It Fit?: tape measures, string, scissors. Arrange access to a locker or cupboard. Borrow a hockey stick and a broom (or use a metre stick plus something longer from the gym).

Option 3 — Chinook!: before Period 1, download one winter week of daily highs and lows for Calgary or Lethbridge. Go to climate.weather.gc.ca → Historical Data → search the station (for example “Calgary Int’l”) → Daily → pick a January or February week that includes a chinook. Print a data card with the dates and the station name. A week with one big warm-up day works best.

Launch — Strand Lesson 1 (10 min)

Beat 1 — Three questions, no answers yet

Show Three photos side by side: a sheet of paper and the Moon; a hockey stick leaning out of a locker; a Calgary street at −20 °C next to the same street melting. Under each, its driving question.

Say “Each of these numbers is impossible to measure directly. By the end of this strand you’ll be able to get them anyway.” “Guess: how many folds to the Moon?” (Take answers. Most guesses are in the thousands or millions. Don’t correct them.) “What would you need to know?” ([how thick one sheet is], [how long the locker is inside], [what the temperatures were].)

Cue ⊡ Think → vote by hand. ★ Write: each student adds one sticky note to a need-to-know wall under their question. Leave the wall up all strand.

Why this is in Lesson 1: it creates the “need to know.” Point back at the wall when Powers Section 1 (repeated doubling), Square Roots Section 3 (Pythagorean theorem) and Rational Numbers Section 2 (subtracting negatives) come up.

Period 1 — Teams, Roles, Plan

Beat 2 — Teams and roles (8 min)

Show The role table from the brief.

Say “Everyone does the math. Your role is what you’re responsible for checking.” “Checker: you put every calculation next to its estimate. If they don’t match, the team stops.”

Cue ★ Write: tick your option and role on the journal cover. ★ Write: the gut guesses and the need-to-know table. ↩︎ Check: teacher initials M1.

Team size: 4–5. Assign teams yourself. Option 2 is the most concrete entry point.

Flag — Pacing Option 1: measure the ream once, carefully, then fold. Don’t let teams measure one sheet with a ruler: it can’t be done, and that is the point. Option 2: inside measurements only, to the nearest 0.5 cm. Target: measuring done by 15 minutes before the bell.

Period 2 — Build the Model

Beat 3 — Mini-lesson A: estimate before you calculate (10 min)

Show Benchmark 1. 210 = 1024 ≈ 103. So 230 = (210)3 ≈ ?
Benchmark 2. √50 is between √49 and √64, so it’s between ? and ?
Benchmark 3. −3.8 − (−6.1) ≈ −4 + 6 = ?

Say “[230 ≈ (103)3 = 109, a billion.] The power-of-a-power rule turns a monster into a one-liner.” “[√50 is between 7 and 8, very close to 7, because 50 is just past 49. About 7.1.]” “[About 2.] Calculator: [2.3]. Close enough to trust.” “If your calculator says something nowhere near your estimate, one of them is wrong. Find out which.”

Cue ★ Write all three in the M2 mini-lesson box. ↩︎ Check the calculator answers against the estimates.

Beat 4 — Build the model (rest of period)

Show Journal page M2.

Say Option 1: “Your measured folds will be thicker than the rule says. Why? [air between the layers.] Use the rule, and explain the difference.” Option 2: “Two triangles. Which side of the first triangle becomes a side of the second? [the floor diagonal.]” Option 3: “On the number line, which low is the coldest? [the most negative one.]”

Cue ★ Write the measurements, the table or diagram, and the rule. ↩︎ Check: the Checker’s two-case test. ★ Write: each student fills the blue On your own box (marked for Criterion 3).

Flag — Misconception “30 = 0” / “20 layers means no paper.” Zero folds is the one sheet you started with: 1 layer. Use the pattern from Powers Section 2: 23 = 8, 22 = 4, 21 = 2, 20 = [1]. Each step divides by 2.

Flag — Misconception “√(30² + 38²) = 30 + 38.” That’s the √(a + b) = √a + √b error. Have the Checker test it: 30 + 38 = 68 cm, but the floor diagonal can’t be longer than the two sides put end to end, and a right triangle’s diagonal is always shorter. [√2344 ≈ 48.4]

Flag — Misconception “−15 to 4 is a change of 11.” Students subtract the sizes. Ask them to count on the number line: [15 steps up to 0, then 4 more = 19]. Then connect it to 4 − (−15) = 4 + 15.

Period 3 — Calculate and Check

Beat 5 — Mini-lesson B: make a calculation checkable (10 min)

Show Example 1. A stack of 200 coins is 35 cm tall. How thick is one coin? Write it as a decimal and in the form a × 10n.
Example 2. The mean of −4.5, −1.2, 3.0 and −6.3.

Say Example 1: “Estimate: [35 ÷ 200 is a bit less than 0.2]. [0.175 cm = 1.75 × 10−1 cm]. Write the unit at every step.” Example 2: “Add the negatives together first: [−4.5 − 1.2 − 6.3 = −12.0], then [−12.0 + 3.0 = −9.0], then [÷ 4 = −2.25]. Does a negative mean make sense when most values are negative? [Yes.]” “Someone else should be able to follow every line without asking you a question.”

Cue ★ Write both in the M3 mini-lesson box. ⊡ Think: which step in your option is easiest to get wrong?

Flag — Misconception “A negative exponent means a negative answer.” In Option 1, 232 ÷ 242 = 2−10 = 1/1024: a small positive number. Ask: “Can a stack of paper have negative thickness?”

Flag — Misconception Rounding too early (Option 2). Teams round the floor diagonal to 48 and then square it: 48² + 150² = 24 804 → 157.5 cm, not 157.6. Teach them to carry the exact value: the floor diagonal squared is just 30² + 38² = 2344, so the long diagonal is √(2344 + 150²).

Flag — Misconception Order of operations in the °F → °C formula. 5/9 × (−2 − 32): the brackets come first [−34], then × 5/9. Teams that do 5/9 × −2 − 32 get −33.1 °C. The Checker’s estimate catches it: −2 °F is cold, but it isn’t −33 °C.

Flag — Misconception Rounding without thinking about the situation. 41.x folds → 42 (round up: 41 folds doesn’t reach). A diagonal of 157.6 cm vs a 150 cm stick → fits, but the stick has thickness and the locker may have a shelf. Ask: “If you round the other way, what goes wrong in real life?”

Period 4 — Critique and Revise

Beat 6 — Gallery walk (25 min)

Show Each team’s M2–M3 work posted on chart paper. Focus question for round 1: “Pick one calculation. Is there an estimate next to it, and are they close?” Round 2: “Is the answer a believable size, and does it have a unit?”

Say “Kind, specific, helpful. ‘Looks good’ gets handed back.”

Cue Two rounds × 8 min, each team visiting a different poster. Yellow sticky = round 1, pink = round 2. ★ Write: one sticky per round, plus a copy in your journal.

Beat 7 — Revise (rest of period)

Say “Sort your stickies: what do we fix, what do we answer, what do we politely ignore, and why? Log each change.”

Cue ★ Write: at least two revision-log rows. ↩︎ Check: teacher initials M4.

Flag — Pacing If a team wants to start over, don’t let them. Revision means fixing the weakest part. By 15 minutes before the bell, every team should know what goes in its final product.

Period 5 — Show It

Option Format Audience Time
1 “Fold to the Moon” explainer (one page) or a 60-second video script. Read the best one aloud, or show it to another class. Another class ~20 min
2 “Will it fit?” hallway poster. Do the string test live in front of the class. Hallway / class ~20 min
3 60-second “wild weather” segment on the morning announcements, or a one-page infographic. School ~20 min

Last 10–15 min: the individual reflection on journal page M5. This is not homework. It’s done in class, and it feeds Criterion 3.

Answer Guides

Option 1 — Fold to the Moon

One sheet. A 500-sheet ream of copy paper is usually about 5 cm thick → 50 mm ÷ 500 = 0.1 mm = 1 × 10−1 mm. Measured values of 0.09–0.11 mm are fine.

Model. Folds n → layers 2n → thickness 0.1 × 2n mm: 0 → 1 → 0.1; 1 → 2 → 0.2; 2 → 4 → 0.4; 3 → 8 → 0.8; 4 → 16 → 1.6; 5 → 32 → 3.2 mm. Measured stacks come out thicker because of the air between layers. 20 = 1: zero folds is the one sheet you started with.

Moon. 384 400 km = 3.844 × 105 km = 3.844 × 105 × 106 mm = 3.844 × 1011 mm (product of powers). Layers needed: 3.844 × 1011 ÷ 10−1 = 3.844 × 1012 (quotient of powers: 11 − (−1) = 12). Estimate: 240 = (210)4 ≈ (103)4 = 1012, a bit short. 241 ≈ 2.2 × 1012 (still short), 242 ≈ 4.4 × 1012 ✓ → 42 folds.

32 folds. 232 ÷ 242 = 2−10 = 1/1024 as thick as the 42-fold stack. Thickness: 232 × 0.1 mm ≈ 4.29 × 108 mm ≈ 430 km, about the height of the Space Station’s orbit.

★★ Everest (8849 m): 8.849 × 106 mm ÷ 0.1 = 8.849 × 107 layers. 226 ≈ 6.7 × 107 (short), 227 ≈ 1.3 × 108 ✓ → 27 folds. Shortcut: Moon ÷ Everest ≈ 3.844 × 1011 ÷ 8.849 × 106 ≈ 43 000, between 215 and 216, so about 15–16 fewer folds than 42 → 26–27; check → 27.

Option 2 — Will It Fit? (sample locker 30 cm wide × 38 cm deep × 150 cm tall inside; use yours)

Floor diagonal: √(30² + 38²) = √2344. 48² = 2304 and 49² = 2401, so it’s between 48 and 49, closer to 48. Calculator: 48.4 cm.

Long diagonal: √(2344 + 150²) = √24 844. 157² = 24 649 and 158² = 24 964, so it’s between 157 and 158. Calculator: 157.6 cm.

Rational or irrational? 24 844 isn’t a perfect square, so √24 844 is irrational: its decimal never ends and never repeats. The 157.6 is a rounded approximation.

Fit: an adult hockey stick (~150 cm) fits on the diagonal, just. The blade, the stick’s thickness, a shelf or a coat hook can each stop it. A strong poster says “it depends” and names the reason. The string test settles it.

Why corner to corner: it’s the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose legs are already the longest straight lengths in the floor (the floor diagonal) and the height, so it’s longer than either of them.

★★ TV: (16k)² + (9k)² = 65² → 337k² = 4225 → k = 65 ÷ √337 ≈ 3.541. Width ≈ 56.7 in, height ≈ 31.9 in.

Option 3 — Chinook!

Pincher Creek (January 1962): 22 − (−19) = 22 + 19 = 41 °C. Rate: 41 °C per hour over 1 hour; over 4 hours 41 ÷ 4 = 10 1/4 °C per hour.

°F → °C: −2 °F: 5/9 × (−2 − 32) = 5/9 × (−34) = −170/9 = −18 8/9 ≈ −18.9 °C. 72 °F: 5/9 × (72 − 32) = 5/9 × 40 = 200/9 = 22 2/9 ≈ 22.2 °C. Change: 200/9 − (−170/9) = 370/9 = 41 1/9 °C (or 74 °F × 5/9). It matches the 41 °C record, which was rounded from these readings.

Your week: depends on your data. Made-up sample, to show the method only: highs −12.5, −8.0, 6.5, 9.0, −3.5, −15.0, −10.5; lows −22.0, −18.5, −9.0, −1.5, −14.0, −26.5, −21.0. Swings: 9.5, 10.5, 15.5, 10.5, 10.5, 11.5, 10.5. Mean high −34.0 ÷ 7 ≈ −4.9 °C. Mean low −112.5 ÷ 7 ≈ −16.1 °C. Biggest swing: day 3 (15.5 °C), well under the record’s 41 °C. A real chinook day will often show a swing over 20 °C in the hourly data.

Why −15 to 4 is 19, not 11: subtracting −15 means moving 15 up to zero, then 4 more. 4 − (−15) = 4 + 15.

★★ Extended: set °C = °F: x = 9/5 x + 32 → −4/5 x = 32 → x = −40. −40 °C = −40 °F.

Assessment

  • Rubric (in the brief): 4 criteria × 3 = 12. Criteria 1, 2 and 4 are marked from the Recorder’s journal and the final product, and every team member gets the same mark. Criterion 3 is marked from each student’s blue On your own box (M2) and reflection question 2 (M5). That’s the individual mark.
  • Outcome codes are local, one per notes-package section: RN1–RN5 (Rational Numbers S1–S5, as on the Rational Numbers test), PE1–PE7 (Powers & Exponents S1–S7), SR1–SR4 (Square Roots S1–S4).
  • The project doesn’t replace the strand test. The test checks individual knowledge. The project checks application and explanation.
  • Returning work: hand it back with a comment on each criterion. Students write one paragraph in class (10 min, not homework): (1) What did I misunderstand or leave incomplete? (2) What would I change if I could redo it?

Supports and Extension

  • Most concrete entry point: Option 2. Students who struggle with negatives or exponents can still do strong work there.
  • Scaffolds already in the journal: the measurement table with two tries, the Checker’s two-case test, the “Estimate first” box before every calculation.
  • IPP / significant gaps: give a powers-of-2 table (Option 1), a perfect-squares list to 400 (Option 2), or a vertical number line (Option 3). Keep the explanation in Criterion 3; that’s what’s being assessed. (See research/significant-learning-gaps-research.md.)
  • Extension: each option has a ★★ task.
