Math 9 — Measurement & Geometry Inquiry Project

Teacher Guide

Companion to the student brief (math9-measurement-geometry-project-v1) and the Project Journal (math9-measurement-geometry-project-journal-v1). Teaching reference, not a script.

Why this project is built the way it is. research/project-based-learning-research.md: projects raise achievement only when they are structured. That means the driving question comes early, short explicit lessons happen inside the project, there’s a critique-and-revise cycle, teams are 4–5 with roles, and the work goes to a real audience. Every period below has a mini-lesson or a protocol for that reason.

The strand thread. Each option measures something students can’t reach or that doesn’t exist yet. Both mini-lessons teach one habit across all three options: draw and label it first, then pick the formula, then solve for the missing measurement.

Where it sits in the strand. Circle Geometry → Surface Area & Volume → Similarity & Scale. Run Periods 1–5 after Similarity Section 4. A class that picks only Options 2 and 3 can start after Measurement Section 5.

Flag — Pacing Option 1 needs sun for the shadow method. Check the forecast and swap Periods 1 and 2 if you need to: the mirror method works indoors (a gym wall or the ceiling height). Also watch Period 4, where revision turns into starting over. If a team misses a milestone, they finish it at the start of the next period.

Prep and Materials

All options: printed brief (one per student), Project Journal (one per student), sticky notes in two colours, chart paper for the gallery walk, rubric copies, calculators, rulers, protractors.

Option 1 — Measure the Unmeasurable: tape measures, small flat mirrors (a makeup mirror works), chalk. Pick the object and find its real height (from the caretaker or the building plans) before the project. Keep it secret until the reports are in.

Option 2 — Package Design: a real pop can and a juice box per team if possible, rulers, string (for measuring around a can).

Option 3 — Circle Detectives: cut a “plate fragment” for each team: an arc of a circle about a third of the way round, from card stock. Use a radius of 8–12 cm, and write each radius on your key. Round objects (clocks, lids, a round table), set squares or index cards.

Launch — Strand Lesson 1 (10 min)

Beat 1 — Three questions, no answers yet

Show Three photos side by side: the school flagpole; a row of drink containers of different shapes; the view from the Calgary Tower. Under each, its driving question.

Say “Nobody’s climbing the flagpole, building a factory or flying to the horizon. By the end of this strand you’ll get all three numbers anyway.” “What would you need to know?” ([something we can measure that’s the same shape], [how much the container holds], [how big the Earth is].)

Cue ⊡ Think → vote by hand. ★ Write: each student adds one sticky note to a need-to-know wall under their question.

Why this is in Lesson 1: it creates the “need to know.” Point back at the wall during Circle Geometry Section 4 (tangent–radius), Measurement Section 2 (surface area) and Similarity Section 4 (indirect measurement).

Period 1 — Teams, Roles, Plan

Beat 2 — Teams and roles (8 min)

Show The role table from the brief.

Say “Everyone does the math. Your role is what you’re responsible for checking.” “Checker: every length in your formula has to point to a label on your diagram.”

Cue ★ Write: tick your option and role, the gut guesses and the need-to-know table. ↩︎ Check: teacher initials M1.

Flag — Pacing Option 1 shadow method: both shadows must be measured within a minute or two of each other, because shadows grow fast. Assign two measurers to work at the same time. Target: outdoor measuring done in 25 minutes.

Period 2 — Build the Model

Beat 3 — Mini-lesson A: draw it first, then choose the formula (10 min)

Show Example 1. A grain bin near Lethbridge is a cylinder 6 m across and 5 m tall. How much grain does it hold?
Example 2. A 10 cm × 15 cm photo is enlarged so the short side is 25 cm. How long is the long side?

Say Example 1: “Draw it. Label it. 6 m across is the [diameter], so r = [3 m]. Holds = [volume] → V = πr²h = π(3²)(5) ≈ [141 m³].” Example 2: “Same shape, different size: [similar]. Match short to short and long to long: 10/15 = 25/x → x = [37.5 cm].” “Every number in the formula came from a label on the drawing. That’s the check.”

Cue ✎ Draw and label both. ★ Write the formula and solve. ↩︎ Check: point to where each number came from.

Flag — Misconception Diameter used as radius. Option 2 and Option 3 both trip on this. Ask: “Is that measurement all the way across, or from the centre?” A can “6.6 cm across” has r = 3.3 cm.

Flag — Misconception “Volume and surface area measure the same thing.” Option 2 holds the volume fixed at 500 cm³ and changes the surface area. Ask: “Which one is the juice? [volume] Which one is the cardboard or aluminum? [surface area]” Make teams write the units: cm³ vs cm².

Beat 4 — Build the model (rest of period)

Say Option 1: “Where does each equal angle come from? [Both objects stand at 90° to the ground; the sun’s rays hit both at the same angle (shadow); the mirror reflects at equal angles (mirror).]” Option 2: “If the volume stays 500, and the radius gets bigger, what happens to the height? [it shrinks]” Option 3: “Where do the two bisectors cross? Why there? [The centre. Every point on a chord’s perpendicular bisector is the same distance from both ends of the chord, so the centre has to be on it.]”

Cue ✎ Draw the labelled diagram. ★ Write the formula or proportion. ↩︎ Check: the Checker’s units and believable-size test. ★ Write: the blue On your own box (Criterion 3).

Flag — Misconception Mismatched sides in a proportion (Option 1). Teams put the person’s height over the object’s shadow. Have them write the two triangles side by side, with the same order of letters, and check that each ratio compares “person” to “object” (or “height” to “shadow”) in the same way. “Similar means identical” shows up here too: the triangles have the same angles but different sizes.

Flag — Misconception “All inscribed angles are 90°” / “a chord is a diameter” (Option 3). The set-square trick works only because the 90° corner sits on the circle, so the chord it cuts off is a diameter (inscribed angle in a semicircle). Ask: “Would a 60° corner give a diameter? [No]”

Period 3 — Solve and Check

Beat 5 — Mini-lesson B: solve for the missing measurement (10 min)

Show Example 1. A cylinder holds 1000 cm³ and has a radius of 5 cm. How tall is it?
Example 2. x / 4.5 = 3.2 / 1.8
Example 3. Paint covers 9.5 m² per can. A wall is 41 m². How many cans?

Say Example 1: “1000 = π(5²)h → h = 1000 ÷ (25π) ≈ [12.7 cm]. Estimate first: 25π is about 80, and 1000 ÷ 80 is about [12.5].” Example 2: “Multiply both sides by 4.5: x = 4.5 × 3.2 ÷ 1.8 = [8.0].” Example 3: “41 ÷ 9.5 ≈ [4.3]. You can’t buy 4.3 cans, so [5]. Round up when you’re buying. Round to the precision you can measure when you’re reporting a length.”

Cue ★ Write all three in the M3 mini-lesson box. ⊡ Think: which way should your answer round?

Flag — Misconception Mixed units in the horizon triangle (Option 3). Teams add 6371 km + 158 m = 6529. Make them convert first: 158 m = [0.158 km]. The Checker’s believable-size test catches it: a horizon over 1000 km away is not believable.

Flag — Misconception “A cone has half the volume of the matching cylinder” (Option 2 ★★). It’s one-third. Pour test if you have a cone and a cylinder: it takes [3] cones to fill the cylinder.

Period 4 — Critique and Revise

Beat 6 — Gallery walk (25 min)

Show Each team’s M2–M3 work posted on chart paper. Focus question for round 1: “Pick one number in their formula. Can you find it on their diagram?” Round 2: “Is the answer a believable size, with a unit?”

Say “Kind, specific, helpful. ‘Looks good’ gets handed back.”

Cue Two rounds × 8 min. Yellow sticky = round 1, pink = round 2. ★ Write: one sticky per round, plus a copy in your journal.

Beat 7 — Revise (rest of period)

Say “Sort your stickies: what do we fix, what do we answer, what do we politely ignore, and why? Log each change.”

Cue ★ Write: at least two revision-log rows. ↩︎ Check: teacher initials M4.

Period 5 — Show It

Option Format Audience Time
1 Height report (one page) handed to the caretaker or principal. Then reveal the real height, and each team says how close they came. Caretaker / principal ~20 min
2 2-minute pitch to the “juice company,” with the labelled drawing and the material saved. Admin, another teacher, or the class as a panel ~25 min
3 Case file presented to another class: each case, the property that cracked it, the answer. Another class ~25 min

Last 10–15 min: the individual reflection on journal page M5. This is not homework. It’s done in class, and it feeds Criterion 3.

Answer Guides

Option 1 — Measure the Unmeasurable (sample measurements; use yours)

Shadow method. Student 1.60 m tall, shadow 2.00 m; flagpole shadow 11.25 m, measured at the same time. h / 11.25 = 1.60 / 2.00 → h = 11.25 × 0.8 = 9.0 m. Similar by AA: both make a 90° angle with the ground, and the sun’s rays are parallel, so the angle at the tip of each shadow is equal.

Mirror method. Eye height 1.50 m; student to mirror 1.20 m; mirror to flagpole base 7.20 m. h / 7.20 = 1.50 / 1.20 → h = 7.20 × 1.25 = 9.0 m. Similar by AA: both have a right angle at the ground, and light reflects off the mirror at equal angles.

Scale diagram at 1 cm : 1 m: a base of 11.25 cm and a height of 9 cm. The measured height should read 9 cm.

Comparing methods: real results usually differ by 0.3–1 m. Shadow errors come from shadows measured at different times, sloped ground, or a fuzzy shadow tip. Mirror errors come from where exactly the eye sees the top, and from eye height. A strong answer names a specific error and says which method it affects.

Option 2 — Package Design (500 cm³)

Cylinder (h = 500 ÷ πr², SA = 2πr² + 2πrh):

r (cm) h (cm) SA (cm²)
3 17.68 389.9
4 9.95 350.5
5 6.37 357.1
6 4.42 392.9
4.2 9.02 348.9
4.3 8.61 348.7
4.4 8.22 348.9

Best ≈ r = 4.3 cm, h = 8.6 cm: the height equals the diameter. SA ≈ 348.7 cm².

Square prism (h = 500 ÷ s², SA = 2s² + 4sh): s = 5 → 20.00 cm, 450.0; 6 → 13.89, 405.3; 7 → 10.20, 383.7; 8 → 7.81, 378.0; 9 → 6.17, 384.2. The best is close to a cube (s ≈ 7.9 cm, SA ≈ 378 cm²). The cylinder wins by about 29 cm² (about 8%).

Real can (r = 3.3, h = 12.2): V = π(3.3²)(12.2) ≈ 417 cm³. That’s more than 355 mL because of the headspace and the tapered ends. SA ≈ 321 cm². Per cm³ held: 321 ÷ 417 ≈ 0.77 cm² for the can vs 348.7 ÷ 500 ≈ 0.70 cm² for the best cylinder, so the real can uses about 10% more material per mL. Why: easier to hold, fits cup holders and vending machines, and the ends are thicker metal than the sides (so a narrower can saves the expensive part).

Too tall and skinny → a long side wraps a small volume. Too short and wide → two huge circles for the top and bottom.

★★ Cone cup: h = 3 × 100 ÷ (π × 4²) ≈ 5.97 cm; cylinder h = 100 ÷ (π × 4²) ≈ 1.99 cm. The cone needs 3 times the height because its volume is 1/3 of the matching cylinder’s.

Option 3 — Circle Detectives

Case 1 (sample fragment, radius 10 cm): a 16 cm chord whose midpoint is 6 cm from the centre: r = √(8² + 6²) = √100 = 10 cm. Use half the chord. The measured radius from the bisectors should be within a few millimetres of the calculated one. Check each team’s fragment against your key. Why the bisectors meet at the centre: the centre is the same distance from both ends of any chord, and the points that are the same distance from both ends of a chord all lie on its perpendicular bisector. Two bisectors → one point where they cross.

Case 2: a 90° corner on the circle cuts off a diameter (an inscribed angle that subtends a diameter is 90°). Two diameters cross at the centre.

Case 3 — Calgary Tower horizon: the line of sight is tangent to the Earth, and a tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point where it touches, so there’s a right angle there. d² + 6371² = (6371 + 0.158)² → d = √(6371.158² − 6371²) = √2013.3 ≈ 44.9 km, about 45 km.

★★ Mountain peak 1.855 km above Calgary: √(6372.855² − 6371²) ≈ 153.8 km. Total: 44.9 + 153.8 ≈ 199 km. That’s why peaks 100–150 km away are visible from downtown on a clear day.

Assessment

  • Rubric (in the brief): 4 criteria × 3 = 12. Criteria 1, 2 and 4 are marked from the Recorder’s journal and the final product, and every team member gets the same mark. Criterion 3 is marked from each student’s blue On your own box (M2) and reflection question 2 (M5).
  • Outcome codes are local, one per notes-package section: CG1–CG4 (Circle Geometry S1–S4), ME1–ME5 (Measurement S1–S5), SI1–SI4 (Similarity S1–S4), plus SR3 (Pythagorean theorem) and RN4–RN5.
  • The project doesn’t replace the strand test. The test checks individual knowledge. The project checks application and explanation.
  • Returning work: hand it back with a comment on each criterion. Students write one paragraph in class (10 min, not homework): (1) What did I misunderstand or leave incomplete? (2) What would I change if I could redo it?

Supports and Extension

  • Most concrete entry point: Option 1. Students hold the tape measure and see both triangles.
  • Scaffolds already in the journal: two-try measurement table, “labelled diagram” box before the formula box, the Checker’s units and believable-size test.
  • IPP / significant gaps: give a pre-drawn triangle pair to label (Option 1), a table with r already filled in (Option 2), or a pre-folded fragment (Option 3). Keep the explanation in Criterion 3; that’s what’s being assessed.
  • Extension: each option has a ★★ task.

Sources
