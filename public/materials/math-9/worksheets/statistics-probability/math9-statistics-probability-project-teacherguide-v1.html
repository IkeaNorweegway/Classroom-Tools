Why this project is built the way it is. research/project-based-learning-research.md: projects raise achievement only when they are structured. That means the driving question comes early, short explicit lessons happen inside the project, there’s a critique-and-revise cycle, teams are 4–5 with roles, and the work goes to a real audience. Every period below has a mini-lesson or a protocol for that reason.

The strand thread. Each option tests a claim people repeat without checking. Both mini-lessons teach one habit across all three options: every claim needs evidence and its limits. For Criterion 3, a student who can say where the model breaks down understands it better than one who can only use it.

Prep and Materials

All options: printed brief (one per student), Project Journal (one per student), sticky notes in two colours, chart paper for the gallery walk, rubric copies, grid paper.

Option 1 — Does It Really Correlate?: tape measures (2–3), metre sticks for the ruler drop, a shared class data sheet (one row per student, no names, just a number).

Option 2 — Score More, Win More?: print the NHL standings for all 32 teams (GF, GA, PTS) from NHL.com, with the date. Use last season’s final standings unless you want the extra discussion that mid-season data brings (see the flag in Period 3).

Option 3 — Carnival Game Designer: dice, coins, spinners (or paper-clip spinners), coloured cubes or counters, paper bags. Book a younger class for the booth on Period 5.

Launch — Strand Lesson 1 (10 min)

Beat 1 — Three claims, no answers yet

Show Three images: Leonardo da Vinci’s Vitruvian Man (arms out, in a square); an Oilers goal celebration; a carnival ring-toss booth. Under each, its driving question.

Say “Each of these is a claim people repeat without checking: arm span equals height, more goals means more wins, the carnival game is winnable. By the end of this strand you’ll be able to test them.” “What would you need to know?” ([measurements from lots of people], [every team’s stats], [how many ways there are to win].)

Cue ⊡ Think → vote by hand. ★ Write: each student adds one sticky note to a need-to-know wall under their question.

Why this is in Lesson 1: it creates the “need to know.” Point back at the wall during Statistics Section 3 (line of best fit) and Probability Section 4 (dependent events).

Period 1 — Teams, Roles, Plan

Beat 2 — Teams and roles (8 min)

Show The role table from the brief.

Say “Everyone does the math. Your role is what you’re responsible for checking.” “Data person: ‘fair’ means everyone is measured the same way. Shoes on or off? Arms straight? Decide before you start.”

Cue ★ Write: tick your option and role, the gut guesses and the need-to-know table. ↩︎ Check: teacher initials M1.

Period 2 — Build the Model

Beat 3 — Mini-lesson A: from data to a display (10 min)

Show Example 1. (Made-up data) Hours of practice → free throws made out of 20: (0, 6), (1, 8), (2, 11), (3, 12), (4, 15), (5, 16).
Example 2. Flip a coin, then spin a 3-colour spinner (red, blue, green). List every outcome.

Say Example 1: “Which variable goes on the x-axis? [practice: it’s the one we think explains the other.] Scale: x goes 0–5, y goes 0–20. Even steps. Title. Units.” “Line of best fit: about as many points above as below. It does not have to go through the first point or the origin. Pick two points on your line, not data points, to find the slope: for example (0, 6.5) and (5, 16.5) → m = [2] free throws per hour.” Example 2: “Tree: 2 branches, then 3 on each → [6 outcomes], each with probability [1/6]. The branches from one point add to [1].”

Cue ✎ Draw both in the M2 mini-lesson box. ↩︎ Check: points above vs below the line; branches add to 1.

Flag — Misconception “The line of best fit connects the dots” / “it must go through the first and last points.” Ask: “Is your line a trend or a path?” A good line has about half the points on each side, spread along its length.

Flag — Misconception “Removing a cube doesn’t change the probability” (Option 3). Without replacement, the bag changes: 3 red out of 5 becomes 2 red out of 4 after a red is drawn. Have students physically draw a cube, leave it out, and count what’s left before they write the second-step fractions.

Period 3 — Predict and Test

Beat 4 — Mini-lesson B: claim, evidence, limits (10 min)

Show Example 1. In summer, ice cream sales and drownings both go up. Claim: “Ice cream causes drowning.”
Example 2. From Example 1 in Period 2, the line is y = 2x + 6.5. Predict for 3.5 hours, then for 40 hours.
Example 3. A game has a theoretical chance of 1/4. A team played 20 times and won 7. Is the game broken?

Say Example 1: “Evidence: [they rise together]. Limit: [hot weather drives both]. Correlation doesn’t show causation.” Example 2: “3.5 h → [13.5 free throws]: inside the data, so we can trust it. 40 h → [86.5 out of 20]: impossible. That’s extrapolation going wrong.” Example 3: “Expected [5]. Got 7. With only 20 trials, that’s normal. Theoretical probability tells you the long-run result, not the next 20 games.”

Cue ★ Write: claim / evidence / limit for each one in the M3 mini-lesson box.

Flag — Misconception “Correlation means causation.” Every Option 1 and Option 2 team must answer: “Could something else explain this pattern?” Option 1: height doesn’t cause arm span; both come from overall body growth. Option 2: goals do help win games directly, but goals against matter too, so GF alone isn’t the whole story.

Flag — Misconception “A strong correlation is an exact relationship.” The line predicts a typical value. Ask: “Are any of your real points exactly on the line? [Few or none.] So how exact is your prediction?”

Flag — Misconception Gambler’s fallacy: “I lost 5 times, so I’m due.” Each new game starts fresh: the dice and the spinner have no memory, and the bag is refilled. In the dependent version, the two draws within one game are linked, but one game doesn’t affect the next. That’s a subtle point worth a full-class discussion.

Flag — Misconception Mid-season NHL data (Option 2). If you use current standings, teams have played different numbers of games, so points and goals aren’t comparable. Either use last season’s final standings, or have teams divide by games played. This is a strong ★★ discussion.

Period 4 — Critique and Revise

Beat 5 — Gallery walk (25 min)

Show Each team’s display and model posted on chart paper. Round 1: “Check one prediction: put the x-value into their equation (or follow their tree). Do you get their answer?” Round 2: “What is their limit? Is it a real one?” Option 3 teams leave their game at their poster. Visitors play it 5 times and check the tree diagram.

Say “Kind, specific, helpful. ‘Looks good’ gets handed back.”

Cue Two rounds × 8 min. Yellow sticky = round 1, pink = round 2. ★ Write: one sticky per round, plus a copy in your journal.

Beat 6 — Revise (rest of period)

Say “Sort your stickies: what do we fix, what do we answer, what do we politely ignore, and why? Log each change.”

Cue ★ Write: at least two revision-log rows. ↩︎ Check: teacher initials M4.

Period 5 — Show It

Option Format Audience Time
1 “Myth busted or confirmed?” poster for the hallway, with the scatter plot and a warning about the line’s limits. Hallway / another class ~20 min
2 60-second sports segment on the morning announcements, or a letter to a fan who says “goals are all that matter.” School ~20 min
3 Run the booth for a younger class, with an honest odds sign. Keep a tally, and compare it with the theoretical probability at the end. A younger class ~30 min

Last 10–15 min: the individual reflection on journal page M5. This is not homework. It’s done in class, and it feeds Criterion 3.

Answer Guides

Option 1 — Does It Really Correlate?

(a) Arm span vs height: expect a strong positive correlation. The line is close to span = height: slope about 1 cm of arm span per cm of height, intercept near 0. Don’t over-interpret the intercept, because nobody is 0 cm tall. Class heights will mostly fall between 150 and 185 cm. Interpolation (someone 170 cm tall) is trustworthy. Extrapolation: a 2.20 m player is outside the data. The line predicts about 220 cm, and many elite basketball players actually have spans longer than their height, so the prediction is only rough. A 75 cm toddler: young children’s body proportions differ from teenagers’, so a line built on Grade 9 students doesn’t apply. That’s the best example of an extrapolation limit. Causation: height doesn’t cause arm span. Both come from the same overall growth.

(b) Foot length vs height: usually a moderate-to-strong positive correlation, more scattered than (a).

(c) Sleep vs reaction time: usually weak or no correlation in a class sample. That’s a correct and interesting finding. Limits: one night’s sleep is self-reported, reaction time varies trial to trial, and practice, focus and caffeine all matter. A team that reports “no correlation” with good evidence earns full marks.

Slope from two points on the line: check that the team didn’t pick two data points that are off the line.

Option 2 — Score More, Win More? (check against your printed standings)

GF vs PTS: expect a positive correlation, usually moderate to strong, with visible scatter. The slope means points per extra goal scored, over a season. The y-intercept (points for a team that scores 0 goals) is meaningless: it’s an extrapolation far outside the data.

Oilers and Flames: actual − predicted is positive (above the line: doing better than their goals predict) or negative (below). Good reasons that aren’t goals scored: goals against (goaltending and defence), overtime and shootout points, many close games, and games played (mid-season).

GA vs PTS: expect a negative correlation. Teams that allow more goals earn fewer points.

★★ Goal difference (GF − GA) vs PTS: usually the strongest correlation of the three, with points clustered more tightly around the line, because it counts both ends of the ice. Some goal differences are negative, so the x-axis has to include negative numbers.

Option 3 — Carnival Game Designer (sample games)

Bag game: 3 red and 2 blue cubes; draw two; win if both are red. - Independent (with replacement): 3/5 × 3/5 = 9/25 = 0.36. Too high, so it’s outside 1/5–1/3. The team must adjust it (for example 2 red and 3 blue → 2/5 × 2/5 = 4/25 = 0.16, now too low; or 3 red and 3 blue → 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 ✓). - Dependent (without replacement): 3/5 × 2/4 = 6/20 = 3/10 = 0.30 ✓.

Spinner + die: a spinner that’s half gold, and a die roll of 4 or more: 1/2 × 3/6 = 1/4 = 0.25 ✓ (independent).

30 trials at p = 0.30: expect about 9 wins. Anything from about 4 to 14 wins is normal at 30 trials. Pooled results (60, 90, 120 trials) should drift closer to 0.30. That’s the long-run meaning of theoretical probability.

“I’m due to win”: false. Every game starts fresh, so past losses don’t change the next game’s probability.

★★ Exactly 1/4: many answers. For example, change the bag to 3 red and 3 blue with replacement (1/2 × 1/2), or 2 red and 2 blue without replacement → 2/4 × 1/3 = 2/12 = 1/6 ✗ (doesn’t work: check it!). The tree diagram must prove whichever they choose.

Assessment

  • Rubric (in the brief): 4 criteria × 3 = 12. Criteria 1, 2 and 4 are marked from the Recorder’s journal and the final product, and every team member gets the same mark. Criterion 3 (meaning and limits) is marked from each student’s blue On your own box (M2) and reflection question 2 (M5).
  • Outcome codes are local, one per notes-package section: ST1–ST4 (Statistics S1–S4), PR1–PR4 (Probability S1–S4), plus LR3 (y = mx + b) and RN2–RN3.
  • The project doesn’t replace the strand test. The test checks individual knowledge. The project checks application and explanation.
  • Returning work: hand it back with a comment on each criterion. Students write one paragraph in class (10 min, not homework): (1) What did I misunderstand or leave incomplete? (2) What would I change if I could redo it?

Supports and Extension

  • Most concrete entry point: Option 3. Students play the game before they calculate it.
  • Scaffolds already in the journal: a 16-row data table, a grid for the display, the Checker’s “points above vs below” and “branches add to 1” tests.
  • IPP / significant gaps: give a pre-scaled grid (Options 1–2) or a pre-drawn tree with blank fractions (Option 3). Keep the explanation in Criterion 3; that’s what’s being assessed.
  • Extension: each option has a ★★ task.
