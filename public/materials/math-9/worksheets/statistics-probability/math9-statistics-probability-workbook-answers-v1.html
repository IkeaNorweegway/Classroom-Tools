""The next flip will almost certainly be tails — heads has been so lucky that tails is overdue.""

(a) What is the probability of heads on the next flip? Explain.

P(heads on flip 9) =   Because:  

(b) Name the reasoning error the student is making.

(c) What IS true about a large number of coin flips in the long run? How is this different from what the student is claiming?

Answer

Q7 ★★ — Gambler's Fallacy

(a) P(heads on flip 9) = 1/2. Because: each coin flip is completely independent — the coin has no memory of previous flips. P(heads) is always 1/2.

(b) The Gambler's Fallacy — the belief that past independent events affect future probabilities.

(c) Over a very large number of flips, heads and tails will each appear close to 50% of the time (Law of Large Numbers). BUT this happens because future flips collectively balance out — not because any single flip "compensates" for the past. The coin does not adjust to correct an imbalance.

Answer

Q9 ★ — Interpolation and extrapolation (y = 3.5x + 48)

(a) x = 6: y = 3.5(6) + 48 = 21 + 48 = 69%. Interpolation — 6 hours is within the data range (2–12 h). Reliable.

(b) x = 20: y = 3.5(20) + 48 = 70 + 48 = 118%. Extrapolation — 20 hours is beyond the data range.

(c) 118% is impossible (scores max at 100%). The linear model breaks down — students studying 20 hours/week likely experience diminishing returns, fatigue, or stress. The trend observed in 2–12 hours cannot be assumed to continue indefinitely.

Q10 ★ — Dependent events without replacement (5G, 3Y, 8 total)

Q11★★ Extended

Correlation does not mean causation

Research shows a strong positive correlation between the number of hospitals in a city and the number of deaths per year in that city.

(a) Does this mean hospitals cause deaths? Explain what might actually cause both to be high.

(b) What is the name for the variable that causes both? (Hint: it's a third factor driving both.)

(c) Give one other example of a correlation that is not a causal relationship. Explain the third variable.

Answer

Q11 ★★ — Correlation ≠ causation (hospitals and deaths)

(a) No — city population size causes both. Larger cities have more hospitals (needed to serve more people) AND more deaths (more people total). Neither hospitals nor deaths cause each other.

(b) The third factor is called a confounding variable (confounder).

(c) Example: More swimming pools in a neighbourhood correlates with higher home values. Third variable = wealth — affluent areas have more private pools AND higher property values.

S3★★ Extended

Scatter plot + critical reasoning

A study finds a strong positive correlation between the number of ice cream shops in a neighbourhood and the crime rate.

(a) Someone concludes that ice cream shops cause crime. Identify the error.

(b) Propose a third variable that explains why BOTH ice cream shops and crime might be high in the same area.

(c) Design a simple test (in words — not a calculation) that could help determine whether the relationship is causal or coincidental.

Answer

S3 — Ice cream + crime

(a) Correlation ≠ causation. No mechanism connects ice cream to crime.

(b) Third variable: population density / summer heat. Warm, densely populated areas have more outdoor activity → more ice cream purchases AND more opportunity for crime.

(c) Test: compare cities that opened ice cream shops without other changes (same population, same season, same economic conditions) and measure whether crime actually changed. Or compare the same city with and without ice cream shops. A controlled study with matched confounders over time would help separate correlation from causation.
