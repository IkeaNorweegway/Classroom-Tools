Part A — Knowledge (Selected Response)

Circle the best answer. [1 mark each]   /8

1. A bag has 4 red marbles and 6 blue marbles. P(red) =

A) 4/6B) 1/4C) 3/5D) 2/5
Answer D · Why: 4/10 = 2/5 · Common errors: A: red ÷ blue instead of red ÷ total. C: P(blue)

2. P(A) = 0.35. P(not A) =

A) 0.65B) 0.35C) 1.35D) 0.53
Answer A · Why: 1 − 0.35 = 0.65 · Common errors: C: added 1

3. A spinner landed on red 18 times in 60 spins. The experimental probability of red is

A) 0.6B) 0.18C) 0.3D) 1/18
Answer C · Why: 18/60 = 0.3 · Common errors: B: used the count as the probability

4. A coin is flipped and a standard die is rolled. How many outcomes are in the sample space?

A) 8B) 12C) 6D) 36
Answer B · Why: 2 × 6 = 12 · Common errors: A: added. D: two dice

5. Two coins are tossed. P(two heads) =

A) 1/2B) 1/3C) 1/4D) 3/4
Answer C · Why: HH is 1 of 4 outcomes · Common errors: B: thinks the outcomes are HH, HT, TT only

6. Which pair of events is independent?

A) drawing two cards without putting the first backB) rolling a die twiceC) choosing two students from one class for a teamD) drawing two marbles without replacement
Answer B · Why: One roll doesn't affect the next · Common errors: A, C, D: the first choice changes what's left

7. A and B are independent. P(A) = 0.5 and P(B) = 0.2. P(A and B) =

A) 0.7B) 0.3C) 0.25D) 0.1
Answer D · Why: 0.5 × 0.2 = 0.1 · Common errors: A: added. C: averaged

8. A bag has 3 red and 2 blue marbles. A red marble is drawn and not put back. P(red on the next draw) =

A) 2/4B) 3/5C) 3/4D) 2/5
Answer A · Why: 2 red left out of 4 marbles · Common errors: B: forgot that the marble was removed

Part B — Understanding (Short Answer)

Show your working. [3 marks each]   /30

9. A bag has 5 green, 3 yellow and 2 purple cubes. One cube is drawn. Find: [1 each]

(a) P(yellow)(b) P(not green)(c) P(orange)
Answer (a) 3/10 (b) 5/10 = 1/2 (c) 0 (there are no orange cubes)
  • 1 mark each

10. A student flips a coin 40 times and gets 26 heads.

[1](a) What is the experimental probability of heads?  

[1](b) What is the theoretical probability of heads?  

[1](c) Why are they different, and what would you expect after 1000 flips?

Answer (a) 26/40 = 0.65 (b) 0.5 (c) Experimental results vary by chance in a small number of trials. With 1000 flips, the proportion of heads should be closer to 0.5 (about 500 heads).
  • 1 mark each

11. A spinner has three equal sections: red, blue and green. Spin it, then flip a coin.

[2](a) Draw a tree diagram or list the sample space.

[1](b) Find P(blue and tails).  

Answer (a) Six outcomes: R-H, R-T, B-H, B-T, G-H, G-T (tree: three branches, each splitting into H and T).
  • 2 marks: all six outcomes (1 mark for four or five)

(b) 1/6

  • 1 mark

12. Two standard dice are rolled and the numbers are added. Use the table to find: [1 each]

(a) P(sum of 8)  

(b) P(doubles)  

(c) P(sum less than 4)  

+123456
1234567
2345678
3456789
45678910
567891011
6789101112
Answer (a) Sums of 8: (2,6), (3,5), (4,4), (5,3), (6,2), so 5/36 (b) 6 doubles, so 6/36 = 1/6 (c) Sums of 2 or 3: (1,1), (1,2), (2,1), so 3/36 = 1/12
  • 1 mark each

[3]13. A spinner with four equal sections (1, 2, 3, 4) is spun and a die is rolled. Find P(4 on the spinner and an even number on the die).

Answer Independent events. P(4) = 1/4 and P(even) = 3/6 = 1/2. P(both) = 1/4 × 1/2 = 1/8.
  • 1 mark: P(4) = 1/4
  • 1 mark: P(even) = 1/2
  • 1 mark: multiplies to 1/8

[3]14. A bag has 3 red and 7 blue marbles. A marble is drawn, put back, and a second marble is drawn. Find P(blue, then blue).

Answer With replacement, so the draws are independent: 7/10 × 7/10 = 49/100 (0.49).
  • 1 mark: P(blue) = 7/10 both times
  • 1 mark: multiplies
  • 1 mark: 49/100

[3]15. The captain of an Edmonton hockey team has lost the opening coin toss five games in a row. He says:

“I'm due to win the next one.”

Is he right? Explain using probability.

Answer No. Each coin toss is independent: the coin has no memory of earlier tosses. P(winning the next toss) is still 1/2. Thinking you are "due" is the gambler's fallacy.
  • 1 mark: says no
  • 1 mark: the tosses are independent (no memory)
  • 1 mark: the probability is still 1/2

[3]16. A bag has 4 red and 6 blue marbles. Two marbles are drawn without replacement. Find P(red, then red).

Answer 4/10 × 3/9 = 12/90 = 2/15 (about 0.13)
  • 1 mark: 4/10
  • 1 mark: 3/9 (one fewer red and one fewer marble)
  • 1 mark: 2/15

17. A box has 12 granola bars: 5 chocolate and 7 plain. Two bars are taken at random, one after the other, without putting the first back.

[2](a) Find P(both chocolate).

[1](b) Would the probability be higher or lower if the first bar were put back? Explain.

Answer (a) 5/12 × 4/11 = 20/132 = 5/33 (about 0.15)
  • 1 mark: 4/11 for the second bar
  • 1 mark: 5/33

(b) Higher. With the bar put back, the second chance stays 5/12: 5/12 × 5/12 = 25/144 ≈ 0.17. Without replacement there is one fewer chocolate bar for the second pick.

  • 1 mark

18. A Banff restaurant offers 3 main dishes and 4 desserts. A meal is one main and one dessert.

[1](a) How many different meals are possible?  

[2](b) Two of the mains are vegetarian and one dessert is dairy-free. If a meal is chosen at random, what is P(vegetarian main and dairy-free dessert)?

Answer (a) 3 × 4 = 12 meals (b) 2 × 1 = 2 meals fit, so 2/12 = 1/6
  • (a) 1 mark
  • (b) 1 mark: counts 2 meals (or multiplies 2/3 × 1/4); 1 mark: 1/6

19. For a fundraiser, a box holds 10 tickets: 6 red, 3 gold and 1 black. A player draws tickets without looking.

[2](a) For one draw, find P(gold) and P(not black).

[2](b) A player draws two tickets without replacement. Find P(gold, then gold).

[2](c) Another version of the game puts the first ticket back before the second draw. Find P(gold, then gold), and explain why this answer is different from (b).

[2](d) The class played the put-it-back version 50 times and got gold-gold 7 times. Compare the experimental probability with the theoretical probability from (c). Should the class be worried? Explain.

Answer (a) P(gold) = 3/10. P(not black) = 1 − 1/10 = 9/10.
  • 1 mark each

(b) 3/10 × 2/9 = 6/90 = 1/15 (about 0.067)

  • 1 mark: 2/9 for the second draw
  • 1 mark: 1/15

(c) 3/10 × 3/10 = 9/100 (0.09). It is higher than (b) because the gold ticket goes back in, so the second draw still has 3 gold out of 10. In (b) the second draw had only 2 gold out of 9.

  • 1 mark: 9/100
  • 1 mark: explains the change in the sample space

(d) Experimental: 7/50 = 0.14. Theoretical: 0.09. The experimental value is a bit higher, but 50 games is a small number of trials, so results can vary by chance. The class shouldn't be worried; over many more games the results should get closer to 0.09.

  • 1 mark: 0.14 compared with 0.09
  • 1 mark: explains variation in a small number of trials

Part D — Thinking

One question that asks you to judge a claim. [4 marks]   /4

20. Lee says:

“Taking one marble out of a bag hardly changes anything, so drawing without replacement gives the same probability as drawing with replacement.”

Test Lee's claim with a bag of 2 red and 3 blue marbles, finding P(red, then red) both ways. When is Lee nearly right, and when is Lee wrong?

Answer Model answer: With replacement: 2/5 × 2/5 = 4/25 = 0.16. Without replacement: 2/5 × 1/4 = 2/20 = 0.10. That is a big difference, so Lee is wrong for a small bag: removing one red marble changes the second draw from 2 red out of 5 to 1 red out of 4. Lee is nearly right only when the bag is very large. For example, with 200 red and 300 blue, the second draw changes from 200/500 = 0.4 to 199/499 ≈ 0.399, which hardly changes anything. But removing an item always changes the probability a little.
