Teacher Guide

Canada's Independence and Democratic Rights

Social Studies 9  ·  Time and Place  ·  Suggested pacing: 3–4 classes

Guiding question: How did Canada become a more independent nation and expand democratic rights?

Learning outcome: Students explain how Canada gained independence and expanded democratic rights.

Big idea for students to leave with: Independence and rights both expanded gradually and unevenly — celebrating a milestone (1931, the Persons Case) shouldn't erase who was still left out.

Discussion Prompts

Opener

"Can you think of a time someone said 'we've already fixed that' about a rule or rights issue — but really only fixed it partway?" Use this to prime students for the Persons Case's incomplete-progress theme before you introduce it.

Common Misconceptions to Watch For

Misconception
"The Persons Case gave Canadian women the right to vote."

It did not — most Canadian women had already gained federal voting rights by 1918 (with provinces at different times). The Persons Case was specifically about legal personhood and eligibility for appointed/elected office (e.g., the Senate). It also did not extend equally to Indigenous women or many racialized women at the time. Correct this directly — it is one of the most common mix-ups students bring into this unit.

Using the Workbook

Q6 uses a workplace analogy to make the Balfour Declaration/Statute of Westminster concrete before asking students to evaluate gradual vs. sudden independence — a genuinely open question worth letting students disagree on. Q7 deliberately uses a school-relevant scenario (student council) to help students transfer the "partial progress" lesson of the Persons Case to something in their own experience.
