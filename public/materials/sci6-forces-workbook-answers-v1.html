4 ★ Core

A ball of clay is dropped and hits the floor — it flattens and stays flat. Is this elastic or plastic deformation? Explain how you know.

Answer Plastic deformation. The clay kept its new (flat) shape after the force was removed — it did not return to its original shape. Elastic deformation would have meant the clay returned to its original round form. Since it stayed flat, the deformation was permanent = plastic.

Four forces act on the box. Label each arrow with the correct force name.

Answer Labels: Up arrow = Normal force. Down arrow = Gravity / Weight. Right arrow (longer) = Applied force. Left arrow (shorter) = Friction.

7 ★★ Extended

A student wrote the following explanation in their notes:

“The hockey puck slowed down because it ran out of force. When you push something it has force, and the force runs out slowly until the object stops.”

  1. Identify two scientific errors in this statement.
  2. Rewrite the explanation correctly, using the term friction and including both its direction and size (in general terms).
Answer (a) Error 1: Objects do not contain or "run out of" force — force is an interaction between two objects, applied from the outside, not stored inside. Error 2: The puck slowed because friction (an external force from the ice) acted on it — not because of something inside the puck decreasing.
(b) Correct: The hockey puck slowed because friction — a force from the ice surface — acted in the direction opposite to the puck's motion. Friction has a specific direction (opposite to motion) and a measurable size in Newtons. It is an external force from the ice, not something the puck possesses.

9 ★ Core

A student bends a paper clip slightly — it springs back to its original shape. Then the student bends the same clip sharply and it stays permanently bent.

  1. What type of deformation occurred the first time?  
  2. What type of deformation occurred the second time?  
  3. Explain what happened in terms of the elastic limit.
Answer (a) Elastic deformation (springs back).
(b) Plastic deformation (stays permanently bent).
(c) The first bend kept the force below the paper clip's elastic limit — it returned to its original shape. The sharp bend exceeded the elastic limit — the particles permanently rearranged and the clip stayed in its new shape (plastic deformation).
10 ★ Core

A fish pushes backward against the water with its tail fin.

  1. What is the reaction force?
  2. On which object does the reaction force act?
  3. Why does the reaction force move the fish forward rather than being cancelled by the action force?
Answer (a) Reaction force: Water pushes forward on the fish.
(b) Object: The fish.
(c) Action and reaction forces act on different objects — the action (fish pushing water) acts on the water; the reaction (water pushing fish) acts on the fish. Forces only cancel when they act on the same object. Since these act on different objects, they cannot cancel.

14 ★★ Extended

An engineer is choosing a material for a new bike helmet foam. In a crash, the helmet must protect the rider’s head as effectively as possible.

  1. Should the helmet foam deform elastically or plastically in a crash? Circle one:   Elastically   /   Plastically
  2. Explain your choice. Use the definitions of both types of deformation in your answer.
  3. A suspension bridge uses steel cables. Should these cables deform elastically or plastically? Explain why this is the opposite choice to the helmet.
Answer (a) Plastically.
(b) Plastic deformation absorbs impact energy permanently — the foam crushes and stays crushed, meaning it dissipated the collision force rather than bouncing it back. Elastic deformation would return all energy as a rebound, transmitting the full impact force to the rider's head.
(c) Bridge cables must deform elastically — opposite because the cables must return to original length under every load cycle (traffic, wind, temperature changes). Permanent stretching (plastic deformation) would change bridge geometry and risk structural failure.

19 ★★ Extended

A student claims: “When a large truck and a small car collide, the truck exerts a greater force on the car than the car exerts on the truck — because the truck is so much heavier.”

  1. Is this student correct? Circle one:   Yes   /   No
  2. Use Newton’s Third Law to explain what actually happens to the forces during the collision.
  3. If the forces are equal, why is the small car so much more damaged than the truck?
Answer (a) No.
(b) Newton's Third Law: the force the truck exerts on the car is exactly equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the force the car exerts on the truck. Mass does not change this — the forces are always equal during the collision.
(c) The forces are equal, but the masses are very different. The same force causes much greater acceleration in the small-mass car than in the large-mass truck. Greater acceleration = greater speed change = greater structural damage to the lighter vehicle.

21 ★★ Extended

You push down on the seat of a wooden chair with a large force.

  1. What type of external force are you applying?  
  2. What type of internal force acts in the legs of the chair as a result?
  3. You push hard enough and one leg cracks and stays bent. What type of deformation occurred in the leg?
  4. Trace the full chain of events: external force → internal force → deformation outcome. Write it as one connected explanation.
Answer (a) Applied force (downward).
(b) Compression (particles in the legs are squeezed together by the downward external force).
(c) Plastic deformation (leg cracked and stayed bent — permanent change).
(d) Full chain: the applied force (pushing down on the seat) created a compression internal force in the chair legs as particles were forced toward each other. When the force exceeded the elastic limit of the wood, the particles rearranged permanently — plastic deformation — and the leg cracked and remained bent.
22 ★ Core

A book rests on a table. Gravity pulls the book downward with a force of 12 N. The table pushes the book upward with a force of 12 N.

In the box below, draw a force diagram for the book. Include both forces as arrows. Make the arrow lengths proportional to each other.

Your force diagram
  1. Is the net force on the book zero or non-zero?   How do you know?
  2. Are the two forces in this diagram an action-reaction pair? Explain why or why not.
Answer Diagram: two equal-length arrows — 12 N pointing up (normal force) and 12 N pointing down (gravity). Arrows must be the same length.
(a) Net force = zero. The two 12 N forces are equal and opposite — they balance exactly.
(b) NOT an action-reaction pair. Both forces act on the same object (the book). An action-reaction pair must act on different objects. The actual Newton's Third Law pairs are: Earth pulls book down / book pulls Earth up; AND table pushes book up / book pushes table down.
23 ★★ Extended

A student trying to identify action-reaction pairs wrote:

“When I push a shopping cart forward, the action-reaction pair is: (1) my push on the cart, and (2) the friction force on the cart from the floor pushing back.”

  1. Explain the error in this student’s reasoning.
  2. What is the correct reaction force to “my push on the cart”?
  3. Describe one complete, valid action-reaction pair that exists in this situation (besides the one above).
Answer (a) The student identified two forces both acting on the same object (the cart). Applied force AND friction both act on the cart. An action-reaction pair must act on different objects — you cannot have both forces in the pair acting on the same object.
(b) Correct reaction to "my push on the cart": the cart pushes me backward (equal, opposite, acts on me — a different object).
(c) Any valid pair: e.g., my feet push the floor backward (on floor) / floor pushes me forward (on me). OR: cart pushes me backward / I push cart forward. Both forces on different objects.

S1 ★★ Extended

A skateboarder pushes backward against the ground with one foot and rolls forward.

  1. Draw a force diagram for the skateboarder showing all external forces. Label each arrow with the force name and direction.
    Force diagram
  2. Identify one complete action-reaction pair. For each force: name it, state its direction, and name the object it acts on.
  3. The skateboarder is accelerating forward. What does this tell you about the horizontal forces? Explain using net force.
Answer (a) Force diagram must include: gravity (down), normal force (up from ground), friction from ground (forward — reaction to foot pushing back). All labeled, arrows proportional.
(b) Action-reaction pair: Foot pushes ground backward (on ground) / Ground pushes foot (skater) forward (on skater). Equal, opposite, different objects.
(c) Net horizontal force is forward (ground reaction > any resistive forces) → forward acceleration. If net force were zero, skater would move at constant speed, not accelerate.
S2 ★★ Extended

An engineer drops a concrete block onto a foam pad to test the foam’s properties. The foam stays crushed after the block is removed.

  1. What external force acts on the foam pad when the block lands?  
  2. What internal force acts inside the foam as it is crushed?  
  3. The foam stays crushed. What type of deformation occurred?  
  4. Is this the same deformation type you would want in a bicycle helmet? Explain your reasoning fully.
Answer (a) External force: Applied force (from the concrete block pressing down).
(b) Internal force: Compression (particles squeezed together).
(c) Deformation type: Plastic (stays crushed — permanent).
(d) Yes — same type. Helmet foam SHOULD deform plastically in a crash: plastic deformation absorbs the impact energy by permanently crushing, rather than bouncing the force back elastically toward the rider's head. The crushed foam is evidence the energy was absorbed. A used/crashed helmet must be replaced because the foam has already plastically deformed.
S3 ★★ Extended

Two cars of very different masses collide head-on. The lighter car is crushed significantly; the heavier car is barely dented.

  1. According to Newton’s Third Law, how do the forces on each car compare during the collision?
  2. The lighter car’s frame is permanently deformed. What type of deformation is this?  
  3. An engineer says modern car frames should deform plastically in a crash rather than elastically. Explain why this design decision saves lives.
  4. A classmate says: “The heavier car exerted a greater force, which is why the lighter car was more damaged.” Write a precise correction of this statement using Newton’s Third Law and the concept of mass.
Answer (a) Forces are equal during the collision. Newton's Third Law: the force the heavier car exerts on the lighter is exactly equal (and opposite) to the force the lighter car exerts on the heavier.
(b) Plastic deformation (permanently crushed).
(c) Plastic deformation dissipates impact energy over time and distance by permanently changing shape — "crumple zones." If the frame were elastic, it would rebound and transmit the full collision force instantly to the passenger compartment. Plastic deformation slows the force, reducing its effect on occupants.
(d) Correction: By Newton's Third Law, the forces are exactly equal — the heavier car did NOT exert more force. The lighter car is more damaged because the same force produces much greater acceleration in a smaller mass (a = F/m). Greater acceleration = greater deformation — not a greater force.
S4 ★★ Extended

You are designing a shock absorber spring for a mountain bike. It must: absorb bumps on rough terrain, and return to its original shape after each bump.

  1. Should the spring deform elastically or plastically? Explain.
  2. What type of internal force acts inside the spring when it is compressed by a bump?  
  3. When the rider hits a bump, the spring compresses. Identify the external force causing this compression:  
  4. On a small bump, the spring compresses 2 cm and fully returns. On a huge pothole, the spring compresses 9 cm and only returns to 5 cm compressed. Explain what happened on the huge pothole in terms of the elastic limit and deformation type.
  5. After the huge pothole, the rider feels the bike’s rear wheel pushing up harder than normal. Identify an action-reaction pair involving the damaged spring and the bike frame.
Answer (a) Elastically — must return to original length after each bump to absorb the next one. Plastic deformation would leave it permanently compressed and non-functional.
(b) Internal force: Compression.
(c) External force: Applied force / impact from road (transmitted through the wheel).
(d) Small bump: force below elastic limit → elastic deformation → spring fully returns. Huge pothole: force exceeded elastic limit → partial plastic deformation → spring permanently shortened by 5 cm and stays compressed.
(e) Action-reaction pair: Damaged spring pushes up on bike frame (on frame) / bike frame pushes down on spring (on spring). Equal magnitude, opposite direction, different objects.
