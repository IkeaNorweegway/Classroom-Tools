Section 1: Types of Structures

Learning target: I can classify structures as solid, frame, or shell, and explain why structure type matters.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Solid structure A structure made of solid material all the way through; its mass carries the load Dam, brick, tree trunk
Frame structure A structure made of connected parts (a skeleton) with open spaces between them Bicycle, building frame, spider web
Shell structure A thin, curved outer surface that carries the load through its shape Egg, igloo, helmet
Combination structure A structure that uses two or three of the types together House, car

Guided Notes

A structure is any object that supports a load or withstands forces without changing its shape.

Three basic types:

Type Description How it carries load Examples
Solid Made throughout of solid (continuous) material; no internal spaces Load spreads through the entire mass (structure) dam, brick, glacier, tree trunk
Frame A rigid skeleton (framework) of connected members; open spaces inside Load carried by the member (beam and strut) network bicycle, Eiffel Tower, building frame, spider web
Shell A thin curved surface; thin walls Curved shape distributes forces along the surface egg, igloo, car body, dome, helmet

Most real-world structures are combination structures — they combine elements of two or three types. Examples:

  • A house: wood (timber) frame inside (frame) + brick/siding outside (shell) + concrete foundation (solid)
  • A car: body panels (shell/frame) surrounding the chassis (frame engine)

Why does structure type matter? The same material can support vastly different loads depending on its shape. A flat sheet of cardboard bends easily. Roll it into a tube → increases (multiplies) the load-bearing capacity. Arch it → more again. The shape, not just the material, determines strength.

Alberta examples:

  • The Lethbridge Viaduct: frame structure — one of the longest frame structures in North America
  • Oil storage tanks: shell structure — cylindrical shell
  • Rocky Mountain dams: solid + frame combination
Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. What is the difference between a frame and a shell structure?
    Answer A frame is a rigid skeleton of connected members with open spaces; the members carry the load. A shell is a thin curved surface; the load spreads along the surface.
  2. Classify each: (a) egg   (b) bicycle   (c) concrete dam  
    Answer (a) egg — shell (b) bicycle — frame (c) concrete dam — solid
  3. Name one combination structure and identify which types it combines.
    Answer Example: a house — wood frame (frame) + siding or brick exterior (shell) + concrete foundation (solid). Accept any combination structure with its types correctly identified.
  4. Why can a tube of cardboard hold more weight than the same flat sheet?
    Answer Shape determines strength. The curved wall of the tube spreads the load evenly around its whole surface, and the tube resists bending; a flat sheet has almost no resistance to bending.
  5. Stretch: Design a structure to span a 30 cm gap using only 5 sheets of paper and tape. Before building: predict which structure type will work best and explain why.
    Answer Stretch: A frame or shell made from shaped paper will beat flat sheets: roll the paper into tubes, or fold it into triangular beams or accordion pleats, then tape them into a span. Reason: the same material carries far more load when its shape resists bending. Accept any prediction with a shape-based reason.

Section 2: Loads

Learning target: I can identify dead, live, and dynamic loads, and explain how each affects a structure.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Dead load The permanent weight of the structure itself The steel and concrete of a bridge
Live load The changing weight of the things the structure supports People, furniture, vehicles, snow on a roof
Dynamic load A force that changes suddenly or keeps changing in size or direction Wind gusts, earthquakes, moving traffic

Guided Notes

Every structure must handle multiple types of loads — forces acting on it from many sources.

Dead load: the permanent (constant) weight of the structure itself. Always present. Always acts downward (gravity). Cannot be avoided — part of structural design is keeping dead load manageable.

Examples: weight of steel beams, concrete, glass, roof tiles.

Live load: the weight of the people and objects (contents) the structure is designed to support. Varies — it is not always at maximum. Acts downward (gravity).

Examples: people, furniture, vehicles, snow on a roof.

Dynamic load: changing (moving / sudden) and often unpredictable (variable) in direction. The most dangerous loads in terms of structural failure.

Source Direction Effect
Wind sideways (horizontal) (lateral) pushes on walls, lifts roofs
Earthquake back and forth and up and down (many directions) (lateral and vertical) shakes foundations, causes structural collapse
Moving traffic downward (vertical) and along the direction of travel (horizontal) rhythmic vibration; resonance danger
Impact (crash) any direction sudden concentrated force

Why dynamic loads fail structures:

Static loads are predictable — engineers design for the maximum expected load. Dynamic loads are unpredictable in direction and variable (changing) in magnitude. They can also cause resonance: if a force is applied at a structure's natural frequency, it can cause it to vibrate with increasing amplitude until it fails.

Historical example:

The Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1940) — collapsed not because of extreme wind speed, but because wind matched the bridge's natural frequency. The bridge started oscillating and tore itself apart.

Alberta loads to consider:

  • Wind: high winds on open prairies; chinook winds in foothills
  • Snow: dead load that accumulates — Alberta roofs must be designed to handle snow weight
  • Frost heave: freezing ground expands (dynamic), pushing up foundations
Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. What is the difference between a live load and a dead load? Give one example of each.
    Answer Dead load is the permanent weight of the structure itself (e.g., a bridge's steel). Live load is the changing weight of what the structure supports (e.g., cars, people, snow).
  2. Why are dynamic loads more dangerous than static loads?
    Answer Dynamic loads change suddenly, can come from any direction, and vary in size, so they are hard to design for. They can also cause resonance, where vibrations build until the structure fails.
  3. A school roof in Edmonton collapses in February. It wasn't windy. What type of load likely caused the failure?
    Answer Snow load — a live load that built up on the roof beyond what it was designed to carry.
  4. Classify each load: (a) the weight of a bridge's steel   (b) a truck driving across   (c) an earthquake   (d) the weight of students in a classroom  
    Answer (a) dead (b) dynamic (accept live) (c) dynamic (d) live
  5. Stretch: A new skyscraper is being built downtown in Calgary. What specific dynamic loads should the engineers plan for? For each, describe its direction of force.
    Answer Stretch: Wind and chinook gusts — sideways, pushing on the walls and causing sway. Earth tremors — ground shaking back and forth and up and down. Moving people, elevators, and nearby traffic or trains — vertical and horizontal vibration. Accept temperature swings (expansion and contraction) and impact. Each load needs a direction.

Section 3: Internal Forces

Learning target: I can identify compression, tension, shear, and torsion forces in structures and explain where they act.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Compression A force that squeezes or pushes material together A column holding up a roof
Tension A force that stretches or pulls material apart A cable on a suspension bridge
Shear Forces pushing in opposite directions that make parts of a material slide past each other Scissors cutting paper; a bolt between two plates
Torsion A twisting force Wringing out a towel

Guided Notes

External forces (loads) applied to a structure create internal forces inside the materials of the structure. Engineers must understand what type of internal force each part of a structure experiences, because different materials resist different forces.

The four internal forces:

COMPRESSION: forces that push (squeeze) material together (squeezing inward)

  • Direction: force pushes inward (together)
  • Materials that resist well: hard, rigid materials (masonry) (concrete, stone, brick)
  • Failure mode: buckling (long columns under compression buckle sideways)
  • Example in structures: column holding up a floor, arch bridge under load

TENSION: forces that pull (stretch) material apart (pulling outward)

  • Direction: force pulls outward (apart)
  • Materials that resist well: steel and strong fibres (steel cables, ropes)
  • Failure mode: snapping
  • Example in structures: cables in a suspension bridge, rebar in concrete

SHEAR: forces acting on parallel planes in opposite directions

  • Direction: parallel to the surface, opposing each other
  • Like trying to cut (slide apart / tear) a material
  • Example in structures: bolts in a joint, screws, a knife cutting through material

TORSION: a twisting force

  • Direction: rotational, around the long axis
  • Like wringing (twisting) a wet towel
  • Example in structures: a shaft in an engine, a drill bit, a bridge deck in a twisting wind
Key insight:

A single structural element often experiences multiple types simultaneously. A beam supporting a floor experiences compression on its top surface (being squashed by the floor above) and tension on its bottom surface (being stretched by the weight pulling down). This is why steel I-beams have flanges at top and bottom — more material where compression and tension are greatest.

Material selection:

Material Strong in Weak in
Concrete compression tension
Steel both compression and tension shear under impact
Wood compression and tension along the grain shear (it splits along the grain)
Rope/cable tension compression

Reinforced concrete = concrete (compression) + steel rebar (tension) → handles both. Used everywhere.

  1. What is compression? What is tension? What is the key difference between them?
    Answer Compression pushes or squeezes material together; tension pulls or stretches it apart. They act in opposite directions.
  2. Why is concrete often reinforced with steel?
    Answer Concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension — it cracks when stretched. Steel rebar carries the tension, so reinforced concrete handles both.
  3. Name the force type:
    Answer Cable holding up a drawbridge: tension. Column supporting a roof: compression. Bolt connecting two plates: shear.

Section 4: Stability

Learning target: I can explain how centre of gravity and structural design affect stability, and describe failure modes.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Centre of gravity The single point where all of an object's weight seems to act The middle of a ruler balanced on a finger
Base of support The area on the ground enclosed by the points where a structure touches it The area between the four legs of a chair
Stability The ability of a structure to stay upright and not tip over A wide, low sports car
Triangulation Using triangles in a structure to make it rigid Trusses in a bridge or roof

Guided Notes

Centre of gravity (CoG): the single point where the entire weight of an object can be considered to act. For a uniform object, the CoG is at the centre (geometric centre). For an irregular object, it depends on where mass is distributed.

The stability rule:

A structure is stable when its centre of gravity is above (over / within) its base of support. When the CoG moves outside the base, the structure topples (tips over).

Lower CoG → more stable

Wider base → more stable

MISCONCEPTION

"A heavy bottom always makes a structure more stable." More precisely: What determines stability is where the centre of gravity is relative to the base of support. A very tall structure with a wide base but all its mass near the top can still topple. A short, wide structure with mass at the bottom is highly stable. The relationship between CoG position and base area is what matters — not weight alone.

Increasing stability in design:

  • Widen the base (triangle at the base)
  • Lower the centre of gravity (heavy components at the bottom)
  • Use triangulation (triangles) (rigid triangular units that cannot deform)

Why triangles? A triangle is the only polygon that cannot change shape without changing the length of its sides. A square under force can become a rhombus (deform) — a triangle cannot deform. This is why the triangle (triangulation) is the fundamental structural element in bridges, cranes, and towers.

Failure modes:

Failure Cause Example
Buckling compression in a slender column tall, thin column under excessive compression
Toppling CoG moves outside the base tower crane with too much load on one side
Shear failure opposing forces tear through a joint bolt snapping in a connection
Fatigue repeated loading and unloading over time metal cracking after millions of cycles

Arches redirect the load (compression forces) down and outward to the supports (abutments). They are highly efficient under compression — stone and concrete arches have lasted thousands of years. The weakness: arches fail catastrophically if the abutments move apart.

Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. What determines whether a structure topples?
    Answer Whether its centre of gravity stays above its base of support. If the centre of gravity moves outside the base, the structure topples.
  2. Why is a triangle the strongest polygon?
    Answer A triangle cannot change shape unless the length of one of its sides changes. A square can be pushed into a rhombus without any side changing length, so it deforms easily.
  3. A sports car is designed with a very low body. Why does this make the car more stable than a tall SUV with the same wheelbase?
    Answer The low body gives a lower centre of gravity. With the same wheelbase, the car has to tilt much farther before its centre of gravity moves outside the base, so it is harder to tip.
  4. Name two ways to increase the stability of a tall structure.
    Answer Any two: widen the base; lower the centre of gravity (heavy parts at the bottom); use triangulation or bracing.
  5. Stretch: A boom crane lifts a load at the end of its horizontal arm. As the load increases, at what point does the crane risk toppling? Draw a diagram showing the crane's base of support, the CoG with a small load, and the CoG with an excessive load.
    Answer Stretch: The crane risks toppling when the combined centre of gravity (crane plus load) moves past the edge of its base of support. Diagram: base of support marked; with a small load the CoG sits above the base; with an excessive load the CoG has shifted out toward the load, beyond the base edge. Counterweights and outriggers shift the CoG back and widen the base.

Section 5: Simple Machines and Mechanical Advantage

Learning target: I can identify the six simple machines, calculate mechanical advantage, and explain why machines do not create energy.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Simple machine A basic device that changes the size or direction of a force Lever, pulley, ramp
Mechanical advantage (MA) How many times a machine multiplies your force; load force ÷ effort force An MA of 4 lifts 200 N with 50 N of effort
Effort force The force you apply to the machine Pushing down on a crowbar
Load force The force the machine applies to the object (the weight being moved) The weight of the rock being lifted

Guided Notes

A simple machine changes the size (magnitude) or direction of a force. It does not create energy — it transfers energy from input to output.

Mechanical advantage: MA = load force ÷ effort force

If MA > 1: the machine multiplies your force (you apply less force, but over more distance)

If MA = 1: direction of force changes only

If MA < 1: the machine multiplies your distance (speed) (you apply more force over less distance, but get speed)

MISCONCEPTION

"Simple machines give you more power — you get more out than you put in." Why this is wrong: Energy is conserved. If a machine multiplies your force by 3, you must push 3× the distance. The total work (force × distance) is the same on both sides — minus friction losses. You never get more energy out than you put in. What machines give you is convenience — the ability to apply a smaller force over a longer distance, which is often much more practical.

The six simple machines:

1. LEVER — a rigid bar that pivots on a fixed point (fulcrum)

Three classes based on where the fulcrum (F), effort (E), and load (L) are:

Class Arrangement Example MA
1st class F between E and L seesaw (also scissors, pliers), crowbar can be >1 or <1
2nd class L between F and E wheelbarrow, nutcracker (also bottle opener) always greater than 1
3rd class E between F and L tweezers, fishing rod (also broom, hockey stick, baseball bat) always less than 1

2. WHEEL AND AXLE — a large wheel attached to a smaller axle; rotating one rotates the other

MA = radius of wheel ÷ radius of axle

Examples: doorknob, steering wheel, screwdriver, water tap (also bicycle pedals and crank, winch)

3. PULLEY — a wheel with a rope over it; changes direction or magnitude of force

Type MA How
Single fixed pulley 1 changes direction only
Single movable pulley 2 multiplies force by 2
Compound (block and tackle) = number of supporting rope segments each rope segment supports part of load

4. INCLINED PLANE — a sloped (slanted) surface; reduces force required to raise an object

MA = length of ramp ÷ height of ramp

Example: ramp, staircase, switchback mountain road

5. WEDGE — two inclined planes back to back; converts a pushing force into a splitting force

Examples: axe, knife, chisel

6. SCREW — an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder; converts rotational force into linear force

Examples: wood screw, bolt, jar lid

Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. What is mechanical advantage? What does an MA of 3 tell you?
    Answer Mechanical advantage is load force ÷ effort force — how many times the machine multiplies your force. An MA of 3 means the output force is three times the effort, and you must move the effort three times as far.
  2. Identify the simple machine: (a) a ramp at a loading dock   (b) a fishing rod   (c) a flagpole rope and pulley   (d) a jar lid  
    Answer (a) inclined plane (b) lever (3rd class) (c) pulley (single fixed) (d) screw
  3. A lever has a 200 N load and the effort needed is 50 N. What is the MA?
    Answer MA = 200 ÷ 50 = 4

    4. MA = 200 N ÷ 50 N = 4

  4. Explain: if a machine gives you a mechanical advantage of 4, do you have to do less work? Explain using energy conservation.
    Answer No. You apply one-quarter of the force, but over four times the distance. Work = force × distance, so the total work is the same (slightly more in practice because of friction). Machines do not create energy.
  5. Stretch: Design a compound pulley system to lift a 600 N engine block using only 150 N of effort. How many rope segments does your system need? Draw it.
    Answer Stretch: MA needed = 600 ÷ 150 = 4, so four supporting rope segments. Drawing: a block and tackle with two pulleys on top and two on the bottom, four rope segments holding up the lower block.

Section 6: Materials and Structural Design

Learning target: I can compare material properties and recommend appropriate materials for specific structural roles.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Strength How much force a material can take before it breaks Steel beam
Hardness How well a material resists scratching and denting Diamond, hardened steel
Flexibility / ductility The ability to bend or stretch without breaking Copper wire, steel
Elasticity The ability to return to its original shape after being bent or stretched Rubber band, spring
Brittleness Breaking suddenly with little or no bending first Glass, concrete

Guided Notes

Material properties that matter in structural engineering:

Property What it means Good example Bad example
Strength Resists forces (loads) without breaking steel glass under impact
Hardness Resists denting or scratching diamond, hardened steel copper
Flexibility Can bend without breaking; ductile steel, copper concrete
Elasticity Returns to its original shape after deformation rubber, spring steel clay (plastic — stays deformed)
Brittleness breaks (shatters / fractures) with little deformation glass, concrete, cast iron steel

Choosing the right material:

Structural role Required property Best material choice
Tower column under compression compressive strength concrete or steel
Suspension bridge cable tension strength steel cable
Earthquake-resistant building frame flexibility steel
Cutting edge of a tool hardness hardened steel, carbide

Cost, sustainability, and availability must also factor into material selection — the best engineering material is often not affordable or locally available. In Alberta:

  • Timber framing: wood is locally available, renewable, good strength-to-weight ratio
  • Steel: manufactured, recyclable, high strength
  • Concrete: locally sourced aggregate; low cost; poor in tension without rebar
Connection to Section 1:

Strong materials don't automatically make strong structures. A triangulated truss frame made of cardboard can carry more load than a solid block of the same cardboard, because of how the structure distributes the force.

Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. What is the difference between strength and hardness?
    Answer Strength is how much force a material withstands before it breaks. Hardness is how well its surface resists scratching and denting. A material can be hard but still break easily (glass).
  2. Why is steel often preferred over concrete for building frames in earthquake-prone areas?
    Answer Steel is flexible: it bends and absorbs the shaking without breaking. Concrete is brittle and cracks suddenly under the same sideways forces.
  3. Match: (a) suspension bridge cable needs   (b) bridge pier needs   (c) car bumper needs  
    Answer (a) tensile strength (b) compressive strength (c) flexibility / elasticity (absorbs impact)
  4. Stretch: Evaluate the use of wood as a structural material for a multi-story building in Alberta. Consider strength, flexibility, local availability, sustainability, and any limitations.
    Answer Stretch: For: wood is locally available in Alberta, renewable, light for its strength, and flexible enough to handle wind and movement. Limits: it burns, rots if wet, and is weaker than steel or concrete for very tall buildings, so it needs fire protection, moisture protection, and engineered products (laminated timber). A reasoned judgement either way is acceptable.
