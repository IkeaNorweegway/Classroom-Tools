Concept 1: Mechanical Advantage and the Lever-Arm Ratio

I can: explain how a trebuchet's long-arm/short-arm ratio creates a force/speed trade-off.

A trebuchet's throwing arm pivots on a point with a long side and a short side. The   of the long arm's length to the short arm's length determines the trade-off between force and speed — this directly extends the motor-and-mechanism reasoning from Grade 8's gear ratios to a much larger, counterweight-powered scale. A longer throwing arm relative to the counterweight arm generally means the tip moves   (faster/slower), but the counterweight has to do more work to swing it.

Concept 2: Energy Transfer — Counterweight to Projectile

I can: describe how a counterweight's potential energy becomes a projectile's kinetic energy.

A raised counterweight has potential energy — stored energy due to its height. As it falls and swings the arm, that energy transfers into the arm's motion and finally into the projectile's kinetic energy — the energy of motion — at the moment of release. This is the same energy vocabulary from Grade 5's Slow Coaster unit, now applied at a far larger scale: energy doesn't appear from nowhere, it transfers from one form and one object to another.

Check Yourself — no peeking
  1. Where does the projectile's kinetic energy actually come from?
  2. What happens to a trebuchet's launch if the counterweight starts lower (less height to fall)?

Concept 3: Structural Bracing at Scale

I can: explain why a full-size frame needs different structural thinking than a tabletop model.

A tabletop prototype's frame barely has to resist any real load or twisting force. A full-size, ~1m frame under real launch forces does — the frame has to stay rigid and stable while a heavy counterweight drops and the arm swings hard. Structural bracing — cross-pieces and joints designed specifically to resist twisting and wobble — becomes a real engineering requirement at this scale in a way it simply isn't at tabletop size.

Concept 4: Why Scaling Up Is Not Just "Bigger"

I can: explain why a design validated at small scale can behave differently at full scale.

This capstone has a non-negotiable rule: prototype small, validate it with real measurements, then build full-scale. That rule exists because of a real engineering trap.

MISCONCEPTION

"If my small prototype works well, building the exact same design ten times bigger will just work the same way, only bigger."

Why this fails: weight, material stiffness, and structural stress don't all scale up at the same rate as length does. A frame member that felt rock-solid at tabletop size can flex or fail at full scale even with the "same" proportions, and a counterweight-to-arm ratio tuned for a light prototype arm doesn't automatically stay optimal once the arm itself is heavier, longer wood. A full-scale build following a naive "just make it bigger" plan can genuinely underperform, or fail structurally, even though the small version worked perfectly.

Correct understanding: A validated small-scale ratio is a strong starting point, not a guarantee. The full-scale build has to be tested on its own, measured on its own, and adjusted based on what actually happens at that size — not assumed to work just because the prototype did.
