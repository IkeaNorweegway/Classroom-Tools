Teacher Guide

Post-War Canada: Change, Cooperation, and Conflict

Social Studies 9  ·  Time and Place  ·  Suggested pacing: 6–7 classes (largest unit)

Guiding question: How did political, economic, and social changes reshape Canada after WWII?

Learning outcome: Students analyze how post-war developments shaped Canada as a modern nation.

Big idea for students to leave with: This 30-year period built almost everything students associate with "Canadian identity" today (healthcare, multiculturalism, the Charter) — and it did so unevenly, with real regional and Indigenous harms alongside the gains.

Discussion Prompts

Before Section 3

Set expectations explicitly: "This section holds two true things about the same period at the same time — Canada was becoming more diverse and rights-focused, and Canada was also causing serious, lasting harm to Indigenous families. We are not going to smooth that over." This framing helps prevent the "but also good things happened" deflection some students may reach for.

Regional tension discussion

"Whose 'national interest' counts as national?" Use this to open Section 4 — a useful throughline for the rest of the course's Systems and Citizenship units as well.

Common Misconceptions to Watch For

Misconception
"Multiculturalism and residential schools/the Sixties Scoop are unrelated topics that both happened to occur in this period."

Make the connection explicit: Canada was expanding its embrace of some forms of diversity (through immigration and multiculturalism policy) while simultaneously working to erase Indigenous cultural identity through residential schools and the Sixties Scoop. Naming this contradiction directly is more honest — and more memorable — than presenting the two as separate, disconnected facts.

Using the Workbook

This unit's extended questions (Q7–Q9) each isolate one of the unit's three hardest ideas — regional unfairness (NEP), the coexistence of international praise and internal harm, and reasonable limits on rights — using an accessible analogy before asking students to apply it historically. Expect a wide range of defensible positions on all three; grade for reasoning quality and use of unit evidence, not for reaching a particular conclusion.
