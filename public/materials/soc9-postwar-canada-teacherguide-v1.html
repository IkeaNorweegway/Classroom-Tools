Teacher Guide

Post-War Canada: Change, Cooperation, and Conflict

Social Studies 9  ·  Time and Place  ·  Suggested pacing: 6–7 classes (largest unit)

Unit Overview

Suggested length: 8 classes  Tier: 1  Unit colour: Teal

Guiding question: How did political, economic, and social changes reshape Canada after WWII?

Learning outcome: Students analyze how post-war developments shaped Canada as a modern nation.

What this unit is really about: This is five sections' worth of content compressed into one unit, and it is tempting to just race through the timeline. Resist that — the throughline that makes it cohere is that almost everything students recognize as "Canadian" today (healthcare, multiculturalism, the Charter, peacekeeping) was built by real political choices in these three decades, not by inevitability. Keep coming back to that: the gains (rights, diversity, international reputation) sat alongside real harms (residential schools, the Sixties Scoop) and real regional resentment (Western alienation) in the very same years. Students should leave able to hold both realities at once, not pick one and ignore the other.

Lesson Sequence

Class 1 — The Welfare State
Building the welfare state after WWII
Show Ask students: "If you broke your leg tomorrow, what would happen — for you, and for your family's finances?" Let a few answer, then reveal that the answer they just gave (free treatment, no bill) was not guaranteed for Canadians before this unit's period.
Say "After WWII, Canada built what we call a 'welfare state' — government programs like universal healthcare and old-age pensions that we now take for granted. This didn't appear from nowhere. It came out of two specific pressures: the hardship people had just lived through in the Great Depression, and the fact that fighting a war together raised people's expectations that government should look after its citizens in peacetime too."
Cue "Which of today's government services do you think your grandparents' generation didn't have growing up?"
Notes Maps to Notes Section 1 (p. 1), including the pre-knowledge check that opens the notes package. Feeds Workbook Q1.
Class 2 — Canada on the World Stage
NATO, Korea, NORAD, and Pearson's peacekeeping
Show A simple timeline or map with four stops: NATO founding, the Korean War (Kapyong, Hill 355), NORAD, and the Suez Crisis.
Say "Canada helped found NATO for collective defence, fought in Korea at places like Kapyong and Hill 355, partnered with the US through NORAD to watch North American skies, and when the Suez Crisis nearly escalated into a wider war, Lester B. Pearson proposed something new — a UN peacekeeping force. That idea earned him the Nobel Peace Prize and is a big part of why Canadians think of themselves as peacekeepers."
Cue "Was Canada acting out of pure idealism, out of self-interest, or both? Can it be both?"
Notes Maps to Notes Section 2 (p. 2). Feeds Workbook Q2 and the Section 2 stretch retrieval question (global reputation vs. national identity).
Classes 3–4 — Immigration, Multiculturalism, and Indigenous Policy
Class 3: Immigration and multiculturalism after WWII
Show Point out that the notes describe immigration patterns shifting after WWII toward non-European regions such as India, China, and Vietnam, alongside displaced persons and Holocaust survivors from Europe (1945–1951).
Say "As Canada's immigrant population became more diverse, government policy started actively recognizing and including different cultural groups instead of expecting everyone to assimilate into one dominant culture. That's where multiculturalism as an official policy idea comes from."
Cue "Hold that thought — next class we look at a very different government policy from these same years."
Notes Maps to Notes Section 3 (p. 3), first half. Feeds Workbook Q6.
Class 4: Residential schools and the Sixties Scoop — treat with care
Show Open by reading the framing statement aloud to the whole class before any content, so expectations are set before, not during.
Say "This section holds two true things about the same period at the same time — Canada was becoming more diverse and rights-focused, and Canada was also causing serious, lasting harm to Indigenous families through residential schools and the Sixties Scoop, by separating children from their families and cultures. We are not going to smooth that over, and we are not going to let the good news cancel out the harm. Both are part of the honest history."
Cue "Why might it be tempting — but historically dishonest — to tell only one half of this story?"
Notes Maps to Notes Section 3 (p. 3) misconception box. Feeds Workbook Q3 and the extended Q8. Consider using or adapting locally-approved Indigenous-education resources alongside this class.
Classes 5–6 — National Unity and Regional Tension
Class 5: The October Crisis and Meech Lake
Show Present the October Crisis (1970) and the debates over Quebec separatism as one thread, then the Meech Lake Accord (1987) as a later attempt to resolve the same underlying question.
Say "Canadian identity isn't only built through cooperation — it's also shaped by real disagreement. The October Crisis and the debates over Quebec separatism showed how differently people across the country saw Canada's political future. Almost twenty years later, the Meech Lake Accord tried to address Quebec's status within the constitution — and its failure showed those tensions hadn't gone away."
Cue "What does it tell us when an accord designed to bring a country together fails to pass?"
Notes Maps to Notes Section 4 (p. 4), first half.
Class 6: Western alienation and the National Energy Program
Show The Alberta connection box from the notes: ask why control over natural resources is such a persistent source of federal-provincial tension.
Say "As the petroleum industry grew, especially in provinces like Alberta and Saskatchewan, the federal government's National Energy Program tried to manage energy resources at a national level — but it was felt very differently depending on where you lived. That mismatch between a federal policy and a regional experience is what we call Western alienation."
Cue "Whose 'national interest' counts as national?" — a useful throughline for the rest of the course's Systems and Citizenship units as well.
Notes Maps to Notes Section 4 (p. 4), second half and connection box. Feeds Workbook Q4 and the extended Q7.
Class 7 — The Constitution Act and the Charter
Patriation and the Charter of Rights and Freedoms
Show Ask: "Before 1982, if Canada wanted to change its own constitution, who did it have to ask?"
Say "The Constitution Act of 1982 was a huge step in Canadian independence — for the first time, Canada could amend its own constitution without asking the British Parliament. Bundled into that same act was the Charter of Rights and Freedoms, which gave individual rights and freedoms much stronger legal protection than before."
Cue "If the Charter protects your rights, can the government ever limit them? Hold that question — it's exactly what Workbook Q9 asks."
Notes Maps to Notes Section 5 (p. 5). Feeds Workbook Q5 and the extended Q9.
Class 8 — Workbook and Synthesis
Parts A and B, then the unit synthesis
Show Have students dig out their Pre-Knowledge Check page from Class 1 before opening the workbook.
Say "Now that we've covered the whole unit, go back to page one and see what you'd change or add. Then let's use Part A and Part B of the workbook to pull everything together — especially the three extended questions, which each use an everyday analogy before asking you to apply it historically."
Cue "Which analogy in Q7–Q9 was easiest to connect to the historical example, and which was hardest — and why?"
Notes Workbook Parts A (Q1–Q6) and B (Q7–Q9); Notes Unit Synthesis page and the return to the Pre-Knowledge Check.

Misconception Management

MisconceptionWhen to addressStrategy
"Multiculturalism and residential schools/the Sixties Scoop are unrelated topics that both happened to occur in this period."Class 4Make the connection explicit: Canada was expanding its embrace of some forms of diversity (through immigration and multiculturalism policy) while simultaneously working to erase Indigenous cultural identity through residential schools and the Sixties Scoop. Naming this contradiction directly is more honest — and more memorable — than presenting the two as separate, disconnected facts.
"Canada was militarily neutral or uninvolved after WWII, and only became a peacekeeper."Class 2Clarify the order of events: Canada fought in Korea and helped found NATO for collective defence first. Peacekeeping, as a distinct idea, came later through Pearson's Suez Crisis proposal — it wasn't a pacifist starting point, it was one tool Canada added to its foreign-policy toolkit.
"Canada has always had universal healthcare and pensions — it's just always been this way."Class 1Trace it back explicitly to the two pressures named in the notes: Depression-era hardship and rising wartime expectations of government support. Nothing about the welfare state was automatic or permanent from the start.
"Western alienation is just Alberta wanting more money."Class 6Reframe it around voice and control, not just revenue: the core grievance is feeling that decisions about a region's resources were made without that region's input. Use the "whose national interest counts as national" framing to get past the money-only reading.
"The Constitution Act (1982) is when Canada got its first constitution."Class 7Distinguish patriation from creation: Canada already had a constitution going back to 1867. What changed in 1982 was the ability to amend that constitution domestically, without asking the British Parliament — plus the addition of the Charter.
"The October Crisis and Quebec separatism are settled history with no relevance today."Class 5Connect it forward to ongoing national unity conversations still active in Canadian politics, and back to the Meech Lake Accord's failure a generation later — this is a thread that runs through the rest of the country's political history, not a closed chapter.

Discussion and Source Activity Notes

Framing Section 3 with care (Class 4):

  • Set the "two true things at once" framing explicitly before any content — see the Say line for Class 4 above. Say it, don't just imply it.
  • Watch for the "but also good things happened" deflection some students may reach for; the framing statement exists specifically to head that off.
  • Keep language grounded in what the notes package establishes (family separation, loss of culture) rather than adding unverified specifics.
  • Consider using or adapting locally-approved Indigenous-education resources alongside this class rather than relying on the notes package alone.

Suez Crisis and the invention of peacekeeping (Class 2):

  • Have students explain, in their own words, what problem Pearson's proposal actually solved (a way to separate combatants without either side "losing").
  • Push past the assumption that peacekeeping is the same as neutrality — Canada had already fought in Korea by this point.
  • Use the stretch retrieval question from Notes Section 2 as an exit-ticket prompt: how did these actions shape both reputation abroad and identity at home?

Whose "national interest" counts as national? (Class 6):

  • Open Class 6 with this question before introducing the National Energy Program by name — let students sit with the question first.
  • This is a useful throughline for the rest of the course's Systems and Citizenship units, not just this unit — flag that connection for students.
  • Redirect any answer that treats this as simply "Alberta being difficult" back toward the voice-and-control framing in the misconception table above.

Extended workbook question circles (Class 8):

  • Q7–Q9 each isolate one of the unit's three hardest ideas — regional unfairness (NEP), the coexistence of international praise and internal harm, and reasonable limits on rights — using an accessible everyday analogy before asking students to apply it historically.
  • Run these as small-group discussion before independent writing; the analogies work best talked through out loud first.
  • Expect a wide range of defensible positions on all three questions — grade for reasoning quality and use of unit evidence, not for reaching a particular conclusion.

Assessment Notes

Formative check: The retrieval checks embedded at the end of each notes section, plus Workbook Part A (Q1–Q6), are the fastest formative signal — they're core, word-bank-supported, and map one-to-one onto each of the five sections, so a quick scan tells you exactly which section needs re-teaching before you move to Part B.

What a strong answer looks like: For the hardest extended question — Q8, on whether Canada's international reputation can be judged separately from harms at home — a strong answer takes a clear position and uses specific evidence from both sides of the tension (e.g., NATO/Korea/Suez on one hand, residential schools/the Sixties Scoop on the other), rather than defending its position using only one side.

Watch for: Answers that only discuss peacekeeping or only discuss residential schools, without engaging the other — this is the same one-sided pattern flagged in the Misconception Management table, just showing up in writing instead of discussion.
