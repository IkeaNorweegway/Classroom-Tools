5 ★★ Extended Rock Cycle · LO 7-5-02 · Evaluate
Calgary sits on thick layers of sedimentary rock — mostly limestone and sandstone. Kilometres below those layers lies the Canadian Shield. This is ancient Precambrian igneous and metamorphic rock. It forms the stable core of North America. Calgary is far from any active volcanoes or plate boundaries.

(a) Limestone is a sedimentary rock made mainly of calcium carbonate from marine shells and skeletons. What does the limestone under Calgary tell you about this region's past? Be specific about the environment that must have been here, and about when.

(b) The rock layers under Calgary run, top to bottom: sedimentary layers, then Precambrian igneous/metamorphic basement rock. Explain how igneous and metamorphic rock ended up below sedimentary rock here. What does this order tell you about this region's history?

(c) Rainwater turns slightly acidic as it absorbs CO2 from the air. Predict what happens when this mildly acidic water flows through limestone layers underground near Banff. What type of weathering is this? What landforms could this process create over thousands of years?

Student Claim
"Earthquakes and volcanoes can happen anywhere on Earth. I've seen news stories about eruptions in different places all over the world. So they must happen randomly, all over."

(a) Is this claim true, partly true, or false? Use evidence from plate tectonic theory to back up your answer. Name at least two specific places, and explain why each place has the geological activity it does.

(b) This claim is based on news coverage. Give one reason why news stories about geological events might mislead us. Where do earthquakes and volcanoes really happen most often?

(c) Alberta rarely has earthquakes or big volcanic activity. Use plate tectonics to explain why. What is Alberta's tectonic setting? What evidence in the Alberta landscape shows this was not always true?

8 ★ Core Fossils & Geological Time · LO 7-5-06 · Analyze
A geologist finds a fossil ammonite — a spiral-shelled marine invertebrate — in limestone on a mountainside in Banff National Park. This spot is about 2,000 metres above sea level. Ammonites lived in the ocean and went extinct about 66 million years ago.

(a) Explain how a marine fossil ends up 2,000 metres above sea level in a mountain range. Use geological processes. Your answer must name at least three processes, in the right order.

(b) The fossil is in limestone, a sedimentary rock. Why does this matter? Could you find the same ammonite fossil in a granite layer or a marble layer? Explain your thinking for each rock type.

(c) A paleontologist says this layer can work as an index fossil, to help date other rock layers worldwide. What must a fossil have to be a good index fossil? Does the ammonite qualify? Back up your answer with at least two specific properties.

9 ★★ Extended Weathering, Erosion & Deposition · LO 7-5-05 · Evaluate
The Rocky Mountains formed about 80–50 million years ago through convergent plate tectonics. At their highest, they may have stood as tall as, or taller than, today's Himalayas, which are still rising. Alberta's Rocky Mountain foothills are now much lower than the main peaks. The peaks themselves have eroded a lot from their tallest height.

(a) What geological process or processes best explain why the foothills and peaks are now much lower than before? Name at least two different processes that both helped lower the height. Explain how each is different.

(b) Say you are a geologist looking for proof that the Rockies were once much taller. What specific feature or deposit would you look for, and where would you look? Explain why this evidence would support your idea.

(c) The Himalayas are still rising, because the Indian Plate is still colliding with the Eurasian Plate. The Rockies stopped rising about 50 Ma ago, when the tectonic forces driving them stopped. What does this comparison tell you about how tectonic activity relates to mountain height over geological time?

10 ★★ Extended Plate Tectonics · LO 7-5-03 · Evaluate
Iceland sits right on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, a divergent plate boundary where the Eurasian and North American plates pull apart. Iceland has frequent, fairly gentle eruptions with runny basaltic lava. Japan sits on a convergent boundary, where the Pacific Plate subducts beneath the Eurasian Plate. Japan has large, explosive eruptions (Mt. Fuji is a composite/stratovolcano).

(a) Use the diagram from Question 4 and what you know about plate tectonic theory. Explain why the type of lava and eruption style differs between Iceland and Japan. Your answer must say where the magma comes from in each case, and why its makeup differs.

(b) Both Iceland and Japan have frequent earthquakes. Explain why. Are the causes the same in both places, or different? Be specific about what is happening to the rock in each place.

(c) Judge this claim: "All volcanoes are equally dangerous." Use the Iceland-Japan comparison to decide if this is true. What factors would a scientist look at to judge how dangerous a specific volcano is?

11 ★ Core Earthquakes & Volcanoes · LO 7-5-04 · Analyze
A magnitude 6.0 earthquake strikes with a focus 15 km deep, right beneath a city of 500,000 people. A second earthquake, magnitude 4.0, happens 1,000 km away. Its focus is 200 km deep, beneath a small town of 2,000 people. Emergency managers must decide which event needs more resources.

(a) The Richter scale is logarithmic. Each whole-number step means about 10 times more ground shaking, and about 32 times more energy released. How much more energy did the magnitude 6.0 earthquake release than the magnitude 4.0 earthquake? Show your work.

(b) Explain the difference between the Richter scale and the Modified Mercalli Intensity scale. Which scale would help more when deciding which earthquake caused more damage? Why?

(c) The magnitude 4.0 earthquake is farther away, much weaker, and deeper. But the community it hits may still need a lot of support. What factors, other than magnitude and depth, decide how damaging an earthquake is to a community? Name at least three factors. Explain how each one raises or lowers the actual damage.

Stretch Problems

Three hard questions. No hints. Use everything you know, including links across clusters. These go beyond standard grade-level work.

S1 Cross-cluster · Plate Tectonics + Rock Cycle + Alberta · Evaluate

Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift in 1912. Other scientists rejected the idea. Not because the evidence was weak — but because he could not explain a mechanism. He could not say what force was moving the continents.

Name and explain the mechanism we now use to explain plate movement. Then explain why the rock cycle and plate tectonics are closely connected. Why can't you fully explain the rock cycle without plate tectonics, and the other way around? Use at least two examples from Alberta's geology to support your answer.

S2 Cross-cluster · Earth's Interior + Plate Tectonics + Fossils · Analyze / Evaluate

Earth's magnetic field is made by convection currents in the liquid outer core. This field has flipped its north and south poles hundreds of times in geological history. Evidence of these flips is "frozen" into the ocean floor. It shows up as alternating stripes of magnetized rock on either side of mid-ocean ridges. This is called paleomagnetic striping.

Explain how paleomagnetic striping gives evidence for both sea-floor spreading and magnetic field reversals at the same time. Why is the ocean floor a better record of these flips than continental rock? What does this tell you about the age of oceanic crust compared to continental crust, and why?

S3 Cross-cluster · Full Unit · Create / Evaluate

Write a "geological biography" of Alberta. This is a summary of what this land has been through, from the Precambrian to today. Your biography must include three things. First, at least four geological eras or time periods. Second, at least three geological processes from different clusters in this unit. Third, at least two pieces of physical evidence a person could visit in Alberta today.

Write in full sentences and paragraphs, not point form. Explain it as if to a smart adult who has never studied geology. Use correct geological vocabulary throughout.
