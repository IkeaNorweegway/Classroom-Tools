Teacher Guide

Building a Modern Economy

Social Studies 9  ·  Systems  ·  Suggested pacing: 4 classes

Suggested length: 4 classes   Tier: 1   Unit colour: Green (Systems)

Guiding question: How did economic changes transform Canada into a modern economy?

Learning outcome: Students analyze how economic crisis, growth, and global trade shaped Canada's modern economy.

Big idea for students to leave with: Canada's modern economy was not inevitable — it was built through a specific sequence of crisis, deliberate government intervention, and international integration, and each of those choices had trade-offs.

What this unit is really about: Students arrive with a vague sense that "the Great Depression was bad" and "the economy grew after the war," but rarely see the causal thread connecting the two. The real work of this unit is getting students to see the Depression not as an isolated disaster but as the event that changed what people believed government was for — and to trace that new belief (that government can and should actively manage the economy) forward through public works, into postwar diversification, and finally into deliberate trade policy like NAFTA. Keep steering students back to that thread: crisis → intervention → growth → integration. Each stage created winners and losers, and the unit is strongest when students are asked to weigh those trade-offs rather than just recite the sequence of events.

Lesson Sequence

Class 1 — The Great Depression
Opening: Pre-Knowledge Check
Show Hand out the notes package and have students complete the Pre-Knowledge Check on page 1 in silence, before any instruction — "What do you know about the Great Depression?" and "Should governments spend money they don't currently have to help the economy during a crisis?"
Say "There are no wrong answers on this page — I want to know what you actually think right now, because we're going to come back to this exact page at the end of the unit and see what changed."
Notes Notes p. 1, Pre-Knowledge Check. Keep this page visible for the Unit Synthesis in Class 4.
Section 1: Causes of the Great Depression
Show Put the four causes on the board as an unlabelled list — overproduction, declining consumer demand, collapse of global trade, financial instability — and ask students to guess how they connect before you explain any of them.
Say "Factories were making more goods than people could afford to buy. When people stopped buying, businesses stopped hiring and started laying people off. Once people had even less money, they bought even less — that's the spiral. And because countries were trading heavily with each other, when one country's economy seized up, it dragged down the countries that traded with it too."
Cue "Which of these four causes do you think came first, and which were consequences of the others?" — there's no single right answer, but it forces students to see the causes as a chain, not a list.
Notes Notes p. 1, Section 1 vocabulary (overproduction, deficit spending) and Retrieval Check. Sets up Workbook Q1.
Class 2 — Government Response and Postwar Growth
Section 2: Government Responds to Crisis
Show Present the small-business scenario from Workbook Q6 (a business loses its customers, the government gives it a loan it doesn't have to repay right away, and hires unemployed workers to build a community centre) before naming any of the three policy types.
Say "Before the Depression, most governments believed the economy should basically run itself and government should stay out of it. The Depression changed that. Governments started building public infrastructure to create jobs directly, setting up welfare programs to support people who had no income, and — this is the genuinely new idea — deliberately spending more money than they collected in taxes, on purpose, to get the economy moving again."
Notes Notes p. 1, Section 2 Retrieval Check. Feeds directly into Workbook Q2 and Q6.
Section 3: A Modern, Diversified Economy
Show The sector table from the notes (automobile manufacturing, petroleum and energy, pulp and paper, hydroelectric power) — ask students which of these four they think grew fastest and why, before revealing anything.
Say "After the war, Canada's economy didn't just recover — it diversified. New industries meant new jobs, and new jobs meant people moving to where the jobs were: cities. As cities got crowded and expensive, people started moving just outside them, into new suburbs, looking for affordable housing and space."
Cue Use the notes package's "Connect back" box here — students already studied this growth and urbanization in an earlier unit from an environmental angle. Ask: "What's one social consequence of suburb growth you haven't considered before, now that we're looking at it economically?"
Notes Notes pp. 1–2, Section 3 vocabulary table and Retrieval Check (including the Stretch question). Feeds Workbook Q3–Q4.
Class 3 — Trade with the United States + Workbook Part A
Section 4: Trade with the United States
Show A map or mention of the Canada–US border, and ask students to brainstorm reasons two neighbouring countries might end up trading heavily with each other.
Say "Canada and the United States are right next door to each other, so it made economic sense for trade between them to grow over time. Agreements like NAFTA made that official — they lowered barriers so goods, services, and investment could move across the border more freely. That's what we mean by economic interdependence: each country's economy is genuinely affected by decisions the other one makes."
Notes Notes p. 2, Section 4 Retrieval Check, including the Stretch question tying the whole unit together. Sets up Workbook Q5 and Q7.
Workbook Part A — Core Questions (Q1–Q5)
Show The word bank at the top of Part A (overproduction, deficit spending, public works, urbanization, suburbs, interdependence, NAFTA) — have students self-check which terms they can already define before starting.
Cue Q1–Q3 ask students to list three items each (causes, government responses, growing sectors) — remind them these map directly onto lists already in their notes, so this should feel like retrieval, not new analysis.
Notes Workbook Q1–Q5. Good candidate for independent or partner work with notes open, since it's core-tier recall and application.
Class 4 — Workbook Part B and Synthesis
Workbook Part B — Extended Questions (Q6–Q7)
Show Reread the Q6 scenario (a business gets an emergency loan, the government hires unemployed workers) and the Q7 scenario (two interdependent countries affected by each other's tariff decisions) aloud before students write.
Say "These two questions don't have one correct answer — they want you to take a position and defend it using what we've learned. A strong answer doesn't just say 'yes' or 'no,' it weighs the trade-off: what's the benefit, and what's the real cost or risk of that benefit?"
Notes Workbook Q6–Q7, no word bank. See Assessment Notes below for what distinguishes a strong response.
Unit Synthesis and Revisiting Pre-Knowledge Check
Show Have students turn back to their Pre-Knowledge Check answers from Class 1 and read them silently before starting the synthesis paragraph.
Say "Answer the guiding question — how did economic changes transform Canada into a modern economy — in five to seven sentences, using at least three vocabulary terms from this unit. Then look back at what you wrote on day one and tell me what you'd change now."
Notes Notes p. 2, Unit Synthesis box. This is the moment to informally gauge whether the crisis → intervention → growth → integration thread actually landed.

Misconception Management

MisconceptionWhen to addressStrategy
"Deficit spending and debt are the same thing as financial mismanagement."Class 2, Section 2Distinguish crisis-driven deficit spending (a deliberate, temporary tool used in the Depression and after) from long-term structural debt problems — conflating the two makes it harder for students to evaluate Q6 fairly.
"The Great Depression was just one bad event, not a chain of connected causes" — students often list the four causes as unrelated bullet points rather than seeing overproduction feeding declining demand, feeding trade collapse, feeding financial instability.Class 1, Section 1Use the board-list activity to make students argue for a cause-and-effect order before you confirm anything — ask them to draw an arrow between two causes and explain the connection in their own words.
"Suburbs grew simply because the population grew" — students conflate population growth with the specific economic push (industrial job growth, housing affordability, desire for space) that drove suburbanization.Class 2, Section 3Ask directly: "If the population had grown but no new industries had appeared, would suburbs still have grown the same way?" Push students to name the economic driver, not just the demographic fact.
"Free trade agreements like NAFTA are either simply good or simply bad" — students default to a single verdict instead of recognizing genuine trade-offs on both sides.Class 3, Section 4 / Workbook Q7Before the debate activity, require each student to write one real benefit and one real risk of interdependence — they cannot take a side in the debate until they've named both.
"Canada's close trade relationship with the US started with NAFTA" — students sometimes treat NAFTA as the origin of the relationship rather than the formalization of a much older, geography-driven pattern.Class 3, Section 4Have students name factors that would have pushed Canada and the US toward trade long before any formal agreement existed (shared border, shared language regions, transportation networks) before introducing NAFTA by name.

Discussion and Source Activity Notes

Deficit Spending Opener Poll (Class 2):

  • Poll the class before Section 2 with: "Should a government ever spend money it doesn't have?" Take a quick show-of-hands count and record it somewhere visible.
  • Teach Section 2 as planned.
  • Re-poll the exact same question at the end of the class and compare counts — the point isn't to get everyone to agree, it's to make the shift (or lack of shift) visible.
  • Ask a few students who changed their answer to explain what specifically changed their mind; ask a few who didn't change to explain why the historical example didn't move them.

Connect Back: Urbanization Revisited (Class 2, Section 3):

  • This unit deliberately revisits the postwar growth and suburbanization content from an earlier urbanization unit — remind students explicitly before Section 3 that they've seen this material before, but from an environmental angle.
  • Use the notes package's "Connect back" box to ask students to name one social consequence of suburb growth they haven't already considered — give them 60 seconds of silent thinking before any hands go up.
  • Watch for students who default to repeating the environmental consequences from the earlier unit; redirect them toward social/economic ones (commuting costs, access to services, shifting tax bases between city and suburb).

NAFTA / Interdependence Debate (Class 3 or 4, Workbook Q7):

  • Q7 has genuinely defensible positions on both sides, which makes it well suited to a structured debate rather than silent written work alone.
  • Split the class in half: one side argues interdependence is worth the risk, the other argues it isn't. Give each side five minutes to build their case using the Q7 scenario and Section 4 content before opening the floor.
  • Require each side to acknowledge one legitimate point from the other side before they can close — this keeps the debate from collapsing into simple assertion.
  • After the debate, have students individually write their Q7 paragraph response — many will now write a stronger, more balanced answer than if they'd gone straight to writing.

Assessment Notes

Formative check: The Retrieval Checks at the end of each notes section (especially the Section 1 and Section 2 "cover your notes" checks) are the fastest formative signal — if students can't reproduce the causes list or the three government response types from memory, they aren't ready for Workbook Q1–Q2. Workbook Q1–Q5 (Part A) work well as a whole-class formative check before moving to the extended questions.

What a strong answer looks like: For Q6 and Q7, a strong answer takes a clear position but explicitly weighs the trade-off rather than only listing benefits or only listing risks — for example, on Q7, a strong answer acknowledges that interdependence creates real vulnerability (a factory closing in one country costing jobs in the other) while still making a reasoned case for or against it, using the Canada–US relationship and NAFTA as concrete evidence rather than vague generalities.

Watch for: Weak answers on Q6 and Q7 tend to restate the scenario without taking a position, or take a position without connecting it back to unit content (Depression-era strategies for Q6; interdependence/NAFTA for Q7). Also watch for the deficit-spending-equals-mismanagement misconception resurfacing directly in Q6 responses — it's the clearest sign a student needs the distinction retaught.

Using the Workbook

This unit connects directly to the urbanization unit (Section 3 here revisits postwar growth and suburbs from an economic angle) — consider explicitly reminding students of that unit before Q3–Q4.
