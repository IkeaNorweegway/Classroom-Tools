Creating Canada's Government

Grade 7 Social Studies  ·  Practice Workbook — Answer Key  ·  Alberta Draft Curriculum 2026

Teacher Copy — Not for Students

Learning Outcome: Students examine how the Quebec Conference, London Conference, and the British North America Act (1867) led to the creation of Canada.

Guiding Question: How was Canada's system of government created at Confederation?

Model answers and marking notes for every question. Each card says which notes section must be taught before the question opens. The student copy shows no section labels: announce which questions are open each session.

For opinion and judgement questions, the model answer is one strong example. Accept any position backed by accurate evidence from the unit.

Part A — Core and Extended Questions

Q1★ Core
Opens after Section 3 — The three branches
AnswerPM and Cabinet: executive, carries out laws · Parliament: legislative, makes laws · Courts: judicial, interprets laws.
Q2★ Core
Opens after Section 1 — The road to Confederation
Answer1 Great Coalition (1864) · 2 Charlottetown (Sept. 1864): agree to discuss union · 3 Quebec (Oct. 1864): 72 Resolutions · 4 London (1866–67): finish the plan with Britain · 5 BNA Act (July 1, 1867).
Q3★ Core
Opens after Section 4 — Inside Parliament
Answera) Party A's leader: Party A has the most seats.
b) Only voters in the leader's own riding. Everyone else voted for a local candidate in their own riding.
Q4★★ Extended
Opens after Sections 3 and 4 — Branches and Parliament
AnswerAdvantage: the government can usually get its laws passed, and it answers to MPs every day in the House. Risk: a government with a majority can pass laws with little check from other branches, since its own MPs vote for them.
Q5★ Core
Opens after Section 2 — The ideas behind the plan
Answera) representation by population · b) responsible government · c) representative government
Q6★ Core
Opens after Section 5 — Federalism
AnswerFederal: the army, printing money, criminal law, citizenship. Provincial: schools, hospitals, city governments, oil and gas in the ground (natural resources).
Q7★ Core
Opens after Section 6 — The courts
AnswerTrial court → Alberta Court of Appeal → Supreme Court of Canada (if it agrees to hear the case), which gives the final answer.
Q8★★ Extended
Opens after Section 3 — Why three branches?
AnswerDisagree: faster, but one person with all the power could pass unfair laws, enforce them unfairly and judge their own case. Three branches mean each job is done by a different part of government, which protects citizens.
Q9★★ Extended
Opens after Section 1 — Deadlock
AnswerCanada West and Canada East had the same number of seats and disagreed, so governments kept falling: a deadlock. In 1864 Macdonald, Cartier and Brown formed the Great Coalition to find a new system. That led to the conferences, and at the Quebec Conference they wrote the 72 Resolutions, the plan for Canada's government.
Q10★★ Extended
Opens after Section 4 — Reading a ballot
AnswerThe ballot only lists the candidates in this riding; Canadians vote for an MP, not the PM. The voter's choice helps decide which party wins this seat, and the party with the most seats across Canada forms government; its leader becomes PM.
Q11★★ Extended
Opens after Section 5 — Sharing power
AnswerBenefit: provinces make decisions that fit their people, e.g. Quebec runs French-language schools; Alberta manages its own oil and gas. Challenge: levels disagree or overlap, e.g. a pipeline crossing provinces, or health care paid for partly by Ottawa but run by provinces, so decisions take longer.
Q12★★ Extended
Opens after Section 2 — Rep by pop and the Senate
AnswerThe Senate gave each region 24 seats (Ontario, Quebec, the Maritimes), so the smaller Maritime colonies had an equal voice in the upper house, which balanced rep by pop in the House of Commons.
Q13★★ Extended
Opens after Section 6 — One top court
AnswerDisagree: if each province had the final say, the same law could mean different things in different provinces, and someone could be guilty in one and innocent in another. A general court of appeal, the Supreme Court of Canada, keeps the law consistent across the country.

Part B — Challenge Zone

QS1★★ Extended
Opens after Sections 2 and 4
AnswerCitizens elect MPs; the party with the most seats forms government and its leader becomes PM. If the government loses the support of those elected MPs, it must resign or call an election, so the people's representatives can remove a government that stops listening.
QS2★★ Extended
Opens after Sections 1, 5 and 6
AnswerThe BNA Act was a British law passed in London; Canada's top court was only created in 1875; until 1949 final appeals still went to the Privy Council in London. Canada gained control in steps, not all at once.
QS3★★ Extended
Opens after All sections
AnswerStrong answers trace the deadlock and the Great Coalition, the three conferences (Quebec's 72 Resolutions), the BNA Act (July 1, 1867), the three branches and their jobs, and the division of powers, using at least one of the democratic ideas.
