What Is an Ideology?

An ideology is a set of ideas about how society and government should work. It answers big questions like: Who should make decisions? How much should government control? What matters most — the individual person, or the group as a whole?

An ideology is   (an idea) — it is not a building, not a person, and not a group you can join with a membership card.

Misconception

"An ideology and a political party are the same thing."

A political party is an organization. People join it, it has a name, and it runs candidates in elections. An ideology is just an idea — it can exist even if no party is built around it yet, and the same ideology can show up inside more than one party, in more than one country. Parties also change their platforms over time, but the underlying ideology can stay the same idea for centuries.

Correct understanding: The ideology is the set of beliefs. The political party is the group of people who organize around that belief and try to turn it into law by getting elected.

The Individual or the Group?

Now let's look at one of the biggest questions every ideology has to answer: does the individual person matter most, or does the group matter most?

Individualism is the idea that each person's own choices, freedom, and worth matter most. A person who leans individualist believes people should mostly take care of   and make their own decisions with little interference.

Collectivism is the idea that the needs and goals of the   matter more than any one person's wants. A person who leans collectivist believes people should work together and sometimes put the group's needs ahead of their own.

Long before words like "communism" or "conservatism" existed, people were already arguing about the same basic question: who should be in charge, and why? Two ancient Greek philosophers gave very different answers.

Plato believed the best leaders are the wisest ones. In his ideal city, trained philosopher-rulers — people who had studied for years to understand what is truly good — would make decisions for everyone else. Plato's answer: leadership through  .

Aristotle, Plato's own student, disagreed. He argued that laws — not individual rulers, no matter how wise — should hold the real power. A ruler who answers to no law can become unfair or make mistakes; a system built on shared, known laws protects everyone, including from the ruler. Aristotle's answer: leadership through  .

The Same Argument, in Real Athens

At the same time, in the real city of ancient Athens, a similar argument was playing out — not about philosopher-kings, but about who should get a political voice at all.

Athenian aristocrats believed political power belonged with a small, well-born, wealthy class — the people who had always held it. They valued   and the existing social order.

Democratic reformers (such as Solon and Cleisthenes) pushed to expand political rights to more citizens over time — breaking some of the aristocrats' traditional advantages and letting more ordinary citizens take part in decisions.

Imagine a straight line. On one end, government makes most of the big decisions for everyone. On the other end, people mostly make their own decisions with little government involvement. This line is called the political spectrum.

The left side of the spectrum tends toward   — more group decision-making, more government involvement. The right side tends toward   — more individual choice, less government involvement. People and parties in the middle are called   or centrist.

Misconception

"Left and right mean the exact same thing everywhere, for all time."

A political position that counts as far-left in one country can look moderate — or even right of center — in another country. A party that was considered radical 100 years ago might hold ideas most people now consider mainstream. The spectrum is not a fixed ruler with permanent markings — the whole line can shift depending on the country and the era.

Correct understanding: Left and right are relative positions, not fixed universal labels. Always ask: left or right compared to what, and when?

Example: A government policy that provides free healthcare for everyone is considered a mainstream, centrist idea in Canada. In another country, the same policy might be labeled "far-left." Same policy — different position on the spectrum, depending on where you are.

Section 4  ·  Four Ideologies

Learning target: I can describe communism, socialism, liberalism, and conservatism, and connect each one to individualism or collectivism.

Vocabulary

Communism

Liberalism  
Conservatism  
Means of production  

The political spectrum you just learned is a line. These four ideologies are specific points that can be placed along that line.

Misconception

"Communism and socialism are just two words for the same thing."

They share an idea — that the community, not just private owners, should have a say in the means of production (the factories, land, and tools used to make and grow things). But they are not the same. Socialism means the means of production are owned or regulated by the community, often alongside an active government and existing social classes. Communism goes further: it is the goal of a classless, stateless society with common ownership of everything — no government at all, no separate social classes. Many socialist countries exist today with elected governments; a fully realized communist society, by its own definition, would have no state left to run it.

Correct understanding: Socialism and communism overlap on community ownership, but they are different points on the spectrum with different end goals.

Section 5  ·  Ideology Taken to Extremes

Learning target: I can explain what totalitarianism is, describe how Nazi ideology led to the Holocaust, and reflect on Holocaust victim accounts using primary sources.

Vocabulary

TermDefinition in your own wordsOne example
Totalitarian  
Dictator

The Holocaust  

Every ideology you learned in Section 4 sits somewhere on the political spectrum. But what happens when the collectivist end of that spectrum is pushed to its absolute extreme — total government control, with no limits at all?

Misconception

"Totalitarianism only happened once, in Germany, in the 1930s and 40s — it's just a historical event, not something that can happen again."

Totalitarianism is a category of government, not a single historical event. It means a government holds complete control over political, social, and economic life, crushes any opposition, and uses propaganda to control what people think. Nazi Germany is the historical example you will study in depth — but the pattern it followed (one leader or party seizing total control, removing rights, punishing dissent) is a pattern that historians and political scientists watch for as something that can happen in any era, in any country, if the conditions allow it.

Correct understanding: Totalitarianism is a recurring category, not a closed chapter of history. Nazi Germany is our historical case study of it — not the only possible case.

What Totalitarian Control Looks Like

  • One leader or party holds   political power
  • Opposition and free press are shut down or controlled
  • The government uses   to control what citizens believe
  • Ordinary life — schools, media, even families — comes under state influence

The Historical Case: Nazi Germany

In 1933, Adolf Hitler and the Nazi Party gained complete political control of Germany. They banned other political parties, controlled the media with propaganda, and used the state's full power to remove the rights of anyone the regime targeted.

This total control led directly to the Holocaust: the systematic persecution and mass murder of six million Jewish people, along with millions of others — including Roma people, disabled people, political prisoners, and other targeted groups — carried out by the Nazi state between 1933 and 1945.

This is a heavy piece of history. It is important to understand it clearly and honestly: this was not random violence. It was organized by a government that had already gained total, unchecked control over the country — which is exactly why totalitarianism, as a category, is something every generation needs to be able to recognize.
Primary source reflection. Your teacher will share a Holocaust survivor's account (for example, from a verified archive such as the Anne Frank House, the USC Shoah Foundation, or Yad Vashem). After you read or watch it, answer:

1. What is one specific detail from this account that stayed with you?

2. How does this account connect to what you learned about total government control in this section?

3. Why is reading a real person's account different from reading only numbers and dates in a textbook?

Connection Prompt
