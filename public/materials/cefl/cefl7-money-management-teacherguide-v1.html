Saving for Something Real

7 sessions × 60 minutes · project-based · Outcome 7-MM · Alberta draft curriculum (2026)

At a glance

Outcome7-MM: Students explain how spending, saving, and budgeting decisions affect financial security.
UnderstandingIndividuals aspire to achieve financial security.
Driving questionHow can I reach a money goal without losing control of my spending?
Student materialsMini-workbook `cefl7-money-management-workbook-v1` · project brief `cefl7-money-management-project-v1`
Teams4–5 students. Each student makes their own budget; the team checks math and gives feedback.
AudienceA family member or trusted adult at home (family feedback card), then a short class share in Session 7
AssessmentSingle-point rubric ("How you will be marked") on the report-card scale (4 Excellent · 3 Proficient · 2 Meets · 1 Has yet to meet), scored on Steps 1–4 and the final product. No unit test.

Teacher note

Do this before Session 1

  • Make surprise cards for Step 4 (about 15, mixed). Bad surprises: cracked phone screen $180; lost bus pass $45; friend's birthday gift $25; bike tire $35; school trip fee $40; replacing lost earbuds $30. Good surprises: extra yard job +$40; birthday money +$50; sold an old game +$20. Scale amounts to the income card: $180 is a big hit on card A.
  • Price sources for Step 1: flyers, store websites, or a printed list of common goals with real prices (bike, headphones, a game console, a concert ticket, a gift for family).
  • Note home before Step 7 explaining the family feedback card. Offer another trusted adult (coach, school staff) for students who need it.

Why it is built this way

  • Driving question first. The Maya and Jake hook (same money, different results) creates the need to know what a budget does.
  • Practise the behaviour. Financial education changes knowledge more than behaviour, so students rehearse the real task: a working budget toward a real goal, tested with a surprise.
  • Privacy by design. Income cards mean no student has to reveal their own or their family's money. Students who want to use real income can.
  • Real audience at home. Explaining a budget to a family member makes the plan real and brings the conversation home.

Look for

Goals have a real price and a source, not a guess. Gently steer very large goals (a car at 12) toward something reachable in months, or let students treat it as a long-term goal.

TimeWhat happens
0–5Warm-up: (1) What is a trade-off? (2) Ava spends $45 of $60; how much is left? (3) Need or want: winter boots?

Watch out

"A budget means no fun." Moment 2, Question 3 targets this. Point out that Ava's new budget still has snacks, games and gifts. Come back to it when students build their budgets: every budget should have some money for wants.

Session 3 — Moment 3 and the budget

TimeWhat happens
0–5Warm-up: (1) What is a budget? (2) $360 ÷ $60 = ? (3) Name one need and one want.

Look for

Budget totals equal income; savings is a line in the budget (not "whatever is left"); months = price ÷ savings, rounded up.

Teacher note

Timing: students share at home between Sessions 6 and 7. If cards don't come back, students can share with a staff member during Session 7.

Sample products (show all three in Session 5)

  1. Poster with a budget chart. A bar like Ava's, the goal picture with its price, and a "months to go" countdown.
  2. Savings tracker. A thermometer to colour in each month, with the surprise marked as a dip and a note on how the plan recovered.
  3. Comic, "Saving for a bike." Six panels: the goal, the budget, the trade-off, the surprise, the fix, and reaching the goal.

When a student gets stuck

What you seeWhat to do
Budget doesn't add upHave the Checker add each line out loud with them.
No savings lineAsk: "Where does the money for your goal come from?"
Goal takes 3 yearsShow how to change the budget, choose a cheaper version, or call it long-term.
Surprise wipes out savingsThat's the point. Talk about an emergency fund line.
Upset about money at homeUse income cards; never require real family money. Talk privately.

Access supports (UDL)

  • Math: calculators for everyone. Students can use card A (round numbers) for simpler math.
  • Reading: read scenarios and cards aloud.
  • Expression: poster, tracker, video, comic, or slideshow.
  • Sensitivity: students come from very different money situations. Income cards, sample prices, and "trusted adult" options make sure no one has to share personal finances.

Background for the teacher

  • Card C and Ty's pay: $13/hour (2026 Alberta student minimum wage), 20 hours every two weeks = $260 gross − EI 1.63% ($4.24) = $255.76 a pay; two pays ≈ $511 a month. Under-18s pay no CPP, and this income is below the tax-free amount. Ty in the workbook works twice as many hours ($511.52 every two weeks).
  • Research note: financial education raises knowledge more than behaviour (about 0.2 vs. 0.1 standard deviations in a 76-study meta-analysis), and is most useful close to a real decision. Building a real budget for a real goal is the closest a classroom gets.
