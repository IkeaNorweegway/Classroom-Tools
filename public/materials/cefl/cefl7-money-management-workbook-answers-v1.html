Before you start

Think about the last thing you bought or wanted to buy. Was it a need or a want? How did you decide?

Moment 1Every dollar is a choice

The situation

Ava is 13. She earned $60 babysitting. Her favourite band is playing in town, and a ticket costs $45. She is also saving for a $360 bike.

Worked example

What is Ava's trade-off if she buys the ticket?

  1. A trade-off means choosing one thing means giving up another.
  2. If she buys the ticket, she has $60 − $45 = $15 left to save.
  3. She gives up $45 toward her bike. The bike will take longer to reach.
  4. Neither choice is wrong. A good decision means knowing what you give up and choosing on purpose.

1. Leo has $20. He can buy snacks for the week or put the money toward new headphones. What is the trade-off if he buys snacks?

Answer

If Leo buys snacks, he gives up $20 toward the headphones, so they take longer to buy.

2. Name a trade-off you or your family made with money recently. What was given up?

Answer

Personal. Look for a real choice and the thing given up.

3. Should Ava buy the concert ticket? Give your answer, a reason, and what she gives up.

Answer

Either answer is fine with a reason and the cost named. For example: "Yes. The concert only happens once, and she can still save $15. She gives up $45 toward the bike, so it will take longer."

Extra practice · optional

E1. Ava buys the ticket and saves the other $15. How much more does she need for the $360 bike if she had $120 saved before?

E2. Why is it a trade-off even when you save money instead of spending it?

Answer

E1. $120 + $15 = $135 saved. $360 − $135 = $225 more needed.

E2. Saving means giving up what you could buy now, such as the concert, to get something later.

Moment 2A plan for your money

The situation

Ava wants a plan, so she makes a budget: a written plan for how she will use her money each month. She earns $90 babysitting (3 times × $30) and gets $40 allowance. That's $130 a month.

Worked example

Ava wants the bike sooner. How can she change her budget?

  1. Check the plan. Right now she saves $60 a month, so the bike takes 360 ÷ 60 = 6 months.
  2. Find spending to change. She lowers snacks from $30 to $20 and games from $25 to $15. That frees up $20.
  3. New savings: $60 + $20 = $80 a month.
  4. New time: 360 ÷ 80 = 4.5, so about 5 months. She still has money for snacks, games, and gifts.

Connects to Math: dividing to find how many months

1. Leo earns $100 a month mowing lawns. He spends $40 on snacks, $35 on gaming, and $25 on movies. He wants $150 headphones.
(a) How much is Leo saving now?
(b) Change his budget so he can buy the headphones in 5 months. Show your new budget.

Answer

(a) $100 − $40 − $35 − $25 = $0. Leo isn't saving anything.
(b) He needs $150 ÷ 5 = $30 a month. Any new plan where the spending lines add to $70 and savings is $30. For example: snacks $25, gaming $25, movies $20, savings $30.

2. Is Leo's gaming a need or a want? Is food for his family a need or a want? Explain the difference.

Answer

Gaming is a want: nice to have, but he can live without it. Food is a need: something you must have to live. Needs come first in a budget.

Watch out

3. Leo says: "A budget means you can't have any fun." Use Ava's new budget to explain why that isn't true.

Extra practice · optional

E1. Ava gets $20 extra for her birthday. If she saves all of it, how does that change her timeline?

E2. Make a one-month budget for $50 of your own (real or pretend). Include savings.

Answer

E1. She needs $360 − $20 = $340. At $80 a month: 340 ÷ 80 = 4.25, so a little over 4 months (about a week sooner than before).

E2. Any budget that adds up to $50 and includes a savings line.

Moment 3Setting a money goal

The situation

Ty is 17. He earns $511.52 every two weeks at a part-time job, about $1,023 a month. He has two goals: a $120 concert ticket in 2 months, and a $4,000 used car.

Worked example

How long will Ty take to save $4,000 for the car if he saves $250 a month?

  1. A good goal says what, how much, and by when.
  2. Divide the goal by the monthly savings: 4,000 ÷ 250 = 16 months.
  3. Short-term goals take weeks or months (the concert). Long-term goals take a year or more (the car, school after Grade 12, retirement).
  4. Ty can save for both at once: $60 a month for 2 months for the concert, and $250 a month for the car.

1. Write short-term or long-term.
(a) A $40 video game next month
(b) Saving for college
(c) Saving for retirement

Answer

(a) short-term (b) long-term (c) long-term

2. Ty wants the car in 12 months instead of 16. How much must he save each month? Is that realistic on about $1,023 a month? Explain.

Answer

4,000 ÷ 12 = $333.33 a month. That's about a third of his $1,023. It could be realistic if his needs are covered (for example, he lives at home), but it leaves less for other spending and for surprises. Accept a reasoned yes or no.

3. Write your own money goal. Say what, how much, and by when. Then work out how much you'd save each week.

Answer

A goal with what, how much, and by when; the weekly amount = cost ÷ number of weeks.

Extra practice · optional

E1. Ty saves $250 for the car and $60 for the concert each month. How much of his $1,023 is left for everything else?

E2. Why might it help to write a goal down instead of keeping it in your head?

Answer

E1. $1,023 − $250 − $60 = $713 (or $713.04 using $1,023.04).

E2. For example: you remember it, you can track progress, and it's easier to say no to spending that doesn't fit.

Moment 4Ready for surprises

The situation

Ty drops his phone, and the screen cracks. Fixing it costs $180. What happens next depends on one thing: does Ty have an emergency fund (money saved for surprises)?

Worked example

Which path gives Ty more financial security, and why?

  1. Financial security means having enough money for your needs and for surprises, and being able to plan ahead.
  2. On Path A, the surprise is paid for, and his car goal is safe.
  3. On Path B, he either delays his goal or goes into debt (owes money). Borrowing often costs extra.
  4. So Path A gives more financial security. An emergency fund protects your other goals.

1. Ty decides to build a $300 emergency fund by saving $40 a month. How many months will it take?

Answer

300 ÷ 40 = 7.5, so 8 months (after 7 months he'd have $280).

2. Write one poor money decision and one effective money decision a teenager might make. Say what could happen after each.

Answer

Personal. For example: poor: spending all your pay the day you get it, so you can't cover a surprise or reach a goal. Effective: saving a set amount from every pay first, so your goal and emergency fund grow.

3. Why does having savings make people feel less stressed? Give a reason.

Answer

For example: they know they can handle a surprise without borrowing or giving up their goals, so they worry less.

Extra practice · optional

E1. Ty used $180 of his $300 fund. How much is left? How many months at $40 to refill it?

E2. Name two other surprises that could cost a teenager money.

Answer

E1. $300 − $180 = $120 left. To refill $180 at $40 a month: 180 ÷ 40 = 4.5, so 5 months.

E2. For example: a lost bus pass, a broken bike, a friend's birthday gift, a school trip fee, replacing lost earbuds.
