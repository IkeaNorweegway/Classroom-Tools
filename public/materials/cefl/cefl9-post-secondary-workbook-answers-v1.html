Moment 1Student loans

The situation

Maya plans a 4-year nursing degree. She'll need to borrow $20,000 in total. In Alberta, a student loan comes in two parts: a federal part (from the Government of Canada) and a provincial part (from Alberta). The federal part is interest-free. The Alberta part charges interest at the prime rate.

Worked example

How much does Maya repay in total?

  1. Federal part: $12,000, no interest. She repays exactly $12,000.
  2. Alberta part: $8,000 at 4.5% over 10 years. Monthly payment $82.91 (from a loan calculator). Total: 82.91 × 120 = $9,949.29, so $1,949.29 is interest.
  3. Total repaid: $12,000 + $9,949.29 = $21,949.29.
  4. Compare: if the whole $20,000 were charged 4.5%, she'd pay $4,873.22 in interest. The interest-free federal part saves her almost $3,000.

Connects to Math: multiplying and comparing money; percent

1. What is the difference between a loan and a grant?

Answer

A loan must be paid back (sometimes with interest). A grant does not have to be paid back.

2. Maya's monthly payment is $182.91 for 120 months. Check the total she repays.

Answer

182.91 × 120 = $21,949.20 (matches $21,949.29 apart from rounding of the monthly payment).

3. Should a student borrow as little as possible, even if it means working more hours during school? Give a reason on each side.

Answer

Look for a reason on each side. Borrow less: less to repay and less interest; less stress after graduating. Work less, borrow more: more time to study and better grades; the federal part is interest-free. Accept a reasoned conclusion.

Extra practice · optional

E1. If Maya repays her Alberta part faster, over 5 years, will she pay more or less interest? Why?

E2. What is a grace period? Why do you think loans have one?

Answer

E1. Less interest. The balance shrinks faster, so there is less time for interest to build. (Payments are bigger each month.)

E2. A period after school ends when you don't have to make payments yet. It gives graduates time to find a job.

Moment 2Return on investment

The situation

Return on investment (ROI) compares what a pathway costs with what it gives back over time. One simple way: how many years of higher pay does it take to "pay back" the true cost? For comparison, full-time work at Alberta's $15 minimum wage earns about $31,200 a year.

Worked example

How long does a registered nursing degree take to pay back?

  1. True cost: tuition about 4 × $7,293.60 = $29,174.40, plus lost earnings 4 × (8 months × $2,400) = $76,800. Total: $105,974.40.
  2. Extra pay each year: average RN salary $73,823 − $31,200 = $42,623 more than minimum wage.
  3. Payback: $105,974.40 ÷ $42,623 ≈ 2.5 years.
  4. But: ROI also depends on graduating, finding work in the field, and regional conditions (Alberta may be short of nurses, which helps).

1. A vet tech diploma costs $6,000 a year in tuition (sample) for 2 years, plus 2 years of lost earnings ($19,200 a year). What is the true cost?

Answer

Tuition 2 × $6,000 = $12,000. Lost earnings 2 × $19,200 = $38,400. True cost $50,400.

2. Use the average vet tech salary ($47,812) and the minimum-wage baseline ($31,200). About how many years does it take to pay back your answer to Question 1?

Answer

Extra pay: $47,812 − $31,200 = $16,612 a year. $50,400 ÷ $16,612 ≈ 3 years.

3. Name two things that could make a pathway's real ROI worse than the estimate.

Answer

Any two: not graduating; not finding a job in the field; lower pay where you live or when you finish; taking longer than planned; more borrowing and interest; the labour market changing.

Extra practice · optional

E1. Why does the electrician apprenticeship pay back so quickly?

E2. Is ROI the only thing that matters when choosing a pathway? Explain.

Answer

E1. Apprentices earn wages while they train, so they lose little income, and tuition is low. The true cost is small compared with the pay.

E2. No. Interests, values, job satisfaction, location, and the kind of work also matter. A pathway with a slower payback can still be the right choice.

Moment 3Learning while you earn

The situation

Work-integrated learning mixes school with real work: co-op programs, internships and apprenticeships. In a co-op, students take turns between school terms and paid work terms. It builds human capital and lowers the cost of school.

Worked example

How does a co-op change the true cost of a degree?

  1. Without co-op: true cost includes about $76,800 in lost earnings over 4 years.
  2. With co-op: three paid terms earn about $38,400 (sample wage).
  3. Effect: that money pays tuition and living costs, so Maya borrows less.
  4. Bonus: she graduates with experience and references, which helps her get hired.

1. Name the three kinds of work-integrated learning from the scenario.

Answer

Co-op programs, internships, apprenticeships.

2. Give two benefits of a co-op besides money.

Answer

Any two: real work experience; references; networking; trying out a career before graduating; a better chance of being hired after.

3. A nurse trained in Alberta moves to another province. Why might she need to check if her credential is recognized there?

Answer

Each province (and country) can set its own rules for some professions. Her Alberta credential may need to be checked, or extra steps completed, before she can work there.

Extra practice · optional

E1. A 30-year-old wants to change careers and go back to school. How might their path be different from a recent high school graduate's?

E2. What is one downside of a co-op program?

Answer

E1. For example: they may need to upgrade high school courses, study part-time while working, use mature-student admission routes, or get credit for work experience.

E2. For example: the program can take longer to finish, work terms may be in another city, or placements aren't always guaranteed.

Moment 4Getting in, and having a Plan B

The situation

Competitive programs often look at more than grades. Some ask for a portfolio (art, design), an interview, references, or extracurricular experience. Because seats are limited, a smart plan has a primary pathway, a contingency (backup), and a rationale (reasons).

Worked example

Why is Maya's plan strong?

  1. Primary pathway: RN degree, matching her goal of working in health care.
  2. Strengthen the application: volunteering at a care home shows commitment and gives her references.
  3. Contingency: a practical nursing diploma keeps her in health care if she doesn't get a seat, and she can bridge to RN later.
  4. Rationale: both pathways fit her interests, Alberta needs nurses, and the contingency costs less.

1. Name two things besides grades that competitive programs might look at.

Answer

Any two: portfolio, interview, references, extracurricular or volunteer experience, a personal statement.

2. Why should a contingency usually be related to the primary pathway?

Answer

A related contingency still moves you toward your goal (same field, shared skills), and sometimes lets you bridge to the first choice later.

Watch out

3. A student says: "If I don't get into my first-choice program, my plan has failed." Use Maya's plan to respond.

Extra practice · optional

E1. Name one way you could strengthen an application while still in high school.

E2. Write a one-sentence rationale for a pathway you're curious about.

Answer

E1. For example: volunteer in the field, take related CTS courses, job shadow, keep marks up in required courses, build a portfolio.

E2. One clear sentence linking the pathway to interests, cost or the labour market.
