Grow It

7 sessions × 60 minutes · project-based · Outcome 8-MM · Alberta draft curriculum (2026)

At a glance

Outcome8-MM: Students investigate strategies to achieve financial security by analyzing how costs, interest, and savings help manage personal finances.
UnderstandingsFinancial security depends on managing fixed and variable costs so money is available for saving and investing. Interest works both for and against individuals.
Driving questionHow can I make my money grow without losing control of my costs?
Student materialsMini-workbook `cefl8-money-management-workbook-v1` · project brief `cefl8-money-management-project-v1`
Teams4–5. Each student makes their own plan; teams check math and give feedback.
AudienceA Money Fair (families, staff, other classes), Session 7
GameGrow It (CEFL Town), on the unit page, optional: one chapter after each of Sessions 1–4. See "The Grow It game" below.
AssessmentSingle-point rubric on the report-card scale. No unit test.

Teacher note

Before Session 1: book a space and invite visitors for the Money Fair (Session 7). Check that a couple of bank or credit-union rate pages load for Step 2. Calculators (or a spreadsheet) for everyone.

Hook answer (reveal in Session 3): at 6% compounded yearly, Ana's $1,000 grows for 50 years to about $18,420; Ben's grows for 40 years to about $10,286. Ana has about $8,134 more from the same $1,000, just by starting 10 years earlier.

Income cards: A $180 (age 14); B $511 (age 16, 10 h/week at $13, after EI); C $767 (age 17, 15 h/week at $13, after EI). 2026 figures.

Why it is built this way

  • Driving question first. The Ana and Ben hook creates a real need to understand compound interest.
  • Practise the decision. Students build a budget and choose where money goes for two different timelines. Financial education changes behaviour most when it rehearses a real decision.
  • Real rates. Students look up today's rate, so the numbers aren't pseudo-context.
  • Privacy. Income cards mean no one shares real family money.

Watch out

"Simple and compound interest are about the same." Moment 3, Question 3. True over 2–3 years, very false over 40. Use the hook reveal.

Watch out

"Investing is just saving with a better rate." Moment 4. Investments can lose value; the right choice depends on when you need the money.

Teacher note

Not financial advice. Keep the investment discussion general (savings accounts, GICs, funds). Don't recommend specific products or companies.

The Grow It game (CEFL Town)

A companion game on the unit page, one mission in CEFL Town, the top-down town every CEFL unit shares. Each student makes a character once, or keeps the one from Grade 7. Sam at the bank gives them one of the project's income cards (A age 14, B age 16, C age 17). They build a budget that funds a short-term goal, then decide where two pots of money go.

  • When: sessions are full, so set each chapter as homework, early-finisher work, or a device day before the next session. Chapters play in order. Chapter length has not been timed with students yet.
  • Saving: the game saves in that browser, and the character carries over to every CEFL Town mission. To move to another computer, use Menu → Take your town home (a save code or a file).
  • Controls: arrow keys or tap to walk, Enter or tap to talk. Tapping the gold goal line at the top walks the student there, which suits students who find moving around hard.
  • The side story: The Sure Thing Fund promises "50% a year, no risk." Students compare it with real interest, find its compound table is wrong, and spot the flyer's red flags. At the Money Fair, Dev gets his money back, and the bank's red-flag card is named after the student. The story never changes the goal.
  • The goal: both goals reached on time. The safe choice (a savings account for Pot 1, the GIC for Pot 2) always wins; the investment fund for Pot 2 usually wins; a savings account for Pot 2 never does; the fund for Pot 1 can lose after a bad first year. That is the lesson: the sooner you need the money, the safer it should be. The Interest Sprint game never changes the goal.
  • Gentle and Real (Menu): Gentle gives a hint after a wrong try and shows the answer after 3 tries; Real gives no hints and shows it after 2. Gentle checks the budget as students build it; Real hides the check until they save.
  • Between lessons (optional): after a chapter, students can choose "Not yet: help around town." Townspeople then ask for help with their own version of what the chapter taught, for a little play money in a wallet that never touches the mission, and one Town Mail letter arrives each day.
  • Report (Menu → Report for my teacher): first-try answers by chapter, what the student wrote and chose, game results, how far they got in the side story, and a Between lessons table. It prints. First-try answers make a quick look-for before each step's checkpoint.
  • Simplifications to tell students if asked: the rates are samples (savings 2%, a 5-year GIC 3.5%, a fund with yearly returns from −15% to +20%); interest is compounded yearly; Pot 1 earns interest on about half its money for the year; no fees or taxes on interest.

Sample products (show all three in Session 5)

  1. Poster with a growth graph. Budget pie on the left; a hand-drawn graph of simple vs. compound growth on the right; two "goal jars" labelled with where each goal's money goes.
  2. Spreadsheet with a chart. A budget table, a 10-year compound column using a formula, and a line chart.
  3. "My money plan" booklet. Four small pages: budget, simple, compound, and where it goes.

Access supports (UDL)

  • Calculators or spreadsheets; a formula card (I = P × r × t; value × (1 + r) each year).
  • Product choice, including a spreadsheet for students who like working digitally.
  • Income cards and sample costs protect privacy.

Background for the teacher

  • Rates in the workbook (2%, 3%, 3.5%, 4%, 5%, 6%) are samples. Real savings and GIC rates change with the Bank of Canada policy rate.
  • The Grade 9 unit covers the other side of interest: credit cards, loans and compound interest on debt.
