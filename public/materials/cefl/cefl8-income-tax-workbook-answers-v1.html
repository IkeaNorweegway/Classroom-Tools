Moment 1Tax brackets

The situation

Canada taxes income in brackets: slices of income taxed at different rates. In 2026, the federal government taxes the first $58,523 of income at 14%, and the next slice (up to $117,045) at 20.5%. Then everyone gets a credit for the tax-free basic personal amount.

Worked example

How much federal tax does someone earning $70,000 pay?

  1. First bracket: the first $58,523 is taxed at 14%: 0.14 × 58,523 = $8,193.22.
  2. Second bracket: the rest is 70,000 − 58,523 = $11,477, taxed at 20.5%: 0.205 × 11,477 = $2,352.79.
  3. Bracket tax: $8,193.22 + $2,352.79 = $10,546.01.
  4. Subtract the credit: everyone gets 0.14 × $16,452 = $2,303.28 off. Federal tax = $8,242.73.

Connects to Math: percent of a number, and working in steps

1. Find the federal tax on $50,000 (all in the first bracket). Don't forget the credit.

Answer

0.14 × 50,000 = $7,000. $7,000 − $2,303.28 = $4,696.72.

2. Find the federal tax on $90,000. Show both brackets and the credit.

Answer

First bracket: 0.14 × 58,523 = $8,193.22. Second bracket: 90,000 − 58,523 = 31,477; 0.205 × 31,477 = $6,452.79. Bracket tax: $14,646.01. Minus credit $2,303.28 = $12,342.73.

Watch out

3. Mo gets a raise from $58,000 to $60,000. Mo says: "Now I'm in the 20.5% bracket, so all my income is taxed at 20.5%. The raise will cost me money!" Explain what's wrong. (Hint: only part of the raise is above the line.)

Extra practice · optional

E1. Alberta taxes the first $61,200 at 8%, the next slice at 10%, and gives a credit of $1,821.52. Find Alberta tax on $70,000.

E2. Add federal and Alberta tax on $70,000. What percent of the $70,000 is that? (This is the average tax rate.)

Answer

E1. 0.08 × 61,200 = $4,896. 70,000 − 61,200 = 8,800; 0.10 × 8,800 = $880. Bracket tax $5,776 − $1,821.52 = $3,954.48.

E2. $8,242.73 + $3,954.48 = $12,197.21. 12,197.21 ÷ 70,000 ≈ 17.4% average tax rate (the top bracket rate is 20.5% federal, but the average is much lower).

Moment 2Three ways to tax

The situation

Governments can share the cost of public services in different ways. A progressive tax takes a bigger share from people who earn more. A flat tax takes the same share from everyone. A regressive tax ends up taking a bigger share from people who earn less.

Worked example

Why is GST (a 5% sales tax) regressive, even though everyone pays the same 5%?

  1. Sam earns $20,000 and spends $15,000 on taxable things. GST: 0.05 × 15,000 = $750.
  2. Taylor earns $200,000 and spends $60,000 on taxable things. GST: 0.05 × 60,000 = $3,000.
  3. Share of income: Sam pays 750 ÷ 20,000 = 3.75%. Taylor pays 3,000 ÷ 200,000 = 1.5%.
  4. People who earn less spend more of their income, so a sales tax takes a bigger share of their income. That makes it regressive.

1. Write progressive, flat, or regressive.
(a) Everyone pays 10% of their income.
(b) Higher incomes are taxed at higher rates.
(c) A tax that takes a bigger share of a low income than a high income.

Answer

(a) flat (b) progressive (c) regressive

2. Under a 15% flat tax, how much would someone earning $30,000 pay? Someone earning $150,000?

Answer

0.15 × 30,000 = $4,500. 0.15 × 150,000 = $22,500. (Same share, 15%, different dollar amounts.)

3. Which system do you think is fairest? Give a reason and one trade-off.

Answer

Any system with a reason and a trade-off. For example: Progressive, because people who earn more can afford a bigger share. Trade-off: some people say it discourages earning more. Or: Flat, because it's simple and everyone pays the same rate. Trade-off: it takes a bigger bite out of a low income's essentials.

Extra practice · optional

E1. Canada's GST credit gives money back to people with low incomes. Why might the government do that?

E2. Using the chart, how many times bigger is the higher earner's share than the lower earner's under Canada's progressive system?

Answer

E1. To offset the regressive effect of the GST on people with low incomes, who spend most of what they earn.

E2. 25.6 ÷ 8.3 ≈ 3 times bigger.

Moment 3Total compensation

The situation

Priya gets two job offers. Offer A pays a bigger salary. Offer B pays less salary, but comes with extras. Which is worth more? To decide, she compares total compensation: salary plus everything else the employer gives.

Worked example

Which offer has more total compensation?

  1. Offer A: salary only = $52,000.
  2. Offer B: $48,000 + $3,000 (health and dental) + $2,000 (bonus) + $1,440 (the employer adds 3% of salary to an RRSP) = $54,440.
  3. Difference: $54,440 − $52,000 = $2,440 more in Offer B.
  4. Non-money factors: Offer B also has an extra week of vacation. Priya should still check hours, commute and promotion chances before deciding.

1. An offer pays $40,000 plus benefits worth $2,500 and a $1,000 bonus. What is the total compensation?

Answer

$40,000 + $2,500 + $1,000 = $43,500.

2. Name two non-money factors you would weigh when comparing job offers.

Answer

Any two: hours, schedule, commute, vacation, chances for promotion, training, work environment, job security, location, remote work.

3. Priya loves her current team, but Offer A is $2,000 more in salary. Would you take it? Explain using total compensation and one non-money factor.

Answer

Look for a decision using numbers (A has $2,000 more salary; compare total compensation if benefits differ) and a non-money factor (team, commute, growth).

Extra practice · optional

E1. An employer matches 4% of a $45,000 salary into an RRSP. How much is that each year?

E2. Why might a person choose a lower salary with good health benefits?

Answer

E1. 0.04 × 45,000 = $1,800 a year.

E2. For example: they or their family need regular dental work or prescriptions; benefits can be worth thousands; peace of mind.

Moment 4Taxes and the social contract

The situation

In Canada, people pay taxes, and governments use the money to provide services everyone shares. This idea is called the social contract. In 2026–27, Alberta planned to spend about $84 billion: about $34.4 billion on health and $10.8 billion on education.

Worked example

How does one person's tax help everyone?

  1. One person: someone earning $70,000 pays about $12,197 in income tax (federal + Alberta).
  2. Pooled: millions of people's taxes together pay for things no one could pay for alone, like a hospital.
  3. Shared benefit: anyone can use the emergency room, a public school, or a highway, whatever they paid.
  4. So: taxes link individual contributions to collective benefits. That's the social contract.

1. What share of Alberta's planned spending went to education? (10.8 ÷ 84, as a percent)

Answer

10.8 ÷ 84 ≈ 0.129, so about 13%.

2. Name three services you used this month that taxes help pay for.

Answer

Any three: school, roads, public transit subsidies, library, parks, hospital or doctor visits, police or fire, clean water.

3. Should governments raise taxes to pay for better services, or lower taxes so people keep more? Give your opinion, a reason, and a trade-off.

Answer

Look for an opinion, a reason connected to services or take-home pay, and a real trade-off.

Extra practice · optional

E1. What could happen to services if many people stopped paying their taxes?

E2. Name one service that you think should get more tax money. Why?

Answer

E1. Governments would have less money, so services like hospitals and schools could get worse or cost users directly.

E2. A named service with a reason.
