Teacher note

Listen for: "a raise into a higher bracket means all my income is taxed more" (Moment 1, Question 3, the main misconception target); "flat tax = same dollars for everyone"; "salary is the only thing that matters in a job offer." Calculators are fine; the skill is working through the brackets.

Moment 1Tax brackets

1. 0.14 × 50,000 = $7,000. $7,000 − $2,303.28 = $4,696.72.

2. First bracket: 0.14 × 58,523 = $8,193.22. Second bracket: 90,000 − 58,523 = 31,477; 0.205 × 31,477 = $6,452.79. Bracket tax: $14,646.01. Minus credit $2,303.28 = $12,342.73.

Watch out

3. Only the income above $58,523 is taxed at 20.5%. Of the $2,000 raise, $523 is still taxed at 14% ($73.22) and $1,477 at 20.5% ($302.79). Extra federal tax: $376.01. Mo still keeps about $1,624 of the raise (before Alberta tax and deductions). A raise never lowers take-home pay because of brackets.

Moment 2Three ways to tax

1. (a) flat (b) progressive (c) regressive

2. 0.15 × 30,000 = $4,500. 0.15 × 150,000 = $22,500. (Same share, 15%, different dollar amounts.)

3. Any system with a reason and a trade-off. For example: Progressive, because people who earn more can afford a bigger share. Trade-off: some people say it discourages earning more. Or: Flat, because it's simple and everyone pays the same rate. Trade-off: it takes a bigger bite out of a low income's essentials.

Answer

Moment 3Total compensation

1. $40,000 + $2,500 + $1,000 = $43,500.

2. Any two: hours, schedule, commute, vacation, chances for promotion, training, work environment, job security, location, remote work.

3. Look for a decision using numbers (A has $2,000 more salary; compare total compensation if benefits differ) and a non-money factor (team, commute, growth).
