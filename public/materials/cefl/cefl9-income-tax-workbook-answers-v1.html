Before you start

Have you ever heard someone say "I got a tax refund"? Where do you think that money comes from?

Moment 1The T4 and the tax return

The situation

Jamie is 19 and worked at a sporting-goods store all last year. In February, the store gave Jamie a T4 slip: a summary of a year's pay and deductions. Jamie uses it to file a tax return with the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA). The return works out the tax Jamie really owed, compared with what was already taken off each paycheque.

Worked example

Does Jamie get a refund or owe money?

  1. Federal tax: 0.14 × 24,000 = $3,360. Minus basic personal credit $2,303.28 = $1,056.72.
  2. Alberta tax: 0.08 × 24,000 = $1,920. Minus Alberta credit $1,821.52 = $98.48.
  3. Tax owed: $1,056.72 + $98.48 = $1,155.20.
  4. Compare with box 22: $1,400 was already paid. $1,400 − $1,155.20 = $244.80 refund. A refund is Jamie's own money coming back, because too much was taken off during the year.

1. Use Jamie's T4. Which box shows total pay? Which shows the income tax already taken off?

Answer

Box 14 shows total employment income. Box 22 shows income tax already deducted.

2. Riley's T4 shows $15,000 in box 14 and $300 in box 22. Find Riley's tax owed and the refund or balance owing.

Answer

Federal: 0.14 × 15,000 = $2,100 − $2,303.28 → below zero, so $0. Alberta: 0.08 × 15,000 = $1,200 − $1,821.52 → $0. Tax owed $0. Riley already paid $300, so Riley gets a $300 refund.

Watch out

3. A student says: "A tax refund is a bonus the government gives you." Explain what a refund really is.

Extra practice · optional

E1. Why does the CRA need a tax return if tax was already taken off each paycheque?

E2. Check box 18: is 1.63% of $24,000 equal to $391.20?

Answer

E1. Paycheque deductions are estimates. The return adds up all income for the whole year, applies credits and deductions, and works out the exact tax.

E2. 0.0163 × 24,000 = $391.20. Yes, it matches.

Moment 2Credits and deductions

The situation

Jamie starts college and pays $3,000 in tuition. Jamie also puts $1,000 into an RRSP. Both lower Jamie's tax, but in different ways. A deduction lowers the income that gets taxed. A credit lowers the tax itself.

Worked example

How much does each one save Jamie (federal and Alberta)?

  1. RRSP deduction ($1,000): taxable income drops to $23,000. Federal: 0.14 × 23,000 − 2,303.28 = $916.72. Alberta: 0.08 × 23,000 − 1,821.52 = $18.48. New total $935.20, down from $1,155.20: a saving of $220 (14% + 8% of $1,000).
  2. Tuition credit ($3,000): the federal credit is 14% × 3,000 = $420 off federal tax. (Alberta ended its tuition credit in 2020.)
  3. The difference: a deduction saves your tax rate times the amount; a credit takes a set amount straight off your tax.
  4. Note: these credits can bring tax down to $0, but not below.

1. Write deduction or credit. (a) Tuition (b) RRSP contribution (c) Basic personal amount

Answer

(a) credit (b) deduction (c) credit

2. Morgan earns $30,000 and puts $500 into an RRSP. How much federal and Alberta tax does it save? Then explain why Riley ($15,000 income, from Moment 1) would save nothing from an RRSP this year.

Answer

Morgan's income is above both tax-free amounts, so a $500 deduction saves 14% + 8% = 22%: 0.22 × 500 = $110 ($70 federal, $40 Alberta). Riley's tax was already $0, so lowering taxable income can't save anything this year. (Riley could still contribute and claim the deduction in a later year.)

3. Why is understanding credits and deductions part of making a good financial plan?

Answer

Knowing what lowers your tax helps you plan: whether to save in an RRSP, which receipts to keep, and how much tax to expect, so your budget is accurate.

Extra practice · optional

E1. Jamie's federal tax is $1,056.72 and the tuition credit is $420. Jamie's friend has tuition credits worth $1,500 but federal tax of only $900. How much tax does the friend pay?

E2. Why might someone keep their receipts for donations and tuition?

Answer

E1. $0. Credits can lower tax to zero but not below. (Unused tuition credits can be carried forward to future years.)

E2. Receipts prove the credit or deduction if the CRA asks, and are needed to claim it.

Moment 3RRSP or TFSA?

The situation

Jamie wants to save $1,000 for the future. Two kinds of registered accounts get special tax treatment: the RRSP (Registered Retirement Savings Plan) and the TFSA (Tax-Free Savings Account). In 2026, you can add up to $7,000 to a TFSA (plus unused room), and up to 18% of last year's earned income to an RRSP (to a maximum of $33,810).

Worked example

Jamie earns $24,000. Should the $1,000 go in an RRSP or a TFSA?

  1. RRSP now: saves about $220 in tax this year (14% federal + 8% Alberta). But when Jamie takes it out in retirement, it's taxed then, possibly at a higher rate.
  2. TFSA now: no tax saving this year. But all growth and withdrawals are tax-free, and Jamie can take it out any time, for example for school.
  3. Jamie's situation: a low income now, likely higher later, and might need the money soon.
  4. So: a TFSA probably fits Jamie better now. An RRSP gets more useful when Jamie earns more and is in a higher bracket.

1. Write RRSP or TFSA. (a) Withdrawals are tax-free. (b) Contributions lower your taxable income. (c) The 2026 yearly limit is $7,000.

Answer

(a) TFSA (b) RRSP (c) TFSA

2. Sam earned $50,000 last year. What is Sam's RRSP contribution limit this year (18%)?

Answer

0.18 × 50,000 = $9,000.

3. Dana earns $110,000 and is saving for retirement. Would you suggest an RRSP or a TFSA? Explain using tax rates.

Answer

RRSP. At $110,000, Dana is in a higher bracket (20.5% federal + 10% Alberta on the top dollars), so each RRSP dollar saves about 30% in tax now. In retirement Dana's income, and tax rate, will probably be lower.

Extra practice · optional

E1. Why might a TFSA be better for a goal 3 years away, like a car?

E2. Can someone have both an RRSP and a TFSA? Why might they?

Answer

E1. TFSA money can be taken out any time with no tax, and the room comes back the next year. RRSP withdrawals are taxed and are meant for retirement.

E2. Yes. For example: an RRSP for retirement (tax break now) and a TFSA for shorter goals or extra savings.

Moment 4Gigs, tips and self-employment

The situation

Kai edits videos for local businesses and earns $10,000 in a year. Kai is self-employed: no employer, no T4, no tax taken off. Kai's friend works at a restaurant and gets cash tips. Both kinds of income must be reported on a tax return.

Worked example

How much CPP does Kai owe on $10,000 of self-employment income?

  1. Exemption: CPP isn't charged on the first $3,500. 10,000 − 3,500 = $6,500.
  2. Both halves: a self-employed person pays the employee and employer share: 5.95% + 5.95% = 11.9%.
  3. CPP: 0.119 × 6,500 = $773.50.
  4. Plus income tax: with no tax taken off during the year, Kai should set money aside for tax and CPP before tax time.

1. Which of these must be reported on a tax return? Circle all: cash tips · gig delivery earnings · babysitting money · freelance design work

Answer

All four must be reported: cash tips, gig delivery earnings, babysitting money and freelance design work are all income.

2. A self-employed tutor earns $8,000. How much CPP does the tutor owe?

Answer

8,000 − 3,500 = 4,500. 0.119 × 4,500 = $535.50.

3. Government benefits like the GST/HST credit and the Canada Child Benefit are paid through the tax system and depend on income. Why does that give people a reason to file a tax return, even with low income?

Answer

These benefits are calculated from your tax return. If you don't file, the CRA can't pay them, even if you qualify. People with low incomes often get the most.

Extra practice · optional

E1. Why should a self-employed person keep receipts and records all year?

E2. What could happen to someone who doesn't report their tips or gig income?

Answer

E1. To report income correctly, claim business expenses, and prove amounts if the CRA asks.

E2. They could owe the unpaid tax plus interest and penalties after a CRA review.
