Moment 1Experiences that open doors

The situation

By Grade 9, you can create real experiences that move you toward a career. Aiden volunteers at a seniors' centre and is thinking about nursing. Sofia joined Alberta's Registered Apprenticeship Program (RAP) in high school and works in a cabinet shop for credit and pay. Zoe started a bike repair service in her neighbourhood.

Worked example

How could Aiden's volunteering help his nursing goal?

  1. Exposure: he sees what care work is really like before spending years training for it.
  2. Skills: he practises communication, patience and teamwork, all used by nurses.
  3. Evidence: he can put it on a résumé and nursing applications, and a supervisor can be a reference.
  4. Checking fit: if he discovers he doesn't enjoy care work, he's learned something important cheaply.

1. Write volunteering, work-integrated learning, or entrepreneurship.
(a) A student earns credits working at a veterinary clinic.
(b) A student starts a lawn-care business.
(c) A student helps coach a Special Olympics team.

Answer

(a) work-integrated learning (b) entrepreneurship (c) volunteering

2. Pick a career you're curious about. Name one experience of each kind that could connect to it.

Answer

A career with one experience of each kind. For example, veterinarian: volunteer at an animal shelter; work experience at a vet clinic; a pet-sitting business.

3. Why might trying an experience before choosing a post-secondary program save time and money?

Answer

You find out whether you actually like the work before paying for years of training. If you don't, you can change direction early.

Extra practice · optional

E1. Ask your school what work experience or RAP options it offers. What did you find out?

E2. What could Zoe learn about herself by running her business?

Answer

E1. Real school options (work experience courses, RAP, dual-credit, job shadow days).

E2. For example: whether she enjoys being her own boss, how she handles customers and money, whether she likes hands-on repair work.

Moment 2A simple business plan

The situation

Zoe buys $150 of tools once. Each tune-up uses about $5 of parts. She charges $25 per tune-up. How many tune-ups before she earns back the tools?

Worked example

When does Zoe break even?

  1. Fixed cost (paid once): tools $150.
  2. Profit per tune-up: price − variable cost = $25 − $5 = $20.
  3. Break-even: fixed cost ÷ profit per tune-up = 150 ÷ 20 = 7.5, so after 8 tune-ups she has earned back her tools.
  4. After that, each tune-up adds $20 profit. A business plan also lists customers (who will pay?), risks (what could go wrong?) and roles (who does what?).

Connects to Math: dividing and reading a linear graph

1. How much profit does Zoe make after 20 tune-ups?

Answer

20 × $20 = $400; $400 − $150 = $250 profit.

2. Name two risks for Zoe's business and how she could manage each.

Answer

Any two risks with a strategy. For example: a customer's bike is damaged (be careful, get a parent's help with hard repairs, explain limits up front); not enough customers (advertise at school, start in spring); a tool breaks (budget for replacements).

3. Zoe could raise her price to $30. What might happen to her break-even point? What might happen to the number of customers?

Answer

Profit per tune-up becomes $25, so break-even drops to 150 ÷ 25 = 6 tune-ups. But some customers might go elsewhere if the price is too high. Look for both effects.

Extra practice · optional

E1. A student buys a $60 button maker. Each button costs $0.50 to make and sells for $2. Find the break-even point.

E2. Why should a new business start small?

Answer

E1. Profit per button: $2 − $0.50 = $1.50. 60 ÷ 1.50 = 40 buttons.

E2. Less money at risk; you can test whether people want it and fix problems before spending more.

Moment 3Skills: formal, informal and transferable

The situation

Skills come from formal learning (courses, training, certificates) and informal learning (teaching yourself, learning from family, practice). Sofia learned measuring and safety in her RAP placement (formal) and learned to fix furniture from her grandfather (informal). She also speaks English and French.

Worked example

Which of Sofia's skills will help her in any career, and which mostly in carpentry?

  1. Job-specific: measuring, cutting and shop safety are mostly for carpentry and similar trades.
  2. Employability (transferable): teamwork, time management, problem solving, and speaking two languages help in almost any job.
  3. Bilingualism: Canada has two official languages, English and French. About 18% of Canadians can hold a conversation in both (2021 Census). Some jobs, like many federal government jobs, require both; for others it's an asset.
  4. So: Sofia's résumé should show both kinds of skills, and where she learned them.

1. Sort each skill: formal or informal? (a) A first-aid certificate (b) Learning to cook from a parent (c) Teaching yourself video editing online

Answer

(a) formal (b) informal (c) informal

2. List two job-specific skills and two employability skills for a career you're curious about.

Answer

For example, chef. Job-specific: knife skills, food-safety rules. Employability: teamwork, time management.

3. Name a career where speaking a second language would be an asset or a requirement. Why?

Answer

For example: federal government jobs, tourism, health care in bilingual communities, customer service, teaching French. A second language lets you serve more people.

Extra practice · optional

E1. Which of your informal skills could go on a résumé? How would you describe it?

E2. About 18% of Canadians are bilingual in English and French. In a class of 30, about how many students is that?

Answer

E1. An informal skill described with evidence (for example: "Self-taught video editor; edit videos for my school's announcements").

E2. 0.18 × 30 = 5.4, so about 5 students.

Moment 4Getting hired: cover letters and digital identity

The situation

Aiden applies for a summer job at a care home. He finds the posting on Job Bank, sends a résumé and cover letter, and fills in an online application. Before the interview, the manager searches his name online. What will she find? That's his digital identity.

Worked example

How should Aiden prepare his application?

  1. Résumé: tailored to the posting (see the Grade 9 careers workbook).
  2. Cover letter: a short letter that says which job he wants, why he's a good fit (his volunteering), and thanks them. It's adjusted for each employer.
  3. Digital identity: he searches his own name, sets accounts to private, removes posts he wouldn't want an employer to see, and uses a professional email.
  4. Resources: Job Bank, school counsellors, personal networks, and employment services (including services for newcomers, Francophones and under-represented groups) can help.

1. Name two resources a teenager could use to find a job.

Answer

Any two: Job Bank, alis.alberta.ca, school counsellor, family and friends (networks), local employment services, employer websites, community job boards.

2. Write the first two sentences of a cover letter for a job you'd like.

Answer

Look for the job named, a reason they're a good fit, and a polite tone. For example: "I'm applying for the summer camp assistant position. I have volunteered at my local library's reading program for two years and love working with children."

3. Explain one risk and one benefit of your digital identity when looking for work.

Answer

Risk: inappropriate posts can cost you an interview. Benefit: a positive online presence (awards, volunteering) can support your application.

Watch out

A student says: "What I post online is private. It won't affect getting a job." Respond using the scenario.

Extra practice · optional

E1. What should a professional email address look like? Give an example (make it up).

E2. Why should a cover letter be different for each employer?

Answer

E1. First and last name, nothing silly. Example: aiden.wong.jobs@example.com (made up).

E2. Each employer wants different skills; a tailored letter shows you've read their posting and explains why you fit their job.
