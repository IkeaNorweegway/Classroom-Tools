Create Your Own Experience

Answers and what to look for · Outcome 9-EX · Alberta draft curriculum (2026)

Teacher note

Listen for: "what I post online is private" (Moment 4, the main misconception target); "only formal courses count as skills"; "a business only needs a good idea" (it needs customers, costs and risk planning).

Moment 1Experiences that open doors

1. (a) work-integrated learning (b) entrepreneurship (c) volunteering

2. A career with one experience of each kind. For example, veterinarian: volunteer at an animal shelter; work experience at a vet clinic; a pet-sitting business.

3. You find out whether you actually like the work before paying for years of training. If you don't, you can change direction early.

Answer

E1. Real school options (work experience courses, RAP, dual-credit, job shadow days).

E2. For example: whether she enjoys being her own boss, how she handles customers and money, whether she likes hands-on repair work.

Moment 2A simple business plan

1. 20 × $20 = $400; $400 − $150 = $250 profit.

2. Any two risks with a strategy. For example: a customer's bike is damaged (be careful, get a parent's help with hard repairs, explain limits up front); not enough customers (advertise at school, start in spring); a tool breaks (budget for replacements).

3. Profit per tune-up becomes $25, so break-even drops to 150 ÷ 25 = 6 tune-ups. But some customers might go elsewhere if the price is too high. Look for both effects.

Answer

E1. Profit per button: $2 − $0.50 = $1.50. 60 ÷ 1.50 = 40 buttons.

E2. Less money at risk; you can test whether people want it and fix problems before spending more.

Moment 3Skills

1. (a) formal (b) informal (c) informal

2. For example, chef. Job-specific: knife skills, food-safety rules. Employability: teamwork, time management.

3. For example: federal government jobs, tourism, health care in bilingual communities, customer service, teaching French. A second language lets you serve more people.

Answer

E1. An informal skill described with evidence (for example: "Self-taught video editor; edit videos for my school's announcements").

E2. 0.18 × 30 = 5.4, so about 5 students.

Moment 4Getting hired

1. Any two: Job Bank, alis.alberta.ca, school counsellor, family and friends (networks), local employment services, employer websites, community job boards.

2. Look for the job named, a reason they're a good fit, and a polite tone. For example: "I'm applying for the summer camp assistant position. I have volunteered at my local library's reading program for two years and love working with children."

3. Risk: inappropriate posts can cost you an interview. Benefit: a positive online presence (awards, volunteering) can support your application.

Watch out

Employers often search applicants' names online, as Aiden's manager did. Posts can be shared, screenshotted or public by default, so "private" isn't guaranteed. Students should check and clean up their digital identity before applying.

Answer

E1. First and last name, nothing silly. Example: aiden.wong.jobs@example.com (made up).

E2. Each employer wants different skills; a tailored letter shows you've read their posting and explains why you fit their job.
