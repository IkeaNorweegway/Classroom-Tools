Moment 1Who is in the job?

The situation

In Canada, some careers are filled mostly by men and others mostly by women. That is called representation: who is (and isn't) in a career compared with the whole population. Women and men are each about half of Canada's population.

Worked example

Is representation in electrical apprenticeships equitable?

  1. Women are about 50% of the population.
  2. Women were only 4.8% of electrical apprentices.
  3. 4.8% is far below 50%, so women are under-represented in this career.
  4. Equitable representation would be much closer to the population. A gap this big suggests something is keeping people out, not that they couldn't do the job.

1. Using the chart, name one career where men are under-represented and one where women are.

Answer

Men under-represented: regulated nurses (about 9%). Women under-represented: electrical apprentices (4.8%), all apprentices (13.6%), or new engineers (17.4%).

2. Engineers Canada set a goal: 30% of new engineers should be women by 2030. In 2018 it was 17.4%. How far was it from the goal?

Answer

30 − 17.4 = 12.6 percentage points below the goal.

3. Why might it matter to a whole community, not just to workers, that careers are open to everyone?

Answer

For example: communities get more workers for jobs they need (like electricians and nurses); people with different backgrounds bring different ideas; more people can earn a good living; patients and customers are served by people who understand them.

Extra practice · optional

E1. Name another career you think might have unequal representation. Who might be under-represented?

E2. These numbers are from 2018–2019. Why should you look for newer data?

Answer

E1. Any reasonable career with a group named (for example, men in early childhood education; women in welding; Indigenous people in some professions).

E2. Representation changes over time, and programs like 30 by 30 are trying to change it, so old numbers may not show today's picture.

Moment 2Seeing yourself in a career

The situation

Lena loved fixing things, but she had never met a woman electrician. She assumed it "wasn't for girls." Then a woman electrician visited her school. Lena said, "I never knew I could do that." She signed up for a CTS electrical course the next year.

Worked example

How did representation affect Lena's career vision?

  1. Before: with no role models, Lena couldn't picture herself in the career, so she wasn't motivated to try it.
  2. What changed: meeting someone like her showed the career was possible.
  3. Effect: her motivation grew, and she chose an experience (a CTS course) that builds skills for the career.
  4. So: representation can shape who imagines a career, who tries it, and who stays.

1. Has representation ever affected what you thought you could do (in a sport, a subject, or a job)? Explain.

Answer

Personal. Look for a real example and how it affected motivation or choices.

2. Name two things a school or workplace could do to break the cycle in the diagram.

Answer

Any two: invite role models to visit; show diverse people in career materials; offer hands-on experiences (CTS courses, job shadowing) to everyone; mentorship programs; welcoming workplace culture and anti-harassment policies.

3. A student says: "Some jobs are just for boys and some are just for girls." Use the data and Lena's story to respond.

Answer

The data shows representation is unequal, but that isn't the same as ability. Lena could do the job; she just hadn't seen anyone like her doing it. Jobs are for anyone with the interest and skills.

Extra practice · optional

E1. Who is a role model for a career you're curious about? What makes them a role model for you?

E2. Why might it be hard to be the only woman, or the only man, on a team at work?

Answer

E1. Personal; look for a reason connected to the person's actions or path.

E2. For example: feeling left out, being treated as a stand-in for a whole group, having no one to ask for advice, or facing jokes or bias.

Moment 3Growth mindset and employability skills

The situation

Employability skills are skills that help you succeed in any job: communication, collaboration (teamwork), problem solving, critical thinking, technology use, and time management. Nobody is born with them. They grow with practice, and a growth mindset helps.

Worked example

Ben says, "I'm bad at time management." How can a growth mindset change that?

  1. Notice the fixed thought: "I'm bad at" treats the skill like something you can't change.
  2. Change it: "I'm not good at time management yet."
  3. Pick an experience that builds it: Ben joins the school store team, where he has to show up for shifts on time.
  4. Reflect: after a month, he uses a planner and hasn't missed a shift. The skill grew because he practised it.

1. Rewrite each fixed thought as a growth thought.
(a) "I'm not a team player."
(b) "I can't solve hard problems."

Answer

For example: (a) "I'm learning to work with others; I'll practise in group projects." (b) "Hard problems take time; I can break them into steps."

2. Pick one employability skill you want to build. Name an experience (in or out of school) that could build it.

Answer

A named skill and a matching experience (for example: communication through peer tutoring; time management through a part-time job or team commitment).

3. Why do employers care about employability skills, even more than knowing one specific task?

Answer

Specific tasks can be taught on the job and change with technology; employability skills transfer to every job and show you can learn, work with others and solve problems.

Extra practice · optional

E1. Which employability skill did you use most this week? Where?

E2. How could volunteering build two employability skills at once?

Answer

E1. Personal, with where it was used.

E2. For example: at a food bank you practise collaboration (working with other volunteers) and communication (helping clients), and often time management (showing up for shifts).

Moment 4Skills for entrepreneurs

The situation

Kai, 14, wants to sell custom-printed T-shirts. Each blank shirt costs $8 and printing costs $4. He plans to sell them for $20. He's thinking of buying 50 shirts at once.

Worked example

What is Kai's profit on one shirt, and how can he manage his risk?

  1. Cost per shirt: $8 + $4 = $12.
  2. Profit per shirt: $20 − $12 = $8.
  3. Risk management: if he buys 50 ($600) and only sells 20, he earns 20 × $20 = $400 and loses $200. Starting with 15 tests demand with less money at risk.
  4. Other skills he needs: time management (making shirts around school) and decision making (choosing designs, prices and how many to order).

Connects to Math: multiplying and subtracting money

1. If Kai buys 15 shirts and sells all of them, what is his total profit?

Answer

15 × $8 = $120 profit.

2. Name one risk for Kai's business besides unsold shirts. How could he manage it?

Answer

Any risk with a strategy. For example: the printer breaks (have a backup plan or supplier); a competitor sells cheaper (offer unique designs); he runs out of time during exams (plan production around the school calendar).

3. Which entrepreneurship skill (risk management, time management, decision making) would be hardest for you? How could you build it?

Answer

A named skill and a way to build it.

Watch out

A student says: "Being your own boss means no risk and no rules." Use Kai's business to explain what's wrong.

Extra practice · optional

E1. Kai buys 50 shirts and sells 35. What is his profit or loss?

E2. What could Kai do with unsold shirts to lose less money?

Answer

E1. Sales: 35 × $20 = $700. Cost: 50 × $12 = $600. Profit: $100. (Less than if he'd bought only what sold.)

E2. For example: lower the price to sell them, sell at another event, bundle them, or donate them for a tax receipt.
