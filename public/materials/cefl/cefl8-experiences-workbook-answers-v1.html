Who Gets to Do This Job?

Answers and what to look for · Outcome 8-EX · Alberta draft curriculum (2026)

Teacher note

Listen for: "some jobs are just for boys/girls" (Moment 2, Question 3, the main misconception target); "skills are personality, you have them or you don't"; "being your own boss means no risk." Keep discussion about representation respectful: the point is that careers should be open to everyone, not that any group is better at a job.

Moment 1Who is in the job?

1. Men under-represented: regulated nurses (about 9%). Women under-represented: electrical apprentices (4.8%), all apprentices (13.6%), or new engineers (17.4%).

2. 30 − 17.4 = 12.6 percentage points below the goal.

3. For example: communities get more workers for jobs they need (like electricians and nurses); people with different backgrounds bring different ideas; more people can earn a good living; patients and customers are served by people who understand them.

Answer

E1. Any reasonable career with a group named (for example, men in early childhood education; women in welding; Indigenous people in some professions).

E2. Representation changes over time, and programs like 30 by 30 are trying to change it, so old numbers may not show today's picture.

Moment 2Seeing yourself in a career

1. Personal. Look for a real example and how it affected motivation or choices.

2. Any two: invite role models to visit; show diverse people in career materials; offer hands-on experiences (CTS courses, job shadowing) to everyone; mentorship programs; welcoming workplace culture and anti-harassment policies.

3. The data shows representation is unequal, but that isn't the same as ability. Lena could do the job; she just hadn't seen anyone like her doing it. Jobs are for anyone with the interest and skills.

Answer

E1. Personal; look for a reason connected to the person's actions or path.

E2. For example: feeling left out, being treated as a stand-in for a whole group, having no one to ask for advice, or facing jokes or bias.

Moment 3Growth mindset and employability skills

1. For example: (a) "I'm learning to work with others; I'll practise in group projects." (b) "Hard problems take time; I can break them into steps."

2. A named skill and a matching experience (for example: communication through peer tutoring; time management through a part-time job or team commitment).

3. Specific tasks can be taught on the job and change with technology; employability skills transfer to every job and show you can learn, work with others and solve problems.

Answer

E1. Personal, with where it was used.

E2. For example: at a food bank you practise collaboration (working with other volunteers) and communication (helping clients), and often time management (showing up for shifts).

Moment 4Skills for entrepreneurs

1. 15 × $8 = $120 profit.

2. Any risk with a strategy. For example: the printer breaks (have a backup plan or supplier); a competitor sells cheaper (offer unique designs); he runs out of time during exams (plan production around the school calendar).

3. A named skill and a way to build it.

Watch out

Kai carries all the risk: if shirts don't sell, he loses his own money. Entrepreneurs also have rules to follow (taxes, safety, honest advertising) and must manage their time and decisions. There's no boss, but there's more responsibility.

Answer

E1. Sales: 35 × $20 = $700. Cost: 50 × $12 = $600. Profit: $100. (Less than if he'd bought only what sold.)

E2. For example: lower the price to sell them, sell at another event, bundle them, or donate them for a tax receipt.
