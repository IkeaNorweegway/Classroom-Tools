At a glance

Teacher note

Before Session 1:

  • Hook photos: gather 4–6 photos of people working in careers where they're under-represented. Use real, credited photos from career sites, alis.alberta.ca, or organizations promoting women in trades or men in nursing.
  • Career list for Step 1: women in electrical, welding, carpentry, engineering, pilots; men in nursing, early childhood education, dental hygiene. Note any Indigenous representation data you can find for your region.
  • Guests: if possible, invite one guest from an under-represented group in a career (in person or video) for Step 2.

Why it is built this way

  • Driving question first. The photo hook surfaces the stereotypes students already hold, without anyone being called out.
  • Data before opinion. Students use real figures with sources before discussing reasons.
  • Real stories. A real person's path (Step 2) is the strongest evidence that the door can open.
  • Campaign with a real audience. Younger students are the ones whose ideas about careers are still forming.

Watch out

"Some jobs are just for boys / girls." Moment 2, Question 3. Keep the line clear: representation gaps are about access, culture and role models, not ability.

Teacher note

Respect check. Campaigns should show people as capable professionals, not as tokens or jokes. The Checker role includes this. Watch for campaigns that put down the majority group; the goal is opening doors, not closing others.

The Open Doors game (CEFL Town)

A companion game on the unit page, one mission in CEFL Town, the top-down town every CEFL unit shares. Each student makes a character once, or keeps the one from Grade 7. Mr. Osei at the Community Centre helps them build a campaign that opens career doors for a Grade 6 class.

  • When: sessions are full, so set each chapter as homework, early-finisher work, or a device day before the next session. Chapters play in order. Chapter length has not been timed with students yet.
  • Saving: the game saves in that browser, and the character carries over to every CEFL Town mission. To move to another computer, use Menu → Take your town home (a save code or a file).
  • Controls: arrow keys or tap to walk, Enter or tap to talk. Tapping the gold goal line at the top walks the student there, which suits students who find moving around hard.
  • The side story: The Career Corner posters are from 1985 and show one kind of person per job. The survey shows kids picture the same person too. The campaign replaces the posters with townspeople, and the job-shadow day is named after the student. The story never changes the goal.
  • The goal: the Grade 6 class is convinced at the launch: at least 3 of 4 questions answered right on the first try. The Mindset Flip game counts right answers; it never changes the goal.
  • Gentle and Real (Menu): Gentle gives a hint after a wrong try and shows the answer after 3 tries; Real gives no hints and shows it after 2. In Real the Grade 6 class still needs 3 good answers.
  • Between lessons (optional): after a chapter, students can choose "Not yet: help around town." Townspeople then ask for help with their own version of what the chapter taught, for a little play money in a wallet that never touches the mission, and one Town Mail letter arrives each day.
  • Report (Menu → Report for my teacher): first-try answers by chapter, what the student wrote and chose, game results, how far they got in the side story, and a Between lessons table. It prints. First-try answers make a quick look-for before each step's checkpoint.
  • Simplifications to tell students if asked: representation is shown for one group per career, and the data are several years old (Statistics Canada 2018 via CLAC, Engineers Canada 2018, CNA 2019), so newer data may differ.

Sample products (show all three in Session 5)

  1. Poster series. Three posters, each with a real role model's photo, one quote, and one fact ("Only 4.8% of electrical apprentices were women in 2018. That's changing.").
  2. Mock social media posts. Five posts for a fictional account showing a day in the career, with a call to action ("Try CTS Electrical in Grade 10").
  3. Career day table. A tabletop display with tools from the career, a role-model profile, and a "try it" activity.

When a team gets stuck

What you seeWhat to do
Can't find dataUse the workbook figures, or a profile and outlook from alis.alberta.ca; note the gap can be described without an exact percentage.
Reasons are stereotypesAsk: what does your real story say? What evidence supports that reason?
Campaign message is vagueAsk: who exactly should see this, and what should they do after?
Skill plan is genericAsk for a specific experience with a date.

Access supports (UDL)

  • Read data and stories aloud; the chart in Moment 1 shows the numbers visually.
  • Product choice (posters, video, posts, brochure, table display).
  • Some students may belong to under-represented groups themselves. Let them choose how much personal experience to share.

Background for the teacher

  • Women: 4.8% of electrical apprentices and 13.6% of all apprentice registrations (Statistics Canada 2018 data, reported by CLAC). Women: 17.4% of newly licensed engineers (Engineers Canada 2018); goal 30% by 2030 ("30 by 30", started by APEGA in Alberta). Men: about 9% of regulated nurses (Canadian Nurses Association, 2019). Newer data may differ.
