Teacher note

Do this before Session 1

  • Hook cards: write 10–12 short experience descriptions to read aloud (lemonade stand, coaching kids' soccer, animal shelter helper, library helper, babysitting course, selling crafts online with a parent, school tech crew, community garden, peer tutor, snow shovelling, food bank, leading a school club).
  • Experience ideas list for Step 3: school roles (library helper, tech crew, peer reader, event set-up), community options that accept young volunteers with an adult (food bank, seniors' centre visits, community clean-up, animal shelter events), and safe micro-businesses (pet sitting, crafts, yard work). Check local age rules for each volunteer site.
  • Book the audience for Session 7.

Why it is built this way

  • Driving question first. The "I'd love this / not for me" hook makes students notice their own preferences right away.
  • Real experience, not just reading about it. Teenagers who volunteer, work part-time, or run small ventures tend to have better adult work outcomes (OECD career readiness indicators). The plan asks for a real, specific experience, and students start it if they can.
  • Personal plan, team checklist. Preferences are personal; safety and belonging are shared. The team checklist gives a real reason for structured teamwork.
  • Safety rights matter now. Students in Grades 7–9 often start babysitting, yard work, or their first jobs. Knowing the right to refuse is practical.

Watch out

"Only paid jobs count as work experience." Moment 1, Question 3 targets this. Students who say "I haven't done anything" usually have chores, sibling care, clubs, or helping a family business. Name these as real experience.

Look for

Plans are specific (what, where, when, who gives OK) and realistic for a 12–13-year-old. Volunteering and self-employment aren't covered by the youth employment rules, but they still need a parent or guardian's OK and adult supervision where appropriate.

Watch out

"If a boss asks, I have to do it." Under Alberta's Occupational Health and Safety Act, workers have the right to refuse dangerous work and can't be punished for it. Practise the words: "I haven't been trained to do that safely."

Teacher note

Follow-up: encourage students to actually do their planned experience. A short check-in a few weeks later ("How did it go?") strengthens the link between experience and reflection.

The The Garden Crew game (CEFL Town)

A companion game on the unit page, one mission in CEFL Town, the top-down town every CEFL unit shares. Each student makes a character once (style, skin tone, hair, shirt, first name). Mr. Osei, the volunteer coordinator at the Community Centre, helps them plan a volunteer experience and help the Community Garden.

  • When: sessions are full, so set each chapter as homework, early-finisher work, or a device day before the next session. Chapters play in order. Chapter length has not been timed with students yet.
  • Saving: the game saves in that browser, and the character carries over to every CEFL Town mission. To move to another computer, use Menu → Take your town home (a save code or a file).
  • Controls: arrow keys or tap to walk, Enter or tap to talk. Tapping the gold goal line at the top walks the student there, which suits students who find moving around hard.
  • The side story: Every new volunteer quits after one day. Clue by clue students find the reasons: Kai was left out, the sign-up sheet asks too much of a week, and the shed has hazards and no first-day plan. Their Welcome and Safety checklist becomes the garden's first-day plan, named after the student. The story never changes the goal.
  • The goal: the Grade 6 class signs up to volunteer: at least 3 of 4 of their questions answered right on the first try. The Safety sweep game counts hazards found; it never changes the goal.
  • Gentle and Real (Menu): Gentle gives a hint after a wrong try and shows the answer after 3 tries; Real gives no hints and shows it after 2. In Real the Grade 6 class still needs 3 good answers.
  • Between lessons (optional): after a chapter, students can choose "Not yet: help around town." Townspeople then ask for help with their own version of what the chapter taught, for a little play money in a wallet that never touches the mission, and one Town Mail letter arrives each day.
  • Report (Menu → Report for my teacher): first-try answers by chapter, what the student wrote and chose, game results, how far they got in the side story, and a Between lessons table. It prints. First-try answers make a quick look-for before each step's checkpoint.
  • Simplifications to tell students if asked: a week is 168 hours; only the age 13–14 rules are used (with a parent's OK: up to 2 hours on a school day, up to 8 on a non-school day, never 9 pm to 6 am); the rules cover employees, not volunteers or the self-employed.

Sample products (show all three in Session 5)

  1. Brochure. A folded "About me at work" brochure: past experiences inside, preference sliders on the back, and the planned experience on the front.
  2. Short video. A 90-second "day in my planned experience" walkthrough, filmed where it happens (with permission) or acted out.
  3. Pocket safety card (team). A business-card-size checklist: three rights on one side, "first day questions to ask" on the other.

What you seeWhat to do

No adult to give permission for a community experienceChoose a school role instead.

Access supports (UDL)

  • Reading: read scenarios and experience cards aloud.
  • Expression: poster, brochure, video, comic, or slideshow.
  • Movement: the hook uses movement; the checklist can be built with sticky notes before writing.
  • Sensitivity: some students have big family responsibilities (caring for siblings, working in a family business). Treat these as real, valuable experience, and be aware they may limit time for new experiences.

Background for the teacher

  • Alberta youth employment rules (alberta.ca): ages 13–14 need parental consent, can't work 9 pm–6 am, and can work up to 2 hours on a school day and 8 on a non-school day, in listed jobs only. Ages 15–17 can do most jobs with some limits. Under 13 is limited to artistic work with a permit. The rules cover employees, not volunteers or the self-employed. Check alberta.ca before teaching, since rules change.
  • Three rights (Alberta OHS Act): to know, to participate, to refuse dangerous work.
