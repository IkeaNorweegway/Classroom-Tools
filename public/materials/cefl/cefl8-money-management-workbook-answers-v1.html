If you put $100 in a bank account and left it there for 10 years, how much do you think you'd have? Why?

Moment 1Fixed costs and variable costs

The situation

Ty is 16. He earns about $511 a month from a part-time job. Some of his costs are the same every month. Others go up and down depending on his choices.

Worked example

How much is Ty saving, and how could he save $200 a month?

  1. Total costs: $120 fixed + $270 variable = $390.
  2. Saving now: $511 − $390 = $121 a month.
  3. To save $200: he needs $79 more. Fixed costs are hard to change quickly, so look at variable costs.
  4. One plan: food out $120 → $80 (−$40), games $70 → $40 (−$30), clothes $80 → $71 (−$9). Total change: $79. Now he saves $200.

1. Write fixed or variable.
(a) Rent for an apartment
(b) Groceries
(c) A music streaming subscription

Answer

(a) fixed (b) variable (c) fixed

2. Ty's phone plan goes up to $45. What happens to his savings if nothing else changes?

Answer

Fixed costs rise by $10 to $130; savings drop from $121 to $111 a month.

3. Why is it usually easier to change variable costs than fixed costs? Give an example.

Answer

Variable costs depend on day-to-day choices, so you can change them right away (eat out less this week). Fixed costs are set by contracts or memberships, so changing them takes time (cancel a plan, move).

Extra practice · optional

E1. Make your own list of 3 fixed and 3 variable costs a teenager might have.

E2. Ty wants $1,500 for a used bike by next summer (10 months). Is $121 a month enough? $200?

Answer

E1. For example. Fixed: phone plan, streaming subscription, bus pass. Variable: snacks, clothes, gifts.

E2. $121 × 10 = $1,210: not enough. $200 × 10 = $2,000: enough, with $500 to spare.

Moment 2Simple interest

The situation

Ty puts $500 in a savings account that pays 3% interest a year. Interest is the reward for saving (or the cost of borrowing). The principal is the money he starts with, and the rate is the percent per year.

Worked example

How much simple interest does Ty earn on $500 at 3% for 4 years?

  1. Formula: Interest = Principal × rate × time, or I = P × r × t.
  2. Change the rate to a decimal: 3% = 0.03.
  3. Multiply: I = 500 × 0.03 × 4 = $60.
  4. Total after 4 years: $500 + $60 = $560.

Connects to Math: percents as decimals, and using a formula

1. Find the simple interest on $2,000 at 4% for 3 years. What is the total?

Answer

I = 2,000 × 0.04 × 3 = $240. Total $2,240.

2. Find the simple interest on $800 at 5% for 2 years.

Answer

I = 800 × 0.05 × 2 = $80.

3. Interest can be a reward for saving or a cost of borrowing. Explain the difference with an example of each.

Answer

Saving: the bank pays you interest for keeping your money (for example, 3% a year on savings). Borrowing: you pay interest to use someone else's money (for example, interest on a loan or a credit card balance).

Extra practice · optional

E1. How many years would it take $500 at 3% simple interest to earn $150?

E2. Which earns more: $1,000 at 2% for 5 years, or $500 at 5% for 4 years?

Answer

E1. $15 a year: 150 ÷ 15 = 10 years.

E2. 1,000 × 0.02 × 5 = $100 and 500 × 0.05 × 4 = $100. They earn the same: $100.

Moment 3Compound interest

The situation

With compound interest, you earn interest on your principal and on the interest you already earned. Your money grows faster every year, like a snowball rolling downhill.

Worked example

Ty's $500 earns 3% compound interest. How much after 4 years?

  1. Year 1: 500 × 1.03 = $515.00
  2. Year 2: 515.00 × 1.03 = $530.45
  3. Year 3: 530.45 × 1.03 = $546.36
  4. Year 4: 546.36 × 1.03 = $562.75 (or in one step: 500 × 1.03⁴)
  5. Compare: simple interest gave $560; compound gives $562.75. Small now, but the gap grows over time.

1. Find the value of $2,000 at 4% compound interest after 3 years. Show each year.

Answer

Year 1: 2,000 × 1.04 = $2,080.00. Year 2: 2,080 × 1.04 = $2,163.20. Year 3: 2,163.20 × 1.04 = $2,249.73. (Simple would be $2,240.)

2. Use the graph. After 10 years, how much more does compound interest earn than simple interest?

Answer

$1,628.89 − $1,500 = $128.89 more.

Watch out

3. A student says: "Simple and compound interest give about the same result, so it doesn't matter." Use the graph to explain when it does matter.

Extra practice · optional

E1. $1,000 at 6% compound for 40 years grows to about $10,286. For 50 years, about $18,420. What does that tell you about starting to save early?

E2. Find $800 at 5% compound after 2 years. Compare it with your simple-interest answer in Moment 2.

Answer

E1. Ten extra years nearly doubles the result ($10,286 → $18,420). Starting early matters because compound growth is biggest in the later years.

E2. 800 × 1.05 = 840; 840 × 1.05 = $882.00. Simple gave $880, so compound is $2 more.

Moment 4Saving or investing?

The situation

Saving means putting money aside somewhere safe. Investing means putting money into something that may grow, like a fund that owns parts of many companies. Investing can earn more, but it comes with risk: the chance of losing value.

Worked example

Ty wants a bike in 10 months and a car in 5 years. Where should each pot of money go?

  1. Bike (10 months): short-term. He can't risk losing money he needs soon, so a savings account (low risk, easy to use) fits.
  2. Car (5 years): longer term. A GIC earns a bit more and his money is safe, but it's locked in.
  3. Investment fund: might grow more over many years, but could drop in a bad year. Better for goals 5+ years away, and only money he won't need soon.
  4. Rule of thumb: the sooner you need it, the safer it should be.

1. Write saving or investing.
(a) Putting $200 in a high-interest savings account
(b) Buying units of a fund that owns many companies

Answer

(a) saving (b) investing

2. Why should money for a short-term goal usually be saved, not invested?

Answer

Investments can lose value in the short term. If you need the money soon, you might have to take it out when it's down. Saving keeps the principal safe.

3. Your grandparent gives you $1,000 for when you finish high school (about 4–5 years away). Where would you put it? Explain the risk and the possible return.

Answer

Look for a choice matched to the 4–5 year timeline with risk and return explained. For example: a GIC, because the money is safe and earns a little more than a savings account, and I don't need it until I graduate. Accept a partly invested answer if the risk of losing value is named.

Extra practice · optional

E1. What does "higher-risk investments may offer higher returns" mean in your own words?

E2. Why are real interest rates labelled "sample" here? Where could you find today's rates?

Answer

E1. Investments that might earn more also have a bigger chance of losing money.

E2. Real rates change often (they depend on the bank and the economy). Check bank or credit union websites, or ask at a branch.
