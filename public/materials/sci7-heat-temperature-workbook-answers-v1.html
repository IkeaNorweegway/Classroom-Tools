Q2 — What temperature measures: (2 marks)

Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of particles (atoms or molecules) in a substance. Higher temperature means the particles move faster on average.

Q3 — No negative Kelvin: (2 marks)

Kelvin starts at absolute zero. That's the point where particles have the least possible kinetic energy — almost no movement. You can't have less energy than zero. So you can't go below 0 K. The scale measures energy, not just a comparison.

Q4 — Who is right: (4 marks)

Both are right. They are using different temperature scales. 100°C = 373 K (K = °C + 273). Water boils at 100°C or 373 K — these are the same temperature, just in two different units. Neither student is wrong. But they should say which scale they mean, to avoid confusion.

Part B: Heat vs. Temperature (20 marks)

Q5 — Cup and bathtub analogy: (4 marks)

Temperature: the cup of coffee and the bathtub could be at the same temperature (both 40°C). The average particle speed is equal in both. Heat: the bathtub has far more particles moving at that same speed. So its total kinetic energy — its heat — is much greater. Temperature means "how fast." Heat means "how much total energy." Same average speed, very different total amounts.

Q6 — Cup vs. swimming pool: (3 marks)

The swimming pool has more heat energy. Both are at 40°C, so the particles move at the same average speed. But the pool has trillions more water molecules than the cup. Total heat energy equals the number of particles times the average kinetic energy per particle. The "per particle" value is the same. But with far more particles, the pool stores a much larger total amount of heat energy.

Q7 — Direction of heat flow: (3 marks)

Heat always flows from a hotter region to a colder region. When two objects reach the same temperature, heat flow stops. They are now in thermal equilibrium. At equilibrium, the net energy transfer is zero.

Q8 — Scientific rewrite: (4 marks)

Wrong: "The cold is coming in through the window."

Correct: "Heat is escaping through the window" (or: "Heat is transferring from inside the warm room to the cold outside air through the window glass.")

Explanation: Cold is not a substance. It cannot "come in." Cold is just the absence of heat. What really happens: heat (thermal energy) moves from the warm room to the cold outside air. Heat always flows from high temperature to low temperature. The room feels colder because energy is leaving it.

Q9 — Metal vs. plastic chair: (6 marks)

Both chairs are at 22°C — the thermometer proves it. Your hand is warmer, about 37°C. When you touch metal, heat flows from your hand into the metal. Metal is a strong thermal conductor. Its particles are packed close together, so kinetic energy moves through it fast. Heat leaves your skin very quickly. When you touch plastic or wood (poor conductors, or insulators), heat still flows from your hand. But it moves so slowly that your skin barely notices. Your brain reads "heat leaving my hand fast" as "cold surface." Neither chair is actually colder. The metal just conducts heat away from you much faster.

Part C: The Particle Model and Heat (20 marks)

Q10 — Railroad track expansion: (4 marks)

The track will expand. It will get slightly longer, and the gap between sections will close. If there is no gap, the track will buckle. Reason: as temperature rises, particles in the steel gain kinetic energy and vibrate harder, pushing against each other. The average distance between particles grows a little. Across 25 metres — trillions of particles — this adds up to a measurable length increase. Temperature change = 35 − (−30) = 65°C. Steel expands about 0.000012 m/m/°C. So 25 m × 65°C × 0.000012 ≈ 0.02 m (2 cm) of expansion. Without an expansion gap, the track buckles under the compression.

Q11 — Particle diagrams: (6 marks)

Mark for:

  • Cold solid: particles close together, regular arrangement, small or no movement arrows; minimal spacing
  • Warm liquid: particles slightly farther apart, no fixed arrangement, moderate movement arrows in various directions
  • Hot gas: particles far apart, completely random, fast/large movement arrows; particles occupy entire box space

Q12 — Temperature plateau at 0°C: (4 marks)

At 0°C, the stove is still adding energy. But that energy goes into breaking the bonds holding the ice crystals together — the bonds between water molecules in the solid. Breaking bonds takes energy. It does not speed up particle movement. Temperature reflects average kinetic energy, or particle speed. Since the energy is going into breaking bonds, not speeding up particles, the temperature stays flat. Once all the bonds are broken and the ice has melted, there are no more bonds to break. Now added energy speeds up the particles again, so the temperature starts to rise.

Q13 — Power lines in Alberta: (3 marks)

Alberta winters can reach −40°C or colder. When metal cools, its particles slow down and pull closer together. This makes the wire contract and get shorter. If power lines were strung tight in summer, winter contraction would pull them very tight — and they could snap. Extra slack added in warm weather leaves room for winter contraction, so the wire doesn't go tight.

Part D: Modes of Heat Transfer (24 marks)

Q15 — Identify mode: (6 marks)

a. Conduction (spoon in soup — direct particle-to-particle contact)

b. Radiation (campfire at distance — infrared electromagnetic waves through air)

c. Convection (hot air rises and circulates — fluid movement carrying heat)

d. Radiation (Sun to Earth through vacuum — electromagnetic waves only)

e. Conduction blocked (oven mitt insulates — reduces conduction to hand)

f. Convection (lava lamp — density-driven fluid circulation)

Q16 — Convection cannot occur in solids:

In a solid, particles are locked in a fixed pattern. They cannot flow or change position — they can only vibrate in place. Convection needs the warmer, less-dense part of a fluid to move upward, carrying its energy with it, while cooler fluid sinks to take its place. In a solid, this kind of bulk movement can't happen. Convection needs a fluid — a liquid or gas — that can flow freely.

Q17 — Conductors vs. insulators:

  • Conductors: metals (copper, aluminum); particles closely packed with freely mobile electrons; energy transferred rapidly between adjacent particles. Examples: copper pipe, aluminum frying pan.
  • Insulators: wood, foam, air pockets in wool/down; particles loosely arranged with few mobile charge carriers; very slow energy transfer. Examples: fibreglass batt insulation, wooden spoon handle.

Particle structure: in metals, free electrons carry energy quickly from the hot end to the cool end. In insulators, electrons are bound to atoms, so there is no fast way to carry energy.

Q18 — Thermos design:

Feature Mode blocked How
Vacuum between walls Conduction and convection No particles = no medium for particle-to-particle transfer or fluid circulation
Silvered inner walls Radiation Shiny surfaces reflect infrared radiation back rather than absorbing and re-emitting it
Tight-fitting cap Convection Seals off air movement; prevents warm air from rising out of container

Q19 — Sun warms Earth:

Radiation. The Sun gives off electromagnetic energy — infrared, visible light, and ultraviolet. This energy travels through the vacuum of space with no medium needed. When the radiation reaches Earth's surface, it is absorbed and turned into thermal energy (heat). No particles are needed. The energy travels as waves.

Part E: Specific Heat and Insulation (20 marks)

Q20 — Specific heat capacity:

Specific heat capacity is the amount of energy (in joules) needed to raise the temperature of 1 gram of a substance by 1°C. Water has a much higher specific heat capacity than iron (water ≈ 4.18 J/g°C, iron ≈ 0.45 J/g°C). In practice, water heats up and cools down slowly. Iron heats up fast. That's why a small iron frying pan gets dangerously hot quickly, while a full pot of water takes a long time to boil.

Q21 — Alberta vs. Vancouver temperature swings:

The Pacific Ocean has a very high specific heat capacity. It stores huge amounts of solar energy in summer, then releases it slowly in fall and winter. This keeps Vancouver's air temperature mild. Vancouver rarely gets very cold in winter, because the ocean is releasing stored heat. It rarely gets very hot in summer, because the ocean is absorbing extra heat. Alberta is far from any large body of water. Its air and soil have low specific heat capacities. So they heat up fast in summer (up to +35°C) and cool fast in winter (down to −40°C). There is no thermal reservoir to soften these swings. The Rocky Mountains also block the mild Pacific air from reaching Alberta.

Q22 — Sand vs. water in same sunlight:

Sand has a much lower specific heat capacity than water. Both absorb the same amount of solar energy over the day. But sand needs far less energy to raise each gram's temperature, so it heats up to 50°C easily. Water needs about 4 times more energy per gram per °C. It absorbs the same solar energy but only reaches 20°C. Water also circulates — mixing cooler water from below into the surface — and some of it evaporates, which cools the surface further. Sand has no such cooling tricks.

Q23 — How insulation works:

Insulation slows the rate of heat transfer. It does not generate heat. A good insulator creates barriers — trapped air, or low-conductivity material — that cut down conduction through walls and convection through moving air. Trapped air pockets in wool, fibreglass batts, or foam are very poor conductors. Heat moves through them slowly. In winter, insulation slows heat loss from inside to outside. In summer, it slows heat gain from outside to inside. The warmth you feel under a blanket is your own body heat, kept in. The blanket itself makes no heat.

Q24 — Two insulation improvements:

Accept any two reasonable improvements — full solutions:

  1. Upgrade attic insulation (conduction): Heat rises. Weak attic insulation lets heat move through the ceiling into the cold attic. Thick batts of fibreglass or blown cellulose cut conduction a lot. This is the best improvement per dollar for Alberta homes.
  2. Install double- or triple-pane windows (conduction + radiation): Single-pane glass conducts heat out fast. Double-pane glass adds an air (or argon) gap between the layers, which is a poor conductor. A low-emissivity coating on the inner pane cuts radiation heat loss. Windows can cause 25–30% of a home's heat loss in an Alberta winter.

Part F: Synthesis (★) — answer-only

Q25 — Eco-home design brief:

Give 1 mark for each science idea that is correctly named and used. Mark for at least 3 ideas from the unit.

Examples: thick wall insulation (reduces conduction); triple-pane windows (reduce conduction and radiation); south-facing windows with a roof overhang (the low winter sun heats the home through radiation; the overhang blocks the high summer sun); thermal mass, like a concrete or stone floor (it stores daytime solar heat and releases it slowly at night — this uses specific heat capacity); airtight construction with HRV ventilation (stops convection air leaks); a light-coloured exterior or green roof (reduces radiation absorbed in summer). Each design choice must be linked to the heat transfer principle it uses.
