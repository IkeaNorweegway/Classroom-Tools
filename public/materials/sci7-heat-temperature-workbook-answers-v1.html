Q1 ★ Core Error analysis

Cluster 1 — Temperature and measurement · Apply

Student claim to evaluate

"Fort McMurray hit −40°C last night. That means it was basically at absolute zero. That's way colder than room temperature, which is close to zero Kelvin."

(a) Convert −40°C to Kelvin using K = °C + 273. Show your working.

Working space — K = °C + 273

(b) Convert "room temperature" (20°C) to Kelvin.

(c) Use your two answers to check the student's claim. Find two specific errors in what they said.

HintAbsolute zero is 0 K. What is that in Celsius? Is −40°C anywhere near 0 K?
Answer

(a) K = −40 + 273 = 233 K.

(b) K = 20 + 273 = 293 K.

(c) Error 1: −40°C is 233 K, which is 233 degrees above absolute zero, nowhere near 0 K. Absolute zero is −273°C. Error 2: room temperature is 293 K, not close to zero Kelvin. The two temperatures differ by only 60 K, and both are far above absolute zero.

Q2 ★★ Extended Error analysis

Cluster 2 — Heat vs. temperature · Evaluate

Student claim to evaluate

"I spilled a cup of boiling water (100°C) on my arm. My friend jumped into Sylvan Lake at 22°C. Obviously the cup was more dangerous. It was hotter."

(a) Is the student right that "hotter" means more dangerous? What is the student confusing about heat and temperature?

(b) Which one could transfer more total heat energy to a person's body — the cup or the lake? Use the cup-vs-bathtub analogy to explain your answer.

(c) Heat always flows from   to  . In both scenarios, which way is heat flowing compared to the person's body (37°C)?

Answer

(a) Partly. Temperature tells how fast the particles move on average (how hot), not how much heat an object can give. The student is mixing up temperature with total thermal energy.

(b) The lake holds far more total thermal energy (like the bathtub: more particles at a lower speed). But the lake is at 22°C, which is colder than the body, so it cannot transfer heat into the person at all. The cup is the one that transfers heat into the body, because it is hotter than skin. The cup is more dangerous because heat flows into the arm, not because it holds more energy.

(c) Heat always flows from hot (higher temperature) to cold (lower temperature). Cup: heat flows from the 100°C water into the arm. Lake: heat flows out of the 37°C body into the 22°C water.

Q3 ★ Core Diagram

Cluster 4 — Modes of heat transfer · Analyze

The picture below shows a cross-section of a thermos. Three parts are labelled with arrows. Use the picture to answer the questions below.

Fig. 1 — Thermos cross-section (schematic). Vacuum layer is between outer and inner walls.

(a) For each labelled feature, name which mode(s) of heat transfer it blocks. Fill in the table.

Feature Mode(s) blocked Brief explanation
A — Vacuum layer
B — Shiny inner wall
C — Plastic cap

(b) Explain why a shiny wall blocks radiation better than a dull or dark wall.

Answer

(a)

  • A — Vacuum layer: blocks conduction and convection. There are almost no particles in a vacuum, so there is nothing to pass vibrations along or to circulate.
  • B — Shiny inner wall: blocks radiation. The shiny surface reflects infrared back toward the liquid.
  • C — Plastic cap: blocks convection (warm air and steam cannot rise out) and slows conduction (plastic is a poor conductor).

(b) Shiny, smooth surfaces reflect most infrared radiation. Dull or dark surfaces absorb and give off more radiation, so they let more heat in or out.

Q4 ★★ Extended

Cluster 3 — Particle model applied to heat · Analyze

A railroad track is laid at 20°C. Each section is 18.6 m long. A 1 cm expansion gap is left between sections. On a July afternoon the track reaches 55°C.

Student claim to evaluate

"We don't need the gaps. The track might get a little longer. But it will just stay in place. No big deal."

(a) Use the particle model. The temperature rises from 20°C to 55°C. Explain what happens to the metal particles in the rail. Be specific about vibration and spacing.

(b) Why would removing the gaps make the track buckle? Use your particle-level explanation. What is the track trying to do, and what happens when it can't?

(c) A new engineer asks a question. Why must the gap be sized for the hottest expected summer temperature? Why not the temperature when it was installed? What would you tell them?

Answer

(a) As the rail warms from 20°C to 55°C, its particles vibrate faster and harder and push a little farther apart. The space between particles grows, so the whole rail expands (gets longer). A steel section this long grows by roughly 7–8 mm.

(b) Without gaps, each section pushes on the next as it expands. The rail is trying to get longer but has nowhere to go, so huge compression builds up. The track relieves it by bending sideways or upward: it buckles ("sun kink"), which can derail a train.

(c) The gap must hold the largest expansion the rail will ever have. That happens at the hottest temperature, not at the installation temperature. A gap sized for 20°C would close and the track would buckle on the first hot day.

Q5 ★ Core

Cluster 5 — Specific heat capacity · Apply

Two matching cups sit in direct sunlight. One is full of dry sand and one is full of water. Both start at 18°C. After 20 minutes you measure: sand = 45°C, water = 24°C.

(a) Which material has the higher specific heat capacity? How do you know from the data?

(b) Both cups received the same amount of energy from the sun. Use specific heat capacity. Explain why the sand temperature rose so much more than the water temperature.

(c) A student suggests filling a solar collector with sand instead of water: "Sand gets way hotter — it'll store more energy." Is the student right? Explain what specific heat capacity really tells us about storing energy.

Answer

(a) Water. It received the same energy but its temperature rose only 6°C, compared with 27°C for sand. A material whose temperature changes less for the same energy has the higher specific heat capacity.

(b) Specific heat capacity is the energy needed to raise 1 g of a material by 1°C. Sand needs about one fifth as much energy per degree as water, so the same sunlight raises its temperature about five times as much.

(c) No. A higher temperature does not mean more energy is stored. For each degree of warming, water stores about five times more energy than sand. A water collector stores more energy and gives it back more steadily.

Q6 ★★ Extended Alberta

Cluster 6 — Applications · Evaluate

The Red Deer River is downstream from a power plant. For three summers in a row, many fish have died there. The plant pulls in cold river water to cool its equipment. Then it releases the water 8°C warmer. The plant manager says: "Just wait — the river will fix itself in a few weeks."

(a) Warmer water holds less dissolved oxygen. Use the particle model to explain why warm water can't hold as much dissolved gas.

(b) The Red Deer River is a large body of water. Use specific heat capacity to explain why it takes a long time to cool back down. Even if the plant stops releasing warm water tomorrow, the river stays warm for a while. Why?

(c) Judge the plant manager's claim. Name at least two things that affect whether the river ecosystem recovers. Is "just waiting" a good scientific response? Explain.

Answer

(a) In warm water the particles move faster. Dissolved oxygen molecules gain energy too and escape from the water into the air more easily. So warm water holds less dissolved gas, and fish have less oxygen.

(b) Water has a high specific heat capacity, and the river has a huge mass of water. It stored a lot of thermal energy as it warmed, and it must lose all that energy to cool down. That takes a long time, even after the warm water stops.

(c) "Just waiting" is not a good scientific response. Recovery depends on things like: whether the warm discharge stops or is cooled first (cooling ponds or towers); whether oxygen levels return to normal; whether fish can return and reproduce (spawning season, upstream populations); and other stressors such as algae blooms. A scientist would monitor temperature and oxygen and fix the cause, not wait.

Q7 ★ Core Error analysis

Cluster 2 — Heat vs. temperature · Analyze

A common thing people say on a cold Alberta winter day: "Close the door — you're letting the cold in."

HintThink about what heat always does — which direction it always travels. Is "cold" a thing that moves?

(a) Use heat transfer words. Describe what is really happening when you open the door on a −30°C January morning in Edmonton.

(b) A student says: "Cold air blew in from outside and froze my feet." Rewrite this sentence correctly, using only heat transfer words.

(c) What is actually travelling across the doorway, and in which direction? Why can you never say "cold travels into the house"?

Answer

(a) When the door opens, heat flows out of the warm house into the −30°C air. Warm indoor air escapes out the top of the doorway, and cold outdoor air sinks in along the floor (convection). The room loses thermal energy and its temperature drops.

(b) "Heat flowed from my feet into the colder air and floor, so my feet lost thermal energy and felt cold." (Any version where heat leaves the feet.)

(c) Thermal energy (heat) travels, from inside to outside. "Cold" is not a substance or a form of energy; it only describes having less thermal energy. Only heat moves, and only from hot to cold.

Q8 ★★ Extended Diagram Misconception

Cluster 4 — Modes of heat transfer · Analyze

The picture shows three matching slabs. They are the same size. Each has the same temperature difference across it. The left face is 20°C (room temperature). The right face is 37°C (body temperature). Each slab is made of a different material.

Fig. 2 — Three material slabs with identical temperature difference. Arrow length indicates relative rate of heat flow.

(a) Rank the three materials from fastest to slowest at conducting heat. Use the particle model to explain your ranking. Why do metal particles transfer heat faster than wood or foam particles?

(b) You reach into a freezer and grab a metal tray and a plastic bag. The metal tray feels much colder. Use the picture and your particle model answer above to explain why. Both items are at the same temperature.

(c) This question is about a common wrong idea about heat and materials. Name the wrong idea. Explain exactly why it is wrong.

Answer

(a) Metal (fastest) → wood → foam (slowest). In metal the particles are packed tightly and pass vibrations along quickly (metals also have free electrons that carry energy fast). Wood's particles pass energy more slowly. Foam is mostly trapped air, with particles far apart, so it conducts very little.

(b) Both items are at the same freezer temperature. The metal tray conducts heat away from your hand much faster than plastic, so your skin cools quickly, and that feels "colder." Your skin senses how fast it is losing heat, not the object's temperature.

(c) The wrong idea: "Metal objects are colder than plastic or wood objects in the same place" (or "feeling cold measures temperature"). It is wrong because objects left in the same place reach the same temperature (thermal equilibrium). The metal only feels colder because it conducts heat away from the skin faster.

Q9 ★ Core Diagram

Cluster 3 — Particle model · Apply

The graph below shows a heating curve for water, from −10°C to above 100°C. Heat is added at a constant rate throughout.

Fig. 3 — Heating curve for water. Heat is added at a constant rate throughout. Phase change B is unlabelled — you will name it.

(a) During Phase change A (the flat part at 0°C), heat is still being added. But the temperature does not rise. Use the particle model. Where is the energy going, if not into raising the temperature?

(b) Label Phase change B. What is happening to the water particles at 100°C? Why does the temperature stop rising again?

(c) Predict: If you continued adding heat past 110°C (as a gas), would you see another flat plateau? Explain why or why not.

Answer

(a) The energy goes into breaking the attractions between water particles so they can leave their fixed places and slide past one another (solid → liquid). It changes the particles' arrangement, not their average speed, so the temperature stays at 0°C.

(b) Phase change B is boiling (evaporation / vaporization). At 100°C, the added energy separates particles completely so they escape as gas. The temperature stays flat until all the liquid has turned to gas.

(c) No. Water vapour has no further change of state to go through, so all the added energy now speeds up the particles. The temperature keeps rising steadily.

Q10 ★★ Extended Claim evaluation

Cluster 1 — Temperature and measurement · Evaluate

Two scientists are arguing about what "twice as hot" means:

Scientist A

"200 K is twice as hot as 100 K — the particles have twice the kinetic energy and are moving twice as fast."

Scientist B

"200°C is twice as hot as 100°C — you can see it right there on the thermometer."

(a) Convert both Celsius temperatures (100°C and 200°C) to Kelvin. Show working.

Working space

(b) Does Scientist A's claim make sense? Why does "twice as hot" only make sense when you measure in Kelvin?

(c) Does Scientist B's claim make sense? Use your conversions to explain the problem with doubling a Celsius temperature.

(d) In physical terms, what does "twice the temperature (in Kelvin)" actually mean about the particles?

Answer

(a) 100°C = 100 + 273 = 373 K. 200°C = 200 + 273 = 473 K.

(b) Mostly yes. Kelvin starts at absolute zero (no particle motion), so 200 K means twice the average kinetic energy of 100 K. "Twice as hot" only makes sense on a scale whose zero means zero energy. (Strictly, twice the energy does not mean twice the speed; the particles move about 1.4 times as fast. Accept answers that focus on energy.)

(c) No. 0°C is just the freezing point of water, not zero energy. 473 K ÷ 373 K ≈ 1.27, so 200°C has only about 27% more particle energy than 100°C, not twice as much.

(d) The particles have twice the average kinetic energy (energy of motion).

Q11 ★ Core Alberta

Cluster 6 — Applications and technology · Apply

An Edmonton homeowner is choosing between three wall designs. Edmonton winters reach −40°C. All three walls are 10 cm thick:

  • Design A: 10 cm solid concrete
  • Design B: 10 cm fiberglass batt insulation
  • Design C: 10 cm solid wood framing

(a) Use what you know about conduction. Rank the three designs from best to worst at slowing heat loss through the wall. Give reasons for your ranking — don't just list them.

(b) Which mode of heat transfer does fiberglass insulation mainly reduce? How does it do this? What is happening inside the insulation batt?

(c) A contractor tells the homeowner: "More insulation always keeps your house warmer." Is this true? What does insulation actually do? Does it make heat, or something else?

Answer

(a) Best to worst: B fiberglass → C wood → A concrete. Fiberglass traps tiny pockets of air, and air is a very poor conductor. Wood is a fairly poor conductor. Concrete is dense, with closely packed particles, so it conducts heat out fastest.

(b) Mainly conduction, and also convection. The fibres trap air in tiny pockets. Air conducts poorly, and air that is trapped cannot circulate to carry heat away.

(c) Not exactly. Insulation does not make heat; the furnace does. Insulation only slows the transfer of heat out of the house. Adding more helps less and less after a point, and heat can still escape through windows, doors and air leaks.

Q12 ★★ Extended Alberta

Cluster 5 — Specific heat capacity · Evaluate

Vancouver, BC (49°N) has much milder winters and cooler summers than Edmonton, AB (53°N). This is true even though both cities are at similar latitudes.

(a) Use the term specific heat capacity correctly. Explain how the Pacific Ocean helps keep Vancouver's climate mild. Why does the ocean resist big temperature swings?

(b) Edmonton has much bigger temperature swings than Vancouver (−40°C in winter, +35°C in summer). Vancouver rarely sees either extreme. Explain why. Your answer must use the term thermal mass or thermal reservoir.

(c) Prediction: Climate change is warming ocean surface temperatures. It is also making them more variable. Suppose the Pacific Ocean's heat storage became less steady — its surface temperature swung more from season to season. What would happen to Vancouver's climate? Be specific.

Answer

(a) Water has a high specific heat capacity. The Pacific absorbs huge amounts of energy in summer with only a small rise in temperature, and gives that energy back slowly in winter. The ocean resists big temperature swings, so the air over Vancouver stays mild.

(b) Edmonton is far inland, with no large body of water nearby to act as a thermal reservoir. Land and air have low thermal mass, so they warm up and cool down quickly. Vancouver's ocean acts as a thermal reservoir that buffers both extremes.

(c) Vancouver's climate would become less steady: hotter summer heat waves, colder or more variable winters, and bigger swings from season to season. Accept any specific prediction linked to the ocean's buffering effect weakening.

S1 Stretch

Clusters 1 + 3 — Temperature scales · Particle model · Evaluate / Create

A scientist is building a thermometer that must be accurate to 0.1 K near absolute zero. Room temperature is 293 K.

(a) Convert 0.1 K to Celsius. Show working. What does this tell you about how precise this instrument must be?

Working space

(b) Why do scientists prefer the Kelvin scale over Celsius near absolute zero? What would go wrong if scientists used Celsius to compare ratios near 0 K?

(c) At absolute zero, the particle model predicts that all particle motion stops. But physicists say we can never truly reach absolute zero. In one sentence, say why. Refer to what quantum mechanics says about the smallest amount of energy a particle can have.

Answer

(a) °C = K − 273.15, so 0.1 K = −273.05°C. The instrument must detect changes of a tenth of a degree right next to absolute zero, so it needs extreme precision and careful calibration.

(b) Kelvin is an absolute scale: 0 K means the least possible particle energy, so ratios mean something (2 K has twice the energy of 1 K). Near absolute zero, Celsius values are all about −273, so comparing ratios gives meaningless results (for example, dividing negative numbers).

(c) Particles always keep a smallest possible amount of energy (zero-point energy), so we can never remove all of it and reach exactly 0 K.

S2 Stretch

Clusters 4 + 6 — Heat transfer modes · Applications · Evaluate

A mug of hot tea (85°C) sits on a wooden table in a room at 22°C. Without touching it, list all three modes by which the mug loses heat.

For each mode, answer all three sub-questions:

Mode (a) What is physically happening? (b) What about the mug or the room causes this mode? (c) One design change to reduce this mode
Conduction
Convection
Radiation

Which one design change would cut the most total heat loss? Give reasons for your choice.

Answer
  • Conduction. (a) Fast-vibrating particles in the hot mug bump the particles of the table and pass energy to them. (b) The mug's base is in contact with the cooler table. (c) Put the mug on a cork coaster or another insulating pad.
  • Convection. (a) Air touching the mug and the tea's surface warms, rises, and is replaced by cooler air; steam (evaporation) also carries energy away. (b) The open top and the hot surface exposed to room air. (c) Put a lid on the mug.
  • Radiation. (a) The hot mug gives off infrared radiation in all directions. (b) Its hot, dull surface. (c) Use a shiny or double-walled (vacuum) mug.

Biggest single change: a lid. The open surface loses the most heat through convection and evaporation. Accept another choice if it is well argued.

Design a passive solar home for a family moving to Lethbridge, AB. Lethbridge has big temperature swings and lots of sunshine. It also gets frequent Chinook winds (sudden warm winter winds from the west). The home must keep heating costs low in winter AND cooling costs low in summer.

Give three specific design features. For each one, do three things. Name the heat science idea. Explain how it works. Explain how Lethbridge's climate (Chinooks, sunshine patterns) affects your choice.

Heat science principle:  

How it works:

Why Lethbridge's climate matters for this choice:

Feature 2:

Heat science principle:  

How it works:

Why Lethbridge's climate matters for this choice:

Feature 3:

Heat science principle:  

How it works:

Answer

Accept any three well-explained features. Examples:

  • Large south-facing windows with roof overhangs. Principle: radiation. How: low winter sun shines deep into the house and warms it; the overhang shades out the high summer sun. Lethbridge: very sunny, with a low winter sun angle, so free solar heating is reliable.
