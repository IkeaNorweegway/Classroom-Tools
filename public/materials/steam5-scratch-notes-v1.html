Concept 1: A Story Needs a Shape

I can: plan a story with a clear beginning, problem, solution, and end before I build anything.

Every story in this unit needs four parts: beginning → problem → solution → end. A storyboard is a set of drawn boxes that show what happens in each part, in order — it's the story's version of a sequence, planned on paper before any code exists.

A quieter misconception worth watching for in your own planning: a story doesn't get better just because more happens — more sprites, more effects, more scenes. It gets better when the beginning, problem, solution, and end are clear enough that someone else could follow it.

Concept 2: Events & Broadcasts

I can: use events and broadcasts to control the order things happen in my story.

An event is "when this happens, do this" — like "when this sprite is clicked" or "when the green flag is clicked." This is the same idea as the conditional you used with Dash in Unit 1, applied to a story instead of a robot.

A broadcast is a message one sprite sends that other sprites can listen for — it's how you make a scene change happen for everyone at once (a backdrop switching, a new character appearing) instead of trying to time every sprite separately.

Misconception

"Coding is the math part of this project, and art is the fun part — they're two separate jobs."

Why this fails: the same sequence → loop → conditional/event structure that moved Dash through a maze is exactly what moves this story from scene to scene. A beautifully drawn character with no working events is a picture, not an interactive story — and a perfectly coded story with no character design has nothing for anyone to connect with. Neither half works without the other.

Correct understanding: Code and art are doing the same job here — controlling what the audience experiences and when. Planning your character's look and planning your broadcasts are both design decisions about the same story.

Check Yourself — no peeking
  1. What's the difference between an event and a broadcast?
  2. How is a Scratch event similar to a conditional block on Dash?

Concept 3: Designing a Character on Purpose

I can: explain a specific design decision behind how my character or backdrop looks.

Every choice in a character's design — shape, color, size, expression — sends a message before the character says a word. A sketch on paper, done before building the sprite digitally, lets you try shapes and looks quickly without being slowed down by the software.

Connection

Think of a character from a show or game you know well. What's one design choice (color, shape, size) that tells you something about them before they even speak?

Concept 4: This Story Has a Real Job to Do

Capstone Constraint

I can: explain what a branch point is, and why sharing this story with a real audience changes how I build it.

This is the capstone unit, which means it carries the year's biggest constraint. Every story needs at least one branch — a moment where the story splits based on a choice, instead of running the same fixed sequence every time. A branch is built from the same event/broadcast tools you already know, just used to send the story down one of two paths instead of always the same one.

This story is also being shared with a real audience beyond the classroom — another class, or a family/school screening. That's not just a nicer ending; it's the same role a budget or a material limit plays in other units' constraints — it changes decisions you make while building, because someone outside the room who doesn't already know your story has to be able to follow it.

Check Yourself — no peeking
  1. What is a branch, and what coding tool is it built from?
  2. Why does having a real audience outside the classroom change how you build the story, not just how you feel about sharing it?
