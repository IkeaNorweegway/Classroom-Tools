Section 1: Temperature and Measurement

Learning target: I can distinguish between temperature and heat, read the Celsius and Kelvin scales, and convert between them.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Temperature A measure of the average kinetic energy (speed of motion) of the particles in a substance A room at 21 °C
Kelvin scale A temperature scale that starts at absolute zero; used in science Water freezes at 273 K
Celsius scale A temperature scale based on water freezing at 0 and boiling at 100 Body temperature is 37 °C
Absolute zero The lowest possible temperature, where particles have the least possible motion 0 K = −273 °C
Thermometer A tool that measures temperature A liquid thermometer on a classroom wall

Guided Notes

Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of particles in a substance. "Kinetic energy" means energy of motion (movement). When particles move faster, temperature increases (rises).

The Celsius scale:

  • Water freezes at 0 °C
  • Water boils at 100 °C
  • Normal body temperature: 37 °C
  • Room temperature: approximately 20 (accept 20–22) °C
  • A typical Alberta January day: approximately about −10 to −15 °C

The Kelvin scale:

  • Used in scientific contexts
  • 0 Kelvin (0 K) = absolute zero — the temperature at which particles have no (zero / the minimum possible) kinetic energy (theoretically no movement at all)
  • Absolute zero = −273 °C
  • Conversion: K = °C + 273
  • There is no negative temperature on the Kelvin scale (you can't go below zero movement)

Practice conversions:

Celsius Kelvin
0 °C 273 K
100 °C 373 K
-40 °C 233 K
20 °C 293 K
37 °C 310 K (body temp)

How thermometers work:

Liquid thermometers use the fact that liquids expand when heated and contract when cooled. The liquid rises up the tube as it expands. The scale was calibrated by defining two reference points: the freezing point of water (0 °C) and the boiling point of water (100 °C).

    Answer The average kinetic energy (speed of motion) of the particles in the substance.
  2. Convert: 25°C =   K and −10°C =   K
    Answer 25 °C = 298 K; −10 °C = 263 K
  3. What is absolute zero? Why can't a temperature be lower than absolute zero?
    Answer Absolute zero (0 K, −273 °C) is the temperature at which particles have the least possible motion. You cannot go lower because there is no more motion energy to remove.

    Answer Stretch: 0 °C is just the freezing point of water — particles still have plenty of energy. 0 K means no particle motion, so Kelvin numbers are in proportion to particle energy. A negative Celsius value in an equation would suggest "negative energy" or give a meaningless answer (e.g., doubling −10 °C). Kelvin has no negatives, so the maths works.

Section 2: Heat vs. Temperature

Learning target: I can explain the difference between heat and temperature using specific examples.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Heat Energy that is transferred from a warmer object to a cooler one Heat moving from soup into a spoon
Thermal energy The total kinetic energy of all the particles in a substance A bathtub of warm water has more than a cup at the same temperature
Heat transfer The movement of thermal energy from one place or object to another A hot stove warming a pan
Thermal equilibrium When two objects in contact reach the same temperature and heat stops flowing A drink left out until it reaches room temperature

Guided Notes

MISCONCEPTION — BEFORE WE START

"Heat and temperature are the same thing." The test: A cup of water at 90°C and a bathtub full of water at 90°C have the same temperature. But if you put your hand in each, the bathtub would take much longer to cool down. Why? The bathtub has far more thermal energy (heat energy) — more particles, each moving at the same average speed, so total kinetic energy is much higher. Temperature tells you how fast particles are moving on average. Heat tells you how much total energy is being transferred.

Heat is energy transferred (moving) from a hotter object to a cooler one. Heat is not stored in an object — it is what we call the transfer (flow / movement) of energy between objects of different temperatures.

Direction of heat flow: Heat always flows from hot (warmer) (higher temperature) to cold (cooler) (lower temperature) — never the other way, unless a machine forces it (like a refrigerator or air conditioner).

Thermal equilibrium: When two objects in contact reach the same temperature, heat stops transferring. They are in thermal equilibrium.

The lake and the cup:

Cup of water (80°C) Lake (80°C)
Temperature same same
Number of particles very few trillions × more
Total heat energy small (low) enormous (very high)
Which cools your burn faster if spilled? the cup — it holds so little total energy that it cools almost at once; the same amount of lake water would keep delivering heat —
MISCONCEPTION

"Cold travels — cold air comes in through the window." Why this is wrong: Cold is not a substance that travels. What happens is that heat (thermal energy) leaves your warm house through the window and is lost to the cold outside air. When a cold wind "hits" you, it is not cold travelling to you — it is your body losing (transferring) heat to the moving cold air faster. "Cold" is just the word we use for low heat energy; it doesn't move, arrive, or leave.

Self-check question: Your soup is hot. You wait 20 minutes and it's lukewarm. Is it because "coldness came in from the air"? What actually happened?

Answer No. Heat flowed from the hot soup into the cooler air, bowl, and table until the soup was close to room temperature. Nothing "cold" moved in

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. What is the difference between heat and temperature?
    Answer Temperature is the average kinetic energy of the particles (how fast they move). Heat is energy transferred between objects at different temperatures; it depends on how much matter is involved.
  2. Which direction does heat always flow? What happens when two objects reach the same temperature?
    Answer From hotter to cooler. When both reach the same temperature, heat transfer stops — thermal equilibrium.
  3. A cup of boiling water (100°C) and a swimming pool at 30°C — which has more heat energy? Explain.
    Answer The swimming pool. Its temperature is lower, but it has vastly more particles, so its total thermal energy is far greater.
  4. Correct this statement: "Open the door — you're letting the cold in."
    Answer "You're letting the heat out." Heat flows from the warm room to the cold outside; cold is not a substance that moves in.
  5. Stretch: You put a metal spoon in hot soup. After a minute the whole spoon is warm. Later you take it out and put it on a ceramic plate. After another minute, the spoon is cooler and the plate is slightly warmer. Explain both observations using your understanding of heat transfer and thermal equilibrium.
    Answer Stretch: In the soup: heat flows from the hot soup into the cooler spoon (conduction) until the spoon nears the soup's temperature. On the plate: the spoon is now the warmer object, so heat flows from the spoon into the cooler plate — the spoon cools and the plate warms until they reach the same temperature (thermal equilibrium).

Section 3: The Particle Model and Heat

Learning target: I can use the particle model to explain thermal expansion, contraction, and changes of state.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Particle model The idea that all matter is made of tiny particles that are always moving Particles in a solid vibrate in place
Thermal expansion Matter taking up more space when heated because its particles move farther apart A bridge getting longer in summer
Thermal contraction Matter taking up less space when cooled because its particles move closer together Power lines tightening in winter
Change of state Matter changing between solid, liquid, and gas as heat is absorbed or released Ice melting

Guided Notes

The particle model says: all matter is made of tiny particles that are always (constantly) in motion. The faster the particles move, the higher the temperature.

What happens when you heat a solid:

  1. Particles absorb energy and vibrate (move) faster
  2. Particles vibrate farther apart from each other
  3. The material expands — it takes up more space
  4. This is called thermal expansion

What happens when you cool a solid:

Particles slow down → move closer together → material contracts (shrinks) — takes up less space.

Real-world applications of thermal expansion:

  • Railroad tracks: expansion (small) gaps left between sections so tracks don't buckle in summer heat
  • Bridges: expansion joints allow the bridge to expand without cracking
  • Power lines: hung slack (loose / sagging) in summer; tighten in winter cold (if too tight, they would snap)
  • Thermometers: liquid (coloured alcohol or mercury) in the bulb expands up the tube

Changes of state:

Change Direction Energy change Particle change
Melting solid → liquid heat absorbed particles gain enough energy to overcome the attractive forces holding them in fixed positions
Freezing liquid → solid heat released particles slow until locked in fixed positions
Evaporation/boiling liquid → gas heat absorbed particles escape the liquid's surface entirely
Condensation gas → liquid heat released particles slow down and move close enough for attraction to pull them together into a liquid

Why does temperature stay constant during a change of state?

During melting or boiling, added heat goes into breaking (overcoming) the bonds/attractive forces between particles, not into speeding them up. That's why the temperature stays the same (stays constant) even though you keep adding energy — all the energy is being used to change the state, not raise the temperature.

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

    Answer Heating gives the particles more energy, so they move or vibrate faster and push farther apart. The material takes up more space.

    Answer Steel rails expand in summer heat. Without gaps they would have nowhere to go and would buckle.
  3. During melting, the temperature of an ice-water mixture stays at 0°C even while the stove is on. Why doesn't the temperature rise?
    Answer The added energy is being used to break the attractive forces holding the ice particles in place (changing state), not to speed the particles up. The temperature stays at 0 °C until all the ice has melted.

    Answer (a) Cold solid: particles close together in a regular pattern, small vibration marks. (b) Warm solid: same pattern, slightly farther apart, larger vibration marks. Particles are the same size and number in both.

Section 4: Modes of Heat Transfer

Learning target: I can describe conduction, convection, and radiation, and provide examples of each.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Conduction Heat transfer by direct contact, passed from particle to particle A spoon warming in hot soup
Convection Heat transfer by the movement of a fluid (liquid or gas) Warm air rising from a heat register
Radiation Heat transfer by electromagnetic waves; needs no matter Feeling the Sun or a campfire on your face
Insulator A material that transfers heat slowly Wood, foam, wool, air
Conductor A material that transfers heat quickly Copper, aluminum, steel

Guided Notes

There are three ways heat moves:

CONDUCTION — heat transfer through direct contact, particle-to-particle

How it works: Fast-moving particles in the hot region bump into slower moving particles in the cooler region → energy transfers → heat moves through the material.

Best in: solids (particles are close together and touch)

Good conductors: metals (copper, aluminum, iron) (metals — why pans are metal)

Poor conductors (insulators): non-metals such as wood, plastic, rubber, foam, air (wood, rubber, foam, air — why pot handles are not metal)

MISCONCEPTION

"Metal is colder than wood." The test: Touch a metal desk and a wooden desk that have been in the same room for hours. Metal feels colder — but measure them with a thermometer. They are at the same temperature. Metal feels colder because it conducts heat away from your hand much faster. Your hand is the warm object; the desk is the cooler one. Heat flows from your hand into the desk. Metal conducts so quickly that your hand loses heat fast — your brain reads this as "cold." Wood conducts so slowly that your hand barely loses any heat — your brain reads this as "warm."

CONVECTION — heat transfer through the movement of fluids (liquids or gases)

How it works:

  1. Fluid near heat source warms → particles move farther apart → fluid becomes less dense
  2. Less dense warm fluid rises
  3. Cooler, denser fluid sinks to replace it
  4. A convection current forms, continuously circulating

Examples:

  • Boiling water: hot (warm) water rises from hot bottom, cooler water sinks from surface
  • Home heating: warm air from radiator rises, cool air near windows sinks
  • Sea breeze: land heats up faster than water → warm air rises over land; cool ocean air moves in

Convection only works in liquids and gases — not in solids. (Solids can't circulate.)

RADIATION — heat transfer through electromagnetic (infrared) waves (no medium required)

How it works: All objects above absolute zero emit electromagnetic waves that carry energy. When these waves hit a surface, they are absorbed as heat (thermal energy).

Does NOT require matter — radiation travels through a vacuum (empty space). This is how the Sun heats the Earth across 150 million km of empty space.

Examples: the Sun, a campfire, a toaster or heat lamp, a hot stove element

Dark/matte surfaces absorb radiation better. Light/shiny surfaces reflect radiation.

Comparison table:

Mode Medium needed? Works in solids? Works in fluids? Works in vacuum?
Conduction Yes ✓ ✓ ✗
Convection Yes ✗ ✓ ✗
Radiation No ✓ ✓ ✓

Section 5: Specific Heat Capacity

Learning target: I can explain specific heat capacity and describe its effects on everyday phenomena including climate and cooking.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Specific heat capacity The amount of heat energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 gram of a substance by 1 °C Water needs far more energy than sand to warm up
Thermal reservoir A large body of material that stores a great deal of heat and releases it slowly The Pacific Ocean; a large lake

Guided Notes

Specific heat capacity: the amount of heat energy required to raise the temperature of 1 gram of a substance by 1 °C.

Some materials heat up (and cool down) faster (more quickly) than others. Water requires about 4 times more energy to change temperature by 1°C compared to most metals.

Material Relative specific heat Real-world implication
Water Very high Heats and cools slowly; stores huge amounts of energy
Metal (iron, aluminum) Low heats up and cools down quickly (a pan heats fast; a metal slide burns in the sun)
Sand / soil Medium-low heats quickly in the sun and cools quickly at night (hot beach sand, cold desert nights)
Air Very low temperature changes very quickly (big day-to-night swings; chinooks)

Why water is special:

  • Used as a coolant in car radiators and nuclear reactors — carries heat away efficiently because it can absorb a lot without its temperature changing much
  • Oceans act as a thermal reservoir — they store solar heat in summer and release it slowly in winter, moderating the climate of coastal regions
  • Inland Alberta has larger (more extreme) temperature swings than coastal BC because there is no large water body nearby to moderate temperature

Alberta examples of specific heat in action:

  • Sand dunes at Athabasca (yes, Alberta has dunes): sand heats to 50°C+ in summer sun while air stays cooler
  • Chestermere Lake vs. downtown Calgary: the lake moderates local temperature
  • Why soil freezes faster than rivers in autumn: soil has a much lower specific heat than water, so it has less stored heat to lose before reaching 0 °C; river water holds more heat (and keeps moving)
Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. What is specific heat capacity? Which has higher specific heat — water or metal?
    Answer The amount of heat energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 gram of a substance by 1 °C. Water has the higher specific heat.
  2. Why is water used as a coolant in car engines?
    Answer Water can absorb a large amount of heat with only a small rise in its own temperature, so it carries heat away from the engine efficiently.
  3. On a beach in summer, the sand is scorching but the water is cool. Explain this using specific heat capacity.
    Answer Sand has a low specific heat, so the same sunlight raises its temperature quickly. Water has a high specific heat, so the same energy raises its temperature only a little.
  4. Stretch: Vancouver has milder winters than Calgary even though it's at a similar latitude. Calgary has more extreme temperature swings. Using specific heat capacity and convection, explain this difference.
    Answer Stretch: The Pacific Ocean is a thermal reservoir: it absorbs heat all summer and releases it slowly in winter, and moving air (convection) carries that warmth over Vancouver. Calgary has no large body of water nearby; land and air have low specific heat, so they warm and cool quickly and temperatures swing widely.

Section 6: Insulation and Applications

Learning target: I can explain how insulation works and describe applications of heat knowledge in technology and daily life.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Insulation Material that slows heat transfer Fibreglass batts, a down jacket, foam
R-value A number that rates how well insulation resists heat flow; higher is better Attic insulation rated R-50
Thermal pollution Harmful warming of a lake or river by waste heat Warm cooling water released by a power plant

Guided Notes

Insulation works by slowing heat transfer — all three modes. It does not generate or store heat.

Insulation material Which mode it slows How
Trapped air (down, wool, foam) conduction and convection Air is a poor conductor; prevents convection currents because bubbles are too small
Fibreglass batts (home insulation) conduction (and convection) Poor conductor; traps still air
Vacuum (thermos) conduction and convection No medium means no conduction or convection
Shiny surfaces (space blankets) radiation Reflects radiation rather than absorbing it

R-value: a measure of insulation effectiveness (resistance to heat flow). Higher R-value = better insulator. Alberta building code requires higher R-values than milder provinces because of our cold winters.

Home insulation in Alberta: In a typical Alberta home, the biggest sources of heat loss are: windows and doors, attic/roof, and walls (also air leaks, basement). Proper insulation reduces heating costs and the amount of natural gas burned.

Thermal pollution: Industrial and power plants use water as a coolant and discharge warm water into rivers and lakes. Effects: dissolved oxygen levels in water decrease (warm water holds less dissolved oxygen) → fish and aquatic life are harmed.

A note on "keeping things cold":

An insulated bag keeps your lunch cold — but it doesn't create cold. It simply slows the transfer (flow) of heat FROM the warm air INTO the cool food. The insulation works equally well in both directions.

MISCONCEPTION

"Insulation keeps things warm." More precisely: Insulation slows heat transfer in both directions. A cooler bag keeps ice cold just as well as it keeps hot food hot — by slowing the flow of heat across the temperature difference. The insulation doesn't know which side is "warm."

Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. How does insulation work? Which heat transfer mode(s) does a vacuum stop?
    Answer Insulation slows heat transfer in both directions; it does not make heat. A vacuum stops conduction and convection (it does not stop radiation).
  2. Why does Alberta's building code require higher R-values than British Columbia's?
    Answer Alberta winters are much colder, so the temperature difference between inside and outside is larger and heat is lost faster. More insulation is needed to slow it.
  3. What is thermal pollution and what effect does it have on aquatic ecosystems?
    Answer Thermal pollution is the release of warm water (from industry or power plants) into rivers and lakes. Warm water holds less dissolved oxygen, which harms fish and other aquatic life.
  4. Stretch: Design the ideal insulated container for keeping ice cream frozen on a hot Alberta summer day (38°C). Identify the material for each layer, name which heat transfer mode each layer blocks, and explain why you chose it.
    Answer Stretch: Example design — shiny reflective outer layer (reflects radiation from the sun); thick foam or a vacuum layer (slows conduction and stops convection); tight-fitting insulated lid (stops warm air entering by convection and slows conduction); optional ice packs inside. Each layer must be matched to the mode it blocks, with a reason.
