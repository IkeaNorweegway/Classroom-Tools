Answer Problem: A single hottest year is one data point — still just a weather record. One year, even a record year, cannot by itself confirm a 30-year climate trend. One data point could be an outlier; climate requires a pattern sustained over decades.

Answer Ice melts → darker surface (open water/land) is exposed → darker surface absorbs more heat than ice → more heat causes more ice to melt → cycle continues.

Answer Error 1: The greenhouse effect and the ozone hole are NOT the same problem — they involve different gases and different atmospheric layers. Answer Each layer traps: air bubbles (containing the atmosphere of that time), dust, volcanic ash, pollen, and water molecules from the snowfall of that year.

Proxy data: Ice cores do not directly record temperature or CO₂ — they are an indirect record (a proxy). Scientists infer past conditions from what is preserved in the ice, rather than measuring directly with instruments at the time.

Q18 ★ Core Opens after Lesson 5 · climate-models · CS connection

A climate model is a computer program that simulates Earth's climate system.

What is an abstraction in computing?

A climate model is called an abstraction because it keeps   variables (like CO₂ levels, ocean temperature, and ice cover) while leaving out   details (like individual trees or waves).

How is a climate model similar to a force diagram from the Forces unit? (Think about what both keep and what both leave out.)

Why do scientists run climate models under different scenarios — for example, "what if emissions stay high?" and "what if emissions drop by 2030?"

Answer Abstraction: A simplified representation of a complex system that keeps only the essential features needed to answer a specific question, leaving out unnecessary detail.
Climate model keeps key variables; leaves out minor / trivial details.
Like a force diagram: A force diagram keeps only force arrows (direction and size); leaves out object colour, texture, material. A climate model keeps CO₂, temperature, ice cover; leaves out individual trees, waves, buildings. Both are abstractions that preserve what matters for the question being asked.
Multiple scenarios: To understand which future outcomes are possible under different decisions — "what if we cut emissions?" vs. "what if we don't?" Scientists use models to inform policy by comparing outcomes, not just predict one future.

A researcher studying plant populations in northern Alberta has two data sources:

Answer (a) Longer: Source B (Elder records). By 55 years (80 vs. 25).

Q26 ★★ Extended Opens after Lesson 3 · carbon-cycle-fossil-fuels · cross-unit link: Living Systems

(a) Where did the carbon in coal and oil originally come from? Trace it back using photosynthesis.

(b) Carbon was stored underground for millions of years. We are releasing it in decades. Why does the rate of release matter — not just the total amount?

(c) Deforestation reduces Earth's ability to re-absorb CO₂. Explain this using what you know about photosynthesis.

Answer (a) Carbon in coal and oil originally came from CO₂ in the atmosphere. Ancient plants and marine organisms absorbed CO₂ through photosynthesis → built carbon into their tissues → died and were buried → compressed over millions of years into fossil fuels. The carbon in coal started as atmospheric CO₂ that plants fixed into glucose.
(b) The rate matters because natural carbon sinks (oceans, forests) can only absorb CO₂ at a certain rate. When we release millions of years' worth of stored carbon in a few decades, the rate of release far exceeds the rate of natural removal → CO₂ accumulates in the atmosphere faster than it can be drawn down. Even if the total amount were the same over a longer time, the rapid release overwhelms natural buffers.
(c) Trees absorb CO₂ through photosynthesis — they are carbon sinks. When forests are destroyed, the trees can no longer absorb CO₂. Less forest = less photosynthesis = less CO₂ removed from the atmosphere, leaving human emissions less offset.

Q29 ★ Core Opens after Lesson 5 · TEK-Bow-River · FNMI

Elders from communities along the Bow River have recorded the timing of spring ice break-up across many generations. Scientists with instruments have data going back about 40 years. When the records are compared, both show that ice break-up now happens approximately two weeks earlier than it did 40 years ago.

Answer Why further back matters: A longer baseline allows scientists to determine whether the observed 2-week shift is outside the range of normal variation or just a short-term fluctuation. With only 40 years of instrument data, it is harder to rule out natural decadal cycles. Elder records extending further back add confidence that the change is a genuine trend.

(c) If methane traps heat so much more powerfully than CO₂, why do scientists focus so much on CO₂? (Hint: think about how much of each gas is produced.)

Answer (a) Any two human sources: cattle digestion (enteric fermentation); rice paddies; natural gas leaks during extraction/transport; landfills; wastewater treatment.
(b) Student is wrong / partially wrong: methane matters significantly. While CO₂ is the most abundant long-lived GHG from human activities, methane traps ~80× more heat per molecule over 20 years — so even smaller quantities have large warming effects. Methane is responsible for approximately 30% of human-caused warming to date. The student is right that CO₂ receives the most attention, but wrong to conclude methane "doesn't matter much."
(c) CO₂ is produced in far larger quantities than methane and stays in the atmosphere for centuries to millennia (vs. ~12 years for methane). The sheer volume of CO₂ emitted makes its cumulative effect the dominant long-term driver of warming, even though methane is more potent per molecule.

S1 ★★ weather-vs-climate + evidence quality + signal-vs-noise

A politician says: "We had record cold in January three years in a row in our province. That proves global warming has stopped."

Write a full scientific response to this claim. Your response must: identify the core error in the reasoning (what type of data is being misused) · explain what the word trend means and how you identify one · give a specific example of how a long-term warming trend can still contain cold years · describe what evidence you would actually need to determine whether warming has stopped.

Answer Core error: Three years of cold January data is weather evidence being used to make a climate claim. A trend requires at least 30 years of data; three data points cannot establish a trend.
Trend: A trend is the consistent direction of change over a long period, visible when short-term fluctuations are averaged out. You identify a trend by plotting data over many years and looking at the overall direction — not by selecting a few recent data points.
Example: A long-term warming trend can still contain cold years, cold decades, and even cold winters — because trend ≠ every data point going up. A river's long-term flow can be increasing even if some individual years have low flow.
Evidence needed to determine warming has stopped: 30+ years of temperature data showing no upward trend across multiple independent measuring sources (weather stations, ocean buoys, satellite); natural cause explanations that account for the full pattern; peer-reviewed scientific consensus shifting — none of which currently exists.

S4 ★★ CS climate model + greenhouse effect + causes

A climate model is a computational artifact — a program built to simulate Earth's climate system.

(a) A student says: "Climate models are just guesses. The computer doesn't actually know what will happen." Identify what the student is misunderstanding about what a model is and how scientists use it.

(b) A climate model's prediction matches the temperature record of the last 150 years almost exactly when human greenhouse gas emissions are included — but does not match when only natural causes are included. What does this tell us about the cause of current warming?

(c) Scientists run the same model under different emission scenarios. What scientific principle does this reflect — and why is it more useful than running the model once under a single assumption?

Answer (a) Misunderstanding: a model is not a "guess" — it is a mathematical simulation built on known physical laws (how gases absorb radiation, how oceans circulate heat, how ice reflects sunlight). Scientists use models to test hypotheses, not to guess. Models are validated against historical observations; if the model accurately reproduces the past, it has more credibility for the future. The student is confusing uncertainty in long-range projections with the model having no grounding.
(b) This tells us that human GHG emissions are the dominant cause of current warming. Natural cause models fail to reproduce the observed record — they can explain natural variation up to ~1850 but leave a gap after that. Only when human emissions are added does the model match reality. This is strong evidence that the "missing factor" is human influence.
(c) Running multiple scenarios reflects the scientific principle of testing multiple hypotheses / exploring a range of possibilities under different assumptions. It is more useful than one scenario because: the future is not determined — human decisions can change emission trajectories; showing a range of outcomes allows policymakers to understand the consequences of different choices; it prevents overconfidence in any single prediction.
