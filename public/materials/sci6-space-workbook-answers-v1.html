Q13 ★★ Extended opens after Lesson 3 · Pluto-reclassification · science-self-correcting

In 2006, the International Astronomical Union (IAU) reclassified Pluto from a planet to a dwarf planet.

A student says: "Scientists changed the rules in 2006. That just proves science is unreliable — you can't trust it."

Answer all three parts.

(a) What new evidence prompted the reclassification?

(b) What are the three conditions a body must meet to be called a full planet? Which one does Pluto NOT meet?

Three conditions:

Pluto does NOT meet condition:

(c) Write a response to the student. Explain why changing a classification when evidence demands it is a sign of science working well — not failing.

Answer (a) Discovery in the early 2000s of dozens of icy Kuiper Belt objects similar in size to Pluto. If Pluto remained a planet, all similar bodies would also need to be classified as planets — leading to an inconsistent, expanding list.
(b) Three conditions: 1. Orbits the Sun; 2. Large enough to pull itself into a round/spherical shape; 3. Has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit of other debris.
Pluto does NOT meet condition: 3 (shares the Kuiper Belt with many similar objects).
(c) Science is self-correcting. When new evidence (Kuiper Belt discoveries) demanded updating the classification system, scientists revised it — not to be arbitrary, but because a consistent, evidence-based definition is more reliable than historical familiarity. Changing a classification in response to evidence is science working as intended.

A comet is far from the Sun. It has no tail and looks like a dim, fuzzy smudge. As it travels closer to the Sun, a bright glowing tail develops.

Explain what causes the tail to appear:

Now explain why a comet's tail always points away from the Sun, not behind the comet like smoke from a rocket:

Hint: Solar wind — a stream of particles from the Sun — pushes material away from the Sun.
Answer Drawing: at R and at S the tail points directly away from the Sun (at R, up and slightly left; at S, down and to the right, ahead of the comet's direction of travel). Q19 ★★ Extended opens after Lesson 3 · ISS-living-in-space · scientific-methods

Astronauts on the ISS exercise for about two hours every day. They use special machines that push back against their muscles.

(a) Why must astronauts exercise so much in space? Use the word "microgravity" in your answer.

(b) Scientists on Earth study how astronaut bones and muscles change during long missions. How is this an example of scientists using a difficult situation to learn something useful?

(c) This research could help people on Earth too. Name one group of people who might benefit from this research and explain why.

Answer (a) In microgravity, there is little gravitational force pulling on muscles and bones. Without resistance, muscles weaken and bones lose density rapidly. Exercise simulates the mechanical stress that gravity normally provides continuously on Earth.
(b) Astronauts experience extreme muscle and bone loss under conditions impossible to replicate safely on Earth. Scientists use this as a natural experiment to study how the human body responds — generating data about adaptation that could not otherwise be collected ethically.
(c) Elderly people / patients with osteoporosis / people with muscle-wasting conditions — research on maintaining muscle and bone strength without gravitational load helps develop treatments for anyone who loses mobility or bone density.

(f) Comparison to natural satellite: correctly identifies gravity as what keeps both in orbit; identifies that artificial satellite is designed for a purpose while natural satellite formed through physical process
Deduct marks if emit/reflect is confused (e.g., satellite "emits" Earth's reflected light — the satellite's sensors detect reflected sunlight, not emitted light).
