Immigration After Confederation

Grade 7 Social Studies  ·  Practice Workbook — Answer Key  ·  Alberta Draft Curriculum 2026

Teacher Copy — Not for Students

Learning Outcome: Students examine immigration in post-Confederation Canada.

Guiding Question: Did post-Confederation immigration shape the development of the Canadian state?

Model answers and marking notes for every question. Each card says which notes section must be taught before the question opens. The student copy shows no section labels: announce which questions are open each session.

For opinion and judgement questions, the model answer is one strong example. Accept any position backed by accurate evidence from the unit.

Part A — Core and Extended Questions

Q1★ Core
Opens after Section 1 — Push and pull
AnswerPush: not enough land at home, war, poverty. Pull: free farmland, family already in Canada, work on the railway.
Q2★ Core
Opens after Section 4 — Laws that kept groups out
Answera) Chinese immigrants · b) immigrants from India · c) Black immigrants (Black farmers from Oklahoma).
Q3★ Core
Opens after Section 2 — The homestead
AnswerPay the $10 registration fee, live on the land, and build a house and plough part of it; after three years the 160 acres are theirs.
Q4★ Core
Opens after Section 6 — Fact or opinion?
AnswerFact · Opinion · Fact · Opinion. Example: a white settler or newspaper opposed to Black settlement, out of racist beliefs or fear of competition.
Q5★ Core
Opens after Section 3 — Building the Prairies
AnswerThey brought farming knowledge that turned prairie land into productive farmland; they built communities, churches and towns east of Edmonton; their wheat helped grow the economy.
Q6★ Core
Opens after Section 5 — Cause and consequence
AnswerRiots: rising Asian immigration / fear of job loss → new limits on Asian immigration (1908). Komagata Maru: the Continuous Journey Regulation → passengers turned away, ship forced out; deaths in India; 2016 apology.
Q7★★ Extended
Opens after Sections 1 and 4 — Two policies at once
AnswerThe government chose immigrants partly by race and where they came from: it recruited white settlers from Britain, Europe and the U.S., and made it very hard for Chinese immigrants to come.
Q8★★ Extended
Opens after Section 1 — Reading a poster
Answera) Free land / free homes. b) First Nations and Métis peoples who lived there; also groups the government didn't want. c) To attract many farmers quickly, to fill and hold the West.
Q9★★ Extended
Opens after Sections 2 and 3 — Land and settlement
AnswerHomesteads were on the Prairies, so land drew settlers there (often near the railway). Groups settled in blocs to share language, faith and help, e.g. Ukrainians east of Edmonton and Francophones near Saint-Paul.
Q10★★ Extended
Opens after Section 2 — "Free" land?
AnswerNot free: a $10 fee and three years of work were needed. Not empty: it was First Nations and Métis land, opened through the treaties. Correct: the government offered homesteads with conditions on land opened by treaty.
Q11★★ Extended
Opens after Section 3 — Opportunity
AnswerYes: settlers farmed the West and grew wheat for export, built towns along the railway, bought eastern goods, and filled the land so Alberta and Saskatchewan could become provinces in 1905.
Q12★★ Extended
Opens after Section 4 — Why restrict?
AnswerReasons given: protecting jobs and wages; underlying racist beliefs held by many politicians and voters. Example: the head tax applied only to Chinese immigrants, so discrimination was part of the law itself.
Q13★★ Extended
Opens after Section 5 — The Komagata Maru
AnswerMost will judge it unfair: the passengers were British subjects; the Continuous Journey rule was written so they couldn't meet it; they were held two months and forced out by the navy; Canada apologized in 2016.
Q14★★ Extended
Opens after Section 6 — Minority rights
AnswerTheir rights had been promised when Manitoba joined Canada, and the new English-speaking majority removed them. It was a constitutional crisis because it raised whether a province could take away rights protected when it joined, and it divided the whole country.

Part B — Challenge Zone

QS1★★ Extended
Opens after Sections 3, 4 and 5
AnswerOpportunity: homesteads and the wheat economy. Conflict: the head tax or the Komagata Maru. Connection: the government chose which groups got the opportunity, so the same policy of building the West included some people and excluded others.
QS2★★ Extended
Opens after Section 6
AnswerCheck facts in both (damage, dates, places) against other records; the headline's "invasion" is an opinion showing bias; the letter gives a first-hand account of damage (fact) and a perspective (opinion). Use both to see what happened and how different groups saw it.
QS3★★ Extended
Opens after All sections
AnswerStrong answers: yes, immigration filled the West, built the wheat economy and led to new provinces; but policies like the head tax, Continuous Journey and P.C. 1911-1324 were unfair to Asian and Black immigrants, and minority rights were taken away in Manitoba and Ontario.
