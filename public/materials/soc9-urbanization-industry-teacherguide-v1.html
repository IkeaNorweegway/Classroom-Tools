Teacher Guide

Urbanization and the Growth of Industry

Social Studies 9  ·  Time and Place  ·  Suggested pacing: 4–5 classes

Guiding question: How can urbanization shift and shape a society?

Learning outcome: Students examine the impacts of urbanization and the growth of industry on Canada.

Big idea for students to leave with: Growth is never neutral — the same postwar boom that built hospitals and highways also displaced farmland and disrupted ecosystems. Good citizens can hold both truths at once.

Discussion Prompts

Opener

"If you could only pick two of these for your town — jobs, a hospital, clean air, or protected wildlife — which two would you pick, and what would you have to give up?" Use this to introduce the idea that urbanization involves real trade-offs, not just "progress."

Extension

Ask students to name a Canadian city they know something about and identify one piece of "postwar-era" infrastructure (a bridge, a hospital, a highway) that is still in use. This grounds the unit in something tangible in their own experience.

Common Misconceptions to Watch For

Misconception
"Urbanization is just a modern problem — it wasn't a big deal in the past."

Reinforce that the environmental and social consequences of urbanization (pollution, land division, disrupted wildlife migration) were already occurring in the 1950s and 60s boom, even though public awareness and regulation were far more limited than today.

Misconception
"All immigrants to postwar Canada were welcomed easily."

Postwar immigration policy was still restrictive by today's standards, and displaced persons and later non-European immigrants often faced real barriers and discrimination. Avoid letting the unit's framing flatten this into an uncomplicated success story — this sets up the more critical treatment of immigration and multiculturalism policy in the Post-War Canada unit.

Using the Workbook

Part A (core, short answer, word bank provided) is appropriate for all students and can be completed independently or in pairs. Part B (extended, paragraph, real-world scenario, no word bank) is intentionally more demanding: students must apply — not just recall — unit concepts, and Q7 and Q8 deliberately ask them to connect the unit to a present-day issue (modern refugee migration; a modern pipeline debate). Consider having students share their Part B responses in small groups before submitting, since these questions have no single correct answer and benefit from hearing other perspectives.
