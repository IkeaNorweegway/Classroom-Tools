Teacher Guide

Urbanization and the Growth of Industry

Social Studies 9  ·  Time and Place  ·  Suggested pacing: 4–5 classes

Suggested length: 4–5 classes   Tier: 1   Unit colour: amber

Guiding question: How can urbanization shift and shape a society?

Learning outcome: Students examine the impacts of urbanization and the growth of industry on Canada.

Big idea for students to leave with: Growth is never neutral — the same postwar boom that built hospitals and highways also displaced farmland and disrupted ecosystems. Good citizens can hold both truths at once.

What this unit is really about: This unit is really two stories layered on top of each other — a demographic story (who came to Canada after 1945, and why) and a development story (what Canada built once they arrived, and what that building cost). Students will want to tell each story as simple good news: refugees found safety, and the boom built the modern city. Your job is to keep both stories complicated — postwar immigration policy was still restrictive and Canada was not uniformly welcoming, and the petroleum-and-infrastructure boom that built hospitals and highways also broke up farmland and disrupted wildlife corridors. The unit works when students can hold "this was a real benefit" and "this had a real cost" in the same sentence, about the same event.

Discussion Prompts

Opener

"If you could only pick two of these for your town — jobs, a hospital, clean air, or protected wildlife — which two would you pick, and what would you have to give up?" Use this to introduce the idea that urbanization involves real trade-offs, not just "progress."

Extension

Ask students to name a Canadian city they know something about and identify one piece of "postwar-era" infrastructure (a bridge, a hospital, a highway) that is still in use. This grounds the unit in something tangible in their own experience.

Common Misconceptions to Watch For

Misconception
"Urbanization is just a modern problem — it wasn't a big deal in the past."

Reinforce that the environmental and social consequences of urbanization (pollution, land division, disrupted wildlife migration) were already occurring in the 1950s and 60s boom, even though public awareness and regulation were far more limited than today.

Misconception
"All immigrants to postwar Canada were welcomed easily."

Postwar immigration policy was still restrictive by today's standards, and displaced persons and later non-European immigrants often faced real barriers and discrimination. Avoid letting the unit's framing flatten this into an uncomplicated success story — this sets up the more critical treatment of immigration and multiculturalism policy in the Post-War Canada unit.

Using the Workbook

Part A (core, short answer, word bank provided) is appropriate for all students and can be completed independently or in pairs. Part B (extended, paragraph, real-world scenario, no word bank) is intentionally more demanding: students must apply — not just recall — unit concepts, and Q7 and Q8 deliberately ask them to connect the unit to a present-day issue (modern refugee migration; a modern pipeline debate). Consider having students share their Part B responses in small groups before submitting, since these questions have no single correct answer and benefit from hearing other perspectives.

Lesson Sequence

Class 1 — Pre-Knowledge Check & Push/Pull Factors
Baseline: what do students already think?
Show The four Pre-Knowledge Check questions on notes p. 1 (why people leave one country for another; what Canada looked like right after 1945; an Alberta industry and why it grew here; can a place grow "too fast"). Have students answer cold, no notes, no discussion yet.
Say "There are no wrong answers on this page. I want to know what you already think before we start, so at the end of the unit you can see how much your thinking changed — or didn't."
Notes Notes p. 1, Pre-Knowledge Check. Tell students explicitly to keep this page — the Unit Synthesis at the end sends them back to it.
Push and pull factors, and who displaced persons were
Show The guiding question banner, then ask directly: "Why do people leave one country to move to another?" Collect answers on the board before naming push/pull as the formal vocabulary.
Say "Migration is almost never just one decision. A push factor is something in your home country that makes you want to leave — war, persecution, poverty. A pull factor is something about the destination that makes it attractive — safety, jobs, family already there. For most migrants, both are happening at the same time."
Cue Use the notes' "Think about it" prompt: for a Holocaust survivor arriving in Canada in 1948, what might the push have been? What might the pull have been? One sentence each.
Notes Notes Section 1 (push/pull factors, displaced persons, pluralistic society). Feeds workbook Q1(a) and Q2.
Class 2 — Urbanization and the Growth of Industry
The postwar boom and what it built
Show A photo set contrasting 1950s Calgary or Edmonton with the same city today (per the suggested-sequence table). Ask students what changed and what stayed the same.
Say "Urbanization means a growing share of the population lives in cities instead of rural areas. After the war, returning soldiers, new immigrants, and a consumer boom all hit Canada's cities at once. That growth didn't just make cities bigger — it changed what Canada built: roads and schools, oil and gas exploration, hydroelectric dams, and factories making cars and paper."
Cue Have students complete the notes' matching table (which industry powers new suburbs using dammed rivers; which drills and refines a resource central to Alberta's economy; and so on) before you confirm answers as a class.
Notes Notes Section 2 (urbanization, public infrastructure, petroleum industry). Feeds workbook Q1(b) and Q3.
Alberta connection: cities and the petroleum industry grew together
Show The notes' Alberta connection box: as Calgary and Edmonton expanded, they needed the energy, jobs, and materials oil and gas provided — and the industry needed a growing urban workforce.
Say "This wasn't two things happening side by side — it was one feedback loop. The city grew because the industry paid, and the industry grew because the city supplied workers. That's worth remembering when we get to the pipeline scenario later in the workbook."
Cue Ask: who might not have shared equally in the benefits of that relationship? (Push students past "everyone benefited" — not every worker, neighbourhood, or landowner benefited the same amount.)
Notes Notes Section 2 connection box. This sets up workbook Q8 (the pipeline scenario) later in the unit.
Class 3 — Environmental Consequences
Growth is never free
Show The source box quote: "Urbanization impacts a country's population, economy, and environment." (Alberta Social Studies 9 draft curriculum, 2026)
Say "As cities and industries expanded after the war, they left lasting marks on the land: pollution from factories and vehicles, farmland and wilderness converted into subdivisions and industrial sites, large natural areas broken into smaller parcels, and roads and fences disrupting wildlife migration routes animals had used for generations."
Cue Have students answer the source box task in 2–3 sentences: one specific example each for population, economy, and environment.
Notes Notes Section 3 (pollution, land consumption, land division, wildlife migration changes). Feeds workbook Q4.
Correcting the "this is a modern problem" misconception
Show Ask the class directly: "Did pollution and habitat disruption start with modern industry, or were they already happening in the 1950s boom?"
Say "These environmental costs were already visible during the first postwar urban boom — they were just less discussed and far less regulated than they are today. Urbanization causing environmental harm isn't a new story; our awareness of it is what's changed."
Notes Notes Section 3 misconception box; retrieval check stretch question (do the benefits of urbanization outweigh its environmental costs?).
Class 4 — Workbook Practice
Part A: core questions, in class
Show The word bank at the top of Part A (push factor, pull factor, displaced persons, urbanization, pluralistic, petroleum industry, public infrastructure, hydroelectricity).
Say "Part A is short answer and you have the word bank — use it. I want to see you can define and apply these terms accurately before we move to writing without support."
Notes Workbook Q1–Q5, completed independently or in pairs in class.
Part B: extended questions, no word bank
Show The Fort Larkin scenario (Q6): a town of 2,000 in 1946 that grew to 18,000 by 1960 after oil was found nearby — new roads, a hospital, and a school were built, but ranchers lost grazing land and a wetland used by migrating birds was drained.
Say "Notice this question doesn't ask you to just describe what happened in Fort Larkin — it asks whether the growth was good for the town overall. That means you have to weigh benefits against costs and take a position, using at least two unit terms to back it up."
Cue Preview Q7 (comparing a modern refugee to a 1945–51 displaced person) and Q8 (the pipeline scenario) as homework or a second period; consider a small-group share-out before submission since these have no single correct answer.
Notes Workbook Q6, Q7, Q8 (Part B, extended, paragraph response).
Class 5 — Synthesis
Bringing it together
Show The Unit Synthesis box: answer the guiding question in a 5–7 sentence paragraph using at least three unit vocabulary terms.
Say "A strong paragraph here doesn't just define urbanization — it traces a chain: immigration changed who lived in Canadian cities, urbanization changed what industries grew, and industrial growth changed the land itself. Show me that chain, with your own vocabulary."
Cue Send students back to their Pre-Knowledge Check answers from Class 1: what would they change or add now?
Notes Notes Unit Synthesis box; closes the loop with the Class 1 Pre-Knowledge Check.

Discussion and Source Activity Notes

Push/Pull Factor Sort (Class 1):

  • Write a mix of conditions on cards or a slide (war, poverty, persecution, jobs, safety, family already there, land availability) and have students sort each into "push" or "pull."
  • Include at least one card that could plausibly go either way (e.g., "better schools") to force discussion about perspective — a push for the person leaving may look like a pull from the destination's side.
  • Close by connecting back to the displaced-persons content: for a specific person or group, which cards would apply as push, and which as pull?
  • Keep this activity short (10–15 minutes) — it's a warm-up for the guided notes, not a replacement for them.

Photo Analysis: Postwar City vs. Today (Class 2):

  • Pair a 1950s photo of Calgary or Edmonton (or another Alberta city) with a modern photo of roughly the same location.
  • Ask students to list three concrete differences before discussing why — resist letting the conversation jump straight to abstractions like "it got bigger."
  • Push students to name what industries or infrastructure they can identify in each photo (roads, refineries, housing density) and connect those to the notes' list of postwar industries.
  • Use this as a bridge into the Alberta connection box: ask what evidence in the photos suggests a relationship between the city's growth and the oil and gas industry.

Source Analysis: "Urbanization impacts a country's population, economy, and environment" (Class 3):

  • Have students work in pairs to generate one concrete unit example for each of the three categories (population, economy, environment) before writing their individual response.
  • Watch for students who default to only one category (usually environment) — prompt them back to the source's full claim, which requires all three.
  • This activity pairs naturally with the misconception correction: ask whether their environmental example could have applied equally in the 1950s, not just today.

Assessment Notes

Formative check: Workbook Part A (Q1–Q5) and the retrieval-check questions at the end of each notes section are the fastest formative signal — they're short, word-bank supported for Part A, and catch vocabulary gaps before students move on to the paragraph-length Part B work, where those gaps are much more costly.

What a strong answer looks like: For Q6 (Fort Larkin), a strong answer takes a clear position on whether the growth was good "overall," uses at least two unit terms accurately (e.g., public infrastructure, land consumption, pull factors) and applies them to specific details in the scenario rather than restating them generically, and acknowledges both the benefits (roads, hospital, school) and the costs (lost grazing land, drained wetland) rather than picking only one side.

Watch for: Students who list Fort Larkin's benefits or costs without weighing them against each other — a strong answer takes a position, a weak one just summarizes the scenario. Also watch for unit vocabulary dropped into a sentence without being connected to a specific detail in the case (e.g., naming "land consumption" without saying what land, for what purpose).
