Section 1: Cell Theory and Plant Cells

Learning target: I can state the three tenets of cell theory and identify plant cell structures visible under a light microscope.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Cell theory The idea that all living things are made of cells, cells are the basic unit of life, and all cells come from other cells An onion and a person are both made of cells
Cell wall The rigid outer layer of a plant cell that gives support and shape The stiff outline seen in onion skin cells
Cell membrane The thin layer just inside the cell wall that controls what enters and leaves the cell Lets water in, keeps the cell contents in
Vacuole A large storage space in a plant cell that holds water and keeps the cell firm A wilted plant has vacuoles low on water
Chloroplast The green structure where photosynthesis happens Green ovals in a leaf cell
Nucleus The control centre of the cell; holds the DNA Dark round body in an onion cell

Guided Notes

Cell Theory — three core ideas that all biology is built on:

  1. All living things are made of cells.
  2. Cells are the basic unit of life.
  3. All cells come from pre-existing (other living) cells.
Why cell theory matters:

Before 1839, most biologists thought complex organisms were built from a mix of specialized tissues and simple materials. Schleiden (plants) and Schwann (animals) unified all of biology under one principle: everything living is cellular.

Plant cells under a microscope — key structures:

Plant cell — all major organelles shown without labels. Use the table below to identify each structure by colour and position.

Structure What it looks like What it does
Cell wall thick outer (outermost) border; rigid Provides support (strength, protection) and shape; made of cellulose
Cell membrane Inside the cell wall Controls what moves (passes) in and out of the cell
Central vacuole Large central space (takes up ~90% of plant cell) Stores water (cell sap), maintains cell pressure (turgor), stores waste
Chloroplasts Green, oval bodies Site of photosynthesis; contain chlorophyll
Nucleus Dark, round body Controls cell activities; contains DNA (genetic information)

Plant cell vs. animal cell:

Feature Plant cell Animal cell
Cell wall ✓ present ✗ absent
Large central vacuole ✓ ✗ (small vacuoles only)
Chloroplasts ✓ (in green cells) ✗
Cell membrane ✓ ✓
Nucleus ✓ ✓

Microscopy note: At light microscopy, you can see cell wall, cell membrane outline, central vacuole, nucleus, and chloroplasts (in green tissues). You cannot see individual molecules or most organelle structures.

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. State the three tenets of cell theory.
    Answer (1) All living things are made of cells. (2) Cells are the basic unit of life. (3) All cells come from pre-existing cells.
  2. Name the structure that: (a) controls what enters the cell   (b) contains the cell's DNA   (c) is only found in plant cells and is bright green  
    Answer (a) cell membrane (b) nucleus (c) chloroplast
  3. What does the central vacuole do? (Two functions)
    Answer Any two: stores water; keeps the cell firm (turgor pressure); stores waste.
  4. A student observes a cell under a microscope. It has a rigid outer wall, a large central space, and green oval bodies. Is this a plant cell or an animal cell? How do you know?
    Answer Plant cell — the rigid outer wall is a cell wall, the large central space is the central vacuole, and the green oval bodies are chloroplasts. Animal cells have none of these.
  5. Stretch: Why do you think animal cells don't have a cell wall? Consider what animal cells need to do that plant cells typically don't.
    Answer Stretch: Animal cells need to move, change shape, and squeeze past each other (muscle cells contract, blood cells travel); a rigid wall would prevent this. Animals get support from skeletons instead. Plants stay in one place and use cell walls for support.

Section 2: Plant Systems and Vascular Tissue

Learning target: I can describe the functions of the root and shoot systems and explain how vascular tissue moves materials through a plant.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Root system The underground part of the plant that absorbs water and minerals, anchors the plant, and stores food Carrot taproot; grass fibrous roots
Shoot system The above-ground part of the plant: stem, leaves, flowers, fruit The stalk, leaves, and head of a wheat plant
Vascular tissue The tubes that carry water and food through a plant Veins in a leaf; strings in celery
Xylem Tubes that carry water and minerals up from the roots Dyed water rising in a celery stalk
Phloem Tubes that carry sugars from the leaves to the rest of the plant Maple sap carrying sugar

Guided Notes

Two systems, one plant:

System What it includes Main functions
Root system roots, root hairs absorb water and minerals, anchor the plant, store food
Shoot system stem, leaves, flowers, fruit support (hold leaves up to the light), photosynthesis, reproduction

Root system details:

  • Root hairs: tiny extensions that massively increase surface area for water absorption
  • Taproot vs. fibrous roots: taproots (carrot, dandelion) go deep; fibrous roots (grasses) spread wide
  • Roots also store food: carrot, beet (name two crops where we eat the root)

Shoot system details:

  • Stems: provide support; transport materials; some store food (potato stem tubers)
  • Leaves: primary site of photosynthesis; blade captures light; petiole connects blade to stem
  • Flowers: reproductive structures (covered in Section 4)

Vascular tissue — the plant's transport system:

Tissue Carries Direction Think of it as
Xylem water and dissolved minerals from roots to leaves (shoots) supply pipes going up
Phloem food (sugars) made by photosynthesis from leaves to the rest of the plant (roots, fruit, growing tips) distribution pipes going down
Connection:

Xylem and phloem together form the vascular system. "Vascular" comes from the Latin for vessel — the same root word used in cardiovascular (heart and blood vessels). A plant's vascular system is analogous to the circulatory system in animals, but it uses passive forces (capillary action, osmosis, pressure) rather than a pump.

Why vascular tissue is important in plant evolution: Early plants (mosses, liverworts) have no vascular tissue — they must remain close (low, small) to ground water. Vascular plants can grow taller because they can transport water up from the ground regardless of how far above the soil they are.

Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. Name two functions of the root system and two functions of the shoot system.
    Answer Root (any two): absorbs water and minerals, anchors the plant, stores food. Shoot (any two): support, photosynthesis, reproduction.
  2. Complete the table: Xylem carries   from   to  . Phloem carries   from   to  .
    Answer Xylem carries water (and minerals) from roots to leaves. Phloem carries food (sugars) from leaves to the rest of the plant.
  3. A plant is placed in water dyed with blue food colouring. After 24 hours, the stem is cut and the inside shows blue dye. Which tissue carried the dye — xylem or phloem? Why?
    Answer Xylem — it carries water upward from the roots, and the dye is dissolved in the water.
  4. Why can vascular plants grow taller than non-vascular plants like mosses?
    Answer Vascular plants have xylem to carry water up to any height. Mosses have no transport tubes, so every part must stay close to the water source.
  5. Stretch: A fungus attacks the phloem of a tree. Predict what will happen to (a) the leaves and (b) the roots over the following weeks. Explain why.
    Answer Stretch: (a) The leaves keep making sugar at first but cannot send it away. (b) The roots receive no sugar; they have no chloroplasts and cannot make their own, so they starve and die. Once the roots fail, they stop supplying water, and the leaves then wilt and die too.

Section 3: Photosynthesis

Learning target: I can describe the process of photosynthesis including inputs, outputs, and its importance to ecosystems.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Photosynthesis The process plants use to make food (glucose) from carbon dioxide and water using light energy A leaf making sugar in sunlight
Chlorophyll The green pigment in chloroplasts that captures light energy Why leaves are green
Glucose The sugar plants make as food; their energy source and building material Stored as starch in a potato
Carbon dioxide (CO₂) A gas in the air that plants take in through their leaves The gas we breathe out

Guided Notes

MISCONCEPTION — BEFORE WE START

"Plants get their food from the soil through their roots." Why this fails: In 1648, Jan Baptist van Helmont planted a willow seedling in 90 kg of dry soil. After 5 years, the willow gained 74 kg of mass. The soil lost only 57 grams. Where did the 74 kg come from? Not the soil — mostly from water and carbon dioxide in the air. This experiment showed that plants build their mass from air, not soil. Soil provides minerals dissolved in water, but the food (energy) a plant runs on comes from photosynthesis.

The photosynthesis equation:

Fig. — Photosynthesis equation. Fill in the three blanks: where does CO₂ come from? Where does H₂O come from? Where is O₂ released?

Fill in the blanks:

  • CO₂ comes from: the air via tiny pores in leaves
  • Water comes from: the soil via roots and xylem
  • Light energy is captured by: chlorophyll, the green pigment in chloroplasts
  • Glucose is: the food (sugar) the plant uses for energy and growth; stored as starch
  • Oxygen is: a by-product (waste product) released into the atmosphere — vital for all aerobic life

Where does photosynthesis happen? In the chloroplasts of the green parts of plants — primarily in leaves.

Why photosynthesis matters to ecosystems:

Photosynthesis is the entry point of energy into almost every food chain on Earth. Producers convert light (solar) energy into chemical energy that all consumers depend on. Without photosynthesis, almost no life on Earth could survive.

Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. Complete: Photosynthesis uses  ,  , and   to produce   and  .
    Answer Photosynthesis uses carbon dioxide, water, and light energy to produce glucose and oxygen.
  2. Where exactly in the cell does photosynthesis take place?  
    Answer In the chloroplasts.
  3. Why do plants appear green?  
    Answer Chlorophyll absorbs most colours of light but reflects green, so leaves look green.
  4. A student says "photosynthesis makes plants breathe." Correct this misunderstanding precisely — what does photosynthesis actually do?
    Answer Photosynthesis is not breathing. It is how a plant makes its food: it uses light energy to turn carbon dioxide and water into glucose, releasing oxygen as a by-product.
  5. Stretch: A plant is placed in a sealed container. Over time, CO₂ levels drop and O₂ levels rise. The plant is healthy. Then the plant is kept in total darkness for two weeks. Predict what happens to CO₂ and O₂ levels. Explain using what you know about photosynthesis.
    Answer Stretch: In darkness photosynthesis stops, so the plant no longer removes CO₂ or releases O₂. The plant still uses its stored food for energy, so CO₂ rises and O₂ falls. After two weeks the plant is weakened and may die as its stored glucose runs out.

Section 4: Plant Reproduction

Learning target: I can describe sexual and asexual reproduction in plants and explain the advantages of each strategy.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Sexual reproduction Reproduction that combines sex cells from two parents; offspring are genetically different A canola seed formed after pollination
Asexual reproduction Reproduction from one parent with no sex cells; offspring are identical to the parent A strawberry runner
Pollination The transfer of pollen from the anther to the stigma A bee carrying pollen between flowers
Fertilization The joining of the pollen's sex cell with the egg to form a seed A fertilized ovule becoming a pea
Germination When a seed begins to grow into a new plant A bean sprouting
Vegetative reproduction Asexual reproduction from a plant part such as a stem, root, or leaf Potato tubers, cuttings

Guided Notes

Two strategies:

Strategy How it works Offspring are... Advantage
Sexual reproduction Two parents combine sex cells (pollen and egg) Genetically different from parents genetic variation; adaptation to change
Asexual reproduction One parent, no sex cells Genetically identical to parent fast; needs only one parent (no pollinator needed); rapid colonization of space

Sexual reproduction — the flower's job:

Flowers are reproductive structures, not decoration. Their appearance, colour, and scent evolved to attract pollinators.

Flower part Function
Stamen (anther + filament) Produces pollen
Pistil (stigma + style + ovary) Receives pollen; contains ovules (eggs)
Petals attract pollinators
Sepals Protect the developing (unopened) flower bud

Pollination = transfer of pollen from anther to stigma of the same species. Agents: wind, bees, butterflies, birds, bats.

Fertilization = pollen reaches the egg → a fertilized egg (zygote / embryo) forms → develops into a seed inside the ovary, which becomes fruit.

Seed dispersal strategies:

Method Adaptation Example
Wind lightweight, wings or fluff dandelion (also maple, poplar/cottonwood fluff)
Animal (eaten) nutritious fruit; seeds survive digestion saskatoon berry (also raspberry, chokecherry, apple)
Animal (hitchhike) hooks or burrs burdock
Water buoyant coconut
Mechanical pod explodes caragana (also pea pods, lupine, touch-me-not)

Asexual (vegetative) reproduction — no flowers needed:

Method How it works Plant example
Runners (stolons) horizontal stems send out new plants strawberry
Bulbs underground modified stem with stored food tulip, garlic
Tubers underground stem that stores starch potato
Cuttings stem piece roots when placed in soil willow (also geranium, coleus)
MISCONCEPTION

"Cloning and asexual reproduction are unnatural — only done in a lab." Why this is wrong: Vegetative reproduction is a completely natural strategy used by hundreds of plant species. Strawberry runners, aspen groves (which are often one connected organism), and potato tubers all reproduce asexually in the wild. Lab cloning of plants is an extension of a strategy plants evolved millions of years ago.

Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. What is the function of the stamen? The pistil?
    Answer Stamen: produces pollen. Pistil: receives pollen and contains the ovules, which become seeds after fertilization.
  2. Name two asexual reproduction strategies and give a plant example of each.
    Answer Any two with examples: runners (strawberry); bulbs (tulip, garlic); tubers (potato); cuttings (willow, geranium).
  3. A plant produces seeds with hooks that stick to animal fur. What dispersal strategy is this? Why is it advantageous?
    Answer Animal dispersal by hitchhiking (hooks or burrs). The seeds are carried far from the parent plant, which reduces competition and spreads the species to new areas.
  4. Why is genetic variation (produced by sexual reproduction) beneficial to a species over time?
    Answer Variation means individuals have different traits. When conditions change (disease, drought, climate), some individuals are likely to survive and reproduce, so the species continues.
  5. Stretch: An aspen grove in Alberta covers 20 hectares. All the trees are genetically identical and connected underground by one root system. Is this one organism or many? What are the advantages and risks of this strategy?
    Answer Stretch: Biologically it is one organism — one root system and one set of genes — even though it looks like many trees. Advantages: spreads quickly, shares water and nutrients, regrows after fire from the roots. Risk: no genetic variation, so one disease or pest could kill the entire grove.

Section 5: Plants for Food and Fibre — Alberta Agriculture

Learning target: I can identify crops grown for food and fibre in Alberta and describe their uses before and after processing.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Food crop A plant grown to be eaten by people or livestock Wheat, canola, peas
Fibre crop A plant grown for its fibres, used to make materials Flax (linen), hemp (rope, fabric)
Processing Changing a harvested crop into a usable product Milling wheat into flour
Monoculture Growing a single crop species over a large area A quarter section planted entirely in canola

Guided Notes

Alberta is a major agricultural province:

Crop Category Before processing use After processing use
Wheat Food — grain whole grain (kernels) — animal feed, seed, or cooked whole flour → bread, pasta
Canola Food — oilseed seed — used as seed for planting; crushed to release the oil canola oil, biodiesel
Barley Food — grain animal feed malt for brewing; pearl barley; flour
Peas/lentils Food — pulse whole dried seeds eaten directly (soups, stews) protein powder, food
Flax Food + fibre seeds eaten whole (flaxseed); straw from the stem linseed oil; linen fabric
Hemp Food + fibre seeds (edible) hemp oil and protein; fibre for rope, fabric, paper, and building materials

Alberta statistics:

  • Alberta produces ~20% of Canada's wheat
  • ~40% of Canada's canola comes from Alberta
  • Alberta's agricultural industry generates over supply the current figure — farm cash receipts have been in the range of 15 to 20+ billion dollars in recent years billion dollars per year

Monoculture = growing a single crop species over large areas. Advantages: efficient to plant and harvest with large machinery, high yield at lower cost. Disadvantages: one pest or disease can destroy the whole crop, reduces biodiversity and wears out soil nutrients.

Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. Name two Alberta crops grown for food and two grown for fibre.
    Answer Food: any two of wheat, canola, barley, peas, lentils. Fibre: flax and hemp.
  2. What is the difference between a food crop and a fibre crop?
    Answer A food crop is grown to be eaten (or pressed for edible oil). A fibre crop is grown for its fibres to make materials such as fabric, rope, or paper.
  3. Name one advantage and one disadvantage of monoculture farming.
    Answer Advantage: efficient large-scale planting and harvesting. Disadvantage: a single pest or disease can wipe out the whole crop (also lower biodiversity, soil depletion).
  4. Stretch: Explain why the health of bee populations is directly connected to Alberta's food security.
    Answer Stretch: Many Alberta crops, especially canola, depend on or benefit from insect pollination to set seed. Fewer bees means less pollination, lower seed yields, and less food and oil produced. Alberta is also a major honey producer.

Section 6: Plant Biotechnology

Learning target: I can explain selective breeding and genetic modification and evaluate their effects on agriculture and biodiversity.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Selective breeding Choosing parent plants with wanted traits and breeding them over many generations Wild mustard bred into broccoli and cabbage
Genetically modified organism (GMO) An organism whose genes have been directly changed by scientists Herbicide-tolerant canola
Biotechnology Using living things or their parts to make products or solve problems Breeding drought-tolerant wheat

Guided Notes

Selective breeding: Humans choose plants with desirable (wanted) traits and breed only those plants. Repeat over many generations. Results in dramatic changes over thousands of years. Example: wild mustard → cabbage, broccoli, kale, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts (all the same species, selectively bred for different traits).

Genetic modification (GMOs): Scientists directly change (modify / alter) specific genes — inserting, removing, or altering sequences in the DNA. Faster and more precise than selective breeding, but requires laboratory techniques.

Technology Timeline Example Alberta relevance
Selective breeding Thousands of years High-yield wheat varieties All Alberta crop varieties have been selectively bred
GMOs Since ~1990 Canola with herbicide resistance ~95% of Alberta canola is GMO

Common GMO modifications in Alberta crops:

  • Herbicide tolerance: crop survives herbicide that kills weeds → fewer herbicide applications needed
  • Pest resistance: built-in insect repellent → less pesticide needed
  • Drought tolerance: survive with less water

Evaluating biotechnology — multiple perspectives:

Perspective Concern or support
Agronomist (crop specialist) Support: higher yields, less pesticide and tillage, crops that tolerate drought or pests
Ecologist Concern: less genetic diversity, genes spreading to wild relatives, herbicide-resistant weeds, effects on other insects
Consumer Mixed: wants proof of safety and clear labelling; may welcome lower prices or better nutrition
Indigenous farmer Concern: who owns the seeds; being able to save and share traditional seed varieties; responsibility to the land. Some may also see benefits
Note:

This is a contested topic. Scientists, farmers, consumers, and Indigenous communities have genuinely different values that lead to different conclusions — even when they agree on the facts. Recognising this is part of scientific literacy.

Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. What is selective breeding? How long has it been used?
    Answer Choosing plants with desired traits and breeding only those, repeated over many generations. It has been used for thousands of years (about 10,000).
  2. Name two goals of genetic modification in Alberta crop plants.
    Answer Any two: herbicide tolerance, pest resistance, drought tolerance.
  3. What is the difference between selective breeding and genetic modification? (Method, speed, precision)
    Answer Selective breeding: breeds whole plants, slow (many generations), less precise — many genes change at once. Genetic modification: changes specific genes directly in a lab, much faster, very precise.
  4. Stretch: A friend says "GMOs are definitely dangerous." Another says "GMOs are completely safe." Explain why both claims are oversimplified, and what questions would need to be answered to evaluate any specific GMO.
    Answer Stretch: Both are blanket statements about a technique, but safety depends on the specific change. Questions to ask: What gene was changed and what does it do? Has the crop been tested for effects on health? Can it spread to wild plants or affect other species? Who benefits and who carries the risk? Each GMO should be judged on its own evidence.

1. Draw and label a plant cell. Include at least 5 structures and write the function of each.

Answer At least five labelled structures with functions: cell wall (support and shape), cell membrane (controls what enters and leaves), central vacuole (stores water, keeps the cell firm), chloroplasts (photosynthesis), nucleus (controls the cell, holds DNA).

 

 

 

2. Write the photosynthesis equation in words (not symbols). For each input and output, identify where it comes from or where it goes.

Answer Carbon dioxide + water, using light energy, → glucose + oxygen. Carbon dioxide comes from the air through pores in the leaf; water comes from the soil through the roots and xylem; light comes from the sun and is captured by chlorophyll; glucose is used for energy and growth or stored as starch (moved by phloem); oxygen is released into the air.
