Concept 2: Friction & Control

I can: explain why a marble slows down, and identify design choices that control its speed.

Misconception

"The marble slows down because it runs out of energy on its own."

Why this fails: energy doesn't just disappear — something has to remove it. Picture two ramps, same starting height, same marble: one smooth and straight, one rough with turns. Same starting energy in both — but very different results at the bottom. If energy just "ran out" by itself, both marbles would behave the same way.

Correct understanding: Friction (rubbing between the marble and the track) and collisions (hitting turns, bumping into edges) are what remove energy from the marble, turning it into heat and sound you can't easily see. A rougher surface, a narrower channel, or a tighter turn all increase friction and slow the marble down on purpose — that's a design choice, not something that just happens.

A second common idea to check: is steeper always better? A steeper section makes the marble go   (faster/slower) at that point. For this unit's goal — keeping the marble moving slowly without stopping — is steeper usually helpful or unhelpful?

  2. A friend says "my coaster failed because the marble ran out of energy." Rewrite their sentence using "friction" so it's more accurate.

Concept 3: The Design Cycle & Constraints

I can: name the steps of the design cycle, and explain what a constraint is doing to a design problem.

This unit, we're naming a process explicitly for the first time: the engineering design cycle.

Define → Brainstorm → Build → Test → Redesign → Share

A constraint is a limit or rule your design has to work inside — not a suggestion. This unit's constraints work against each other on purpose: your coaster has to keep moving for at least a set amount of time, and it can't stop completely. That's a competing constraint — solving one part (going slow) makes the other part (not stopping) harder.

A prototype is a first attempt you expect to test and change — it is not meant to be perfect the first time. Testing a prototype and finding out what's wrong with it is how the design cycle is supposed to work, not a sign you did something wrong.
