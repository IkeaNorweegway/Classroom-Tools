3★ Core

A small Alberta town of 600 people wants to decide whether to build a new skate park. A large country of 40 million people needs to decide on a new national law.

Hint Think about how many people would need to gather in one place to vote directly.
  1. Which situation fits direct democracy better?  
  2. Which situation fits representative democracy better?  
  3. Explain why population size changes which model makes sense.
Answer (a) The 600-person town — direct democracy. (b) The 40-million-person country — representative democracy. (c) Direct democracy requires gathering everyone to vote on every decision, which is realistic for a small population but not for millions of people spread across a large area; representative democracy solves this by having elected people vote on citizens' behalf.
4★★ Extended

A news article says:

"The President was elected directly by the whole country, and cannot be removed from office before the four-year term ends, no matter how unpopular they become. Meanwhile, the Prime Minister was chosen by their own party and could lose power at any time if the legislature loses confidence in them."

Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of each system, using the article as evidence.

Answer Presidential advantage: stability/predictability — a fixed term means no sudden leadership changes. Presidential disadvantage: an unpopular president stays in power the full term regardless of public opinion. Parliamentary advantage: a PM who loses public/legislative confidence can be replaced quickly. Parliamentary disadvantage: less predictable — elections or leadership changes can happen with little warning.

7★★ Extended

A country holds elections every few years, but a law says only one political party is legally allowed to run candidates. The government calls this country a democracy.

Using what you know about the political spectrum, explain whether this country's claim holds up. What is it missing that a true democracy requires?

Answer No — a defining feature of democracy is that citizens can genuinely choose between competing options. A country that legally allows only one party to run is functioning more like a one-party/dictatorship-leaning system than a true democracy, regardless of what it calls itself. Holding elections is not enough on its own; the elections must offer real choice.
8★ Core

A student wrote:

"Only people who are 18 and can vote are allowed to join a political party or help out at a riding association."
Hint Think about the difference between voting in an election and joining a party as a member.

Identify the error in this statement. Correct it using what you know about party membership.

Answer Error: Party membership and volunteering are open to people of many ages — most major Canadian parties do not require voting age (18) to join as a member or help with riding association activities. Correction: Voting in an election requires being 18+, but joining a party or volunteering with a riding association does not have the same age requirement.
9★★ Extended

A classmate claims:

"The Governor General is actually the most powerful person in Canada's government, because they have to approve every law before it becomes real."
  1. Is this classmate correct? Circle one: Yes / No
  2. Explain what the Governor General's role actually is, and who really holds day-to-day political power in Canada.
Answer (a) No. (b) The Governor General's role is mostly ceremonial — they give Royal Assent (formal approval) to bills that Parliament has already passed and represent the monarch, but they act on the advice of the Prime Minister and Cabinet by long-standing convention. Real day-to-day political power belongs to the Prime Minister and Cabinet, who are accountable to elected MPs.

12★★ Extended

A student says:

"The United States and Canada must be basically the same kind of democracy, since both countries call themselves democracies."

Using what you know about direct vs. representative democracy AND parliamentary vs. presidential systems, explain why this student's claim is a misconception. Be specific about at least two real differences.

Answer Strong answers name at least two real differences, such as: Canada uses a parliamentary system where the PM is chosen through party leadership and seat count, while the U.S. uses a presidential system where the President is elected directly, nationwide, for a fixed term; Canada's executive and legislative branches are closely connected, while the U.S. keeps them separate with checks and balances (e.g., the veto). Both countries are democracies, but "democracy" covers multiple different models, not one single system.

14★★ Extended

Below is a description of a real federal election ballot:

"The ballot lists four names: a Liberal candidate, a Conservative candidate, an NDP candidate, and a Green candidate — all running to represent this specific riding. No party leader's name appears anywhere on the ballot."

A student looks at this and says: "That's weird — shouldn't the Prime Minister's name be on here since we're voting for the government?"

Using the ballot description as evidence, explain exactly why the student's expectation is incorrect, and how someone actually becomes Prime Minister.

Answer The ballot only lists local candidates for that riding — no party leader's name appears anywhere on any ballot in the country, because every riding has different candidates. This proves Canadians vote only for their local MP. A party becomes government by winning the most seats nationwide, and that party's leader becomes Prime Minister — a result of party leadership and seat count, not a direct nationwide vote for a single person.
15★★ Extended

A 16-year-old student wants to influence which candidate their local party runs in the next election, before they are even old enough to vote.

Using what you know about party membership and riding associations, describe a realistic path this student could take. Name at least two specific things they could do.

Answer Accept any two realistic actions such as: joining the party as a member (most parties don't require voting age), attending or voting at riding association meetings on candidate selection, volunteering with the local riding association, or attending party events to express support for a candidate. Should reference that riding associations elect the local candidate, and party members have a real vote in that process.
16★★ Extended

In the United States, Congress passes a bill. The President vetoes it. Congress then votes again and gathers enough votes to override the veto.

  1. Explain what happened at each of these three steps, using the terms Congress, veto, and override correctly.
  2. Could this same sequence of events happen in Canada's parliamentary system? Explain why or why not, based on how power is organized differently in the two systems.
Answer (a) Congress (the elected legislature) passed a bill; the President used the veto to reject it; Congress then voted again and got enough votes to override the President's veto, making it law despite the President's rejection. (b) No — in Canada's parliamentary system, the executive (PM and Cabinet) is drawn from and depends on the support of the legislature (Parliament), so there is no separate presidential veto power built into the system the way there is in the U.S.'s separated executive/legislative structure.
17★★ Extended

An election produces this result: Party A wins 40% of seats, Party B wins 35%, Party C wins 15%, and Party D wins 10%. No party has more than half the seats.

  1. What kind of party system does this result suggest?  
  2. What challenge does this create for forming a government? Explain using the concept of majority rule.
  3. A classmate says this proves multiparty systems are "broken" compared to two-party systems. Write a response that shows the trade-offs on both sides.
Answer (a) Multiparty system. (b) No party has a majority (more than 50%) of seats, so under majority rule no single party can pass laws alone — parties may need to negotiate a coalition or govern as a minority government, which can slow decisions or require compromise. (c) Strong response acknowledges trade-offs both ways: multiparty systems can be slower to reach decisions when no majority exists, but they also give voters more choice; two-party systems decide faster but limit voter options. Neither is simply "broken" or "better."
18★ Core

Some countries use direct democracy for one specific big decision through a referendum, even though they are usually representative democracies (Canada has done this before).

Explain why a country might choose a referendum (direct democracy) for one specific major decision, but use representative democracy for everyday law-making.

Answer A referendum lets citizens weigh in directly on one especially important question (e.g., a constitutional change) where getting direct public input is valued, without requiring that model for every law. Using representative democracy for everyday law-making is more practical because it would be unrealistic to hold a nationwide direct vote on every single bill.

Challenge Zone

Attempt after completing all previous questions. No scaffolding — these questions require connecting ideas from more than one section of the unit.

S1★★

Design an original political party. Give it a name and describe its core ideology in one sentence. Then explain:

  1. What party system (one-party, two-party, or multiparty) would your party need to exist in to have a realistic chance of winning seats? Why?
  2. Would your party run as part of a parliamentary system or a presidential system? Explain one specific feature of that system your party would need to work within.
Answer (a) Answer depends on the student's designed party — key marking point: the student must justify their party system choice using the trade-offs discussed in Section 5 (e.g., "multiparty, because my party represents a narrow issue and wouldn't need to win outright"). (b) Must correctly describe one real structural feature (e.g., "in a parliamentary system, my party would need to win a plurality of ridings, not a nationwide vote, to have a chance at government").
S2★★

Choose one real country with a parliamentary system and one real country with a presidential system (you may use Canada and the United States, or research two others).

Compare them on: (1) how the leader is chosen, (2) how long they can stay in power, and (3) how closely the executive and legislative branches are connected. Then state which model you think handles a sudden loss of public trust in the leader better, and why.

Answer Should correctly compare leader selection (direct election vs. party leadership + seats), term stability (fixed term vs. no fixed term/can lose confidence), and branch connection (separated with checks and balances vs. closely connected). Accept either conclusion on which handles loss of trust better, provided the reasoning is grounded in the system's actual structure (e.g., parliamentary allows faster removal; presidential offers more short-term stability).
S3★★

Trace the complete path from an ordinary citizen joining a political party to that party's leader becoming Prime Minister of Canada. Your answer must use at least five of these terms correctly, in a logical order: membership, riding association, caucus, candidate, riding, seats, government, Prime Minister.

Answer Sample full-mark answer: A citizen joins a party as a member → participates in their local riding association → helps select the party's candidate for their riding → the candidate wins that riding in the general election → the party wins enough seats overall to form government → the party's leader (already head of the party's caucus) becomes Prime Minister. Award marks for correct use of 5+ terms in a logical order, not for verbatim wording.
S4★★

A newly forming country is deciding between these two options:

  • Option 1: A two-party presidential system with a fixed four-year term and a directly elected leader.
  • Option 2: A multiparty parliamentary system where the legislature chooses the executive and can remove them at any time.

Evaluate both options. Consider control levels, individual rights, and stability. Recommend one option and justify your choice using at least two concepts from this unit.

Answer Strong answers weigh control levels (both are democracies — moderate/low central control compared to dictatorship/oligarchy), individual rights (both preserve individual rights via elections, though the presidential model separates power more to prevent one branch dominating), and stability (fixed-term presidential = predictable but can trap an unpopular leader in power; parliamentary can remove an unpopular leader fast but is less predictable). Recommendation can go either way — mark on the quality and unit-grounded justification, not on which option is "correct."
