Retrieval Check — Section 1

  1. Crust → mantle → outer core → inner core
  2. The pressure at the centre of the Earth is so extreme that it holds the particles together as a solid, even at that temperature.
  3. The moving liquid iron and nickel generate Earth's magnetic field, which shields living things from harmful solar radiation.
  4. From seismic waves. Earthquake waves change speed, bend, and reflect when they pass through different materials. By recording when and where the waves arrive at stations around the world, scientists work out what the layers are made of and where they begin.
  5. Stretch: The oceanic plate goes under (subducts) because it is denser. As it sinks into the mantle it heats up and melts, becoming magma that can rise and cool into new igneous rock — so subduction recycles crust through the rock cycle. Continental crust is too light to sink.

Retrieval Check — Section 2

  1. Intrusive rock cools slowly underground and has large crystals; extrusive rock cools quickly at the surface and has small crystals or is glassy. Slow cooling gives crystals time to grow.
  2. Sediment settles in flat layers, one on top of another, over long periods. The layers show the order of events (oldest at the bottom) and what the environment was like when each layer formed.
  3. Heat and/or pressure create metamorphic rock. Melting does not occur — if the rock melted and cooled it would be igneous.
  4. Sedimentary rock. Organisms are buried in sediment and preserved as it hardens. The heat of magma or of metamorphism would destroy the remains.
  5. Stretch: The marble began as limestone, a sedimentary rock formed from the shells of sea organisms; heat and pressure changed it to marble. Acid rain now weathers it; the dissolved and broken material washes into rivers and is deposited in a lake or sea. Over millions of years it could be buried and cemented into new sedimentary rock — or carried deeper and changed again.

Retrieval Check — Section 3

  1. Convection currents in the mantle — hot rock rises, spreads, cools, and sinks, slowly carrying the plates.
  2. Divergent: plates move apart — mid-ocean ridges, new seafloor, rift valleys. Convergent: plates move together — mountains, trenches, volcanoes. Transform: plates slide past each other — earthquakes.
  3. Continental crust is too light to sink into the mantle, so neither plate subducts. The rock crumples and is pushed upward.
  4. Plate boundaries lie where the earthquakes and volcanoes are. Mapping them outlines the edges of the plates.
  5. Stretch: A divergent boundary — the Mid-Atlantic Ridge runs through Iceland. The North American and Eurasian plates are moving apart, magma rises into the gap, and it cools into new crust. This is the same process that makes new seafloor.

Retrieval Check — Section 4

  1. Rock along a fault is locked by friction while the plates keep trying to move, so stress builds. When the stress is greater than the rock's strength, the rock breaks and slips suddenly, releasing energy as seismic waves.
  2. The focus is the point underground where the rock breaks. The epicenter is the point on the surface directly above it.
  3. Richter measures the energy released, using instruments. Mercalli describes the effects — what people feel and the damage observed.
  4. A shield volcano. Its lava has low viscosity (it is runny), so gas escapes easily and the lava flows gently instead of exploding.
  5. Stretch: Mauna Loa's low-viscosity lava lets gas escape gradually, so it erupts as slow flows that people can move away from. Mt. St. Helens has thick, high-viscosity magma that traps gas; pressure built up and was released in a sudden explosion. The eruption style, not the volume of lava, determines the danger.

Term Model definition Example

Retrieval Check — Section 5

  1. Weathering breaks rock down where it sits. Erosion carries the broken material away.
  2. Mechanical, e.g., freeze-thaw — water freezes in cracks, expands, and splits the rock. Chemical, e.g., oxidation — iron in the rock reacts with oxygen and rusts. Mechanical changes only the size of the pieces; chemical changes what the rock is made of.
  3. Hoodoos are tall pillars of soft rock topped by a harder cap. The cap protects the rock directly beneath it while wind and water wear away the softer rock around it (differential erosion).
  4. Glaciers. During the last ice age, ice sheets carried boulders hundreds of kilometres and dropped them when the ice melted.
  5. Stretch: A great deal happened. Ice sheets up to kilometres thick covered Alberta, scraped the land, and left a thick, even layer of glacial till when they melted; meltwater lakes left flat beds of fine sediment. The flatness is the result of that deposition. It happened slowly and long ago, not "never."

Retrieval Check — Section 6

  1. Conditions: quick burial in sediment before the organism decays or is eaten; mineral-rich groundwater; a very long time. Fossils are rare because most organisms decay or are eaten first, soft parts seldom preserve, and the right conditions are uncommon.
  2. In undisturbed rock layers, lower layers are older and upper layers are younger. It lets geologists put layers and the fossils in them in order from oldest to youngest without knowing their age in years.
  3. Alberta's Cretaceous sedimentary layers are exposed at the surface in the Badlands, where erosion has removed the rock above. An inland sea covered Alberta about 75 million years ago, and its coastal sediments buried and preserved both marine life and dinosaurs.
  4. Any two with how they form, e.g., mould — the organism dissolves and leaves a hollow impression; cast — a mould fills with minerals to make a 3D copy; trace fossil — a footprint or burrow is preserved; compression — a leaf is flattened under sediment; amber — an insect is trapped in hardened tree resin.
  5. Stretch: The two rock layers are the same age — they formed during the short period when that organism lived. To find the age in years she would need absolute dating (measuring radioactive decay in the rock, usually a volcanic ash layer).

Unit Synthesis

  1. Cross-section of the Earth. Four labelled layers with a key property each: crust (thin, solid rock; oceanic is thin and dense, continental is thick and lighter); mantle (thickest layer; hot solid rock that flows slowly; convection currents); outer core (liquid iron and nickel; makes the magnetic field); inner core (solid iron and nickel; solid because of pressure).
  2. Rock cycle. Igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic rock connected by at least five labelled arrows: melting, then cooling → igneous; weathering, erosion, deposition, compaction and cementation → sedimentary; heat and pressure → metamorphic. Any rock type can become any other.
