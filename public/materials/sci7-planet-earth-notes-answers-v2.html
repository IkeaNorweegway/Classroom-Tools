Section 1: Earth's Interior

Learning target: I can describe the four layers of Earth's interior and explain how we know what is inside.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Crust Earth's thin, solid outer layer of rock The ground we stand on; the ocean floor
Mantle The thick layer of hot rock below the crust that flows very slowly The source of magma
Outer core The liquid layer of iron and nickel below the mantle Creates Earth's magnetic field
Inner core The solid ball of iron and nickel at Earth's centre Kept solid by extreme pressure
Seismic waves Waves of energy from earthquakes that travel through the Earth Waves recorded on a seismograph

Guided Notes

We have never drilled to the mantle. The deepest drill hole ever (Kola Superdeep, Russia) reached only 12 km — and the crust is up to 70 km thick in some places. Everything we know about Earth's interior comes from seismic waves: waves produced by earthquakes that travel through the Earth and are recorded at stations worldwide.

Earth's four layers:

1. Crust — the outermost layer

  • Oceanic crust: thin (5–10 km), dense (basalt); found under oceans
  • Continental crust: thick (up to 70 km), less dense (granite); the land we live on
  • The crust + upper mantle together = the lithosphere (the rigid outer shell, broken into tectonic plates)

2. Mantle — the largest layer (~2,900 km thick)

  • Made of: hot, solid rock
  • Behaviour: solid, but under extreme heat and pressure it flows extremely slowly (like very thick putty over millions of years)
  • Contains convection currents — slow circular movements of hot mantle rock rising and cool rock sinking; these drive tectonic plate motion

3. Outer core — liquid layer of iron and nickel

  • Temperature: ~4,000–6,000°C — hot enough to be liquid despite being deep
  • The moving liquid metal generates Earth's magnetic field (protects life from solar radiation)

4. Inner core — solid ball of iron and nickel

  • Temperature: ~5,000–6,000°C — hotter than outer core, but solid because pressure is so extreme that it keeps it solid

Fig. — Earth's internal layers. Label each numbered layer with its name.

How do we know? Seismic waves travel at different speeds through different materials and bend (refract) or reflect (bounce) at layer boundaries. By analysing the paths and timings of these waves recorded at many stations, scientists mapped Earth's interior without ever seeing it.

Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. Name Earth's four layers from outermost to innermost.
    Answer Crust → mantle → outer core → inner core
  2. Why is the inner core solid even though it's hotter than the outer core?
    Answer The pressure at the centre of the Earth is so extreme that it holds the particles together as a solid, even at that temperature.
  3. Why is the outer core important to life on Earth?
    Answer The moving liquid iron and nickel generate Earth's magnetic field, which shields living things from harmful solar radiation.
  4. How do scientists know what the inside of Earth looks like if we've never drilled there?
    Answer From seismic waves. Earthquake waves change speed, bend, and reflect when they pass through different materials. By recording when and where the waves arrive at stations around the world, scientists work out what the layers are made of and where they begin.
  5. Stretch: Oceanic crust is thinner and denser than continental crust. When an oceanic plate and a continental plate collide, which one goes under? Use density to explain why this matters for the rock cycle.
    Answer Stretch: The oceanic plate goes under (subducts) because it is denser. As it sinks into the mantle it heats up and melts, becoming magma that can rise and cool into new igneous rock — so subduction recycles crust through the rock cycle. Continental crust is too light to sink.

Section 2: The Rock Cycle

Learning target: I can classify rocks as igneous, sedimentary, or metamorphic and explain the conditions that form each type.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Igneous rock Rock formed when melted rock cools and hardens Granite, basalt
Sedimentary rock Rock formed when layers of sediment are pressed and cemented together Sandstone, shale, limestone
Metamorphic rock Rock changed by heat and pressure without melting Marble, slate
Magma Melted rock below Earth's surface (called lava at the surface) Magma beneath a volcano
Sediment Small pieces of rock, shell, or once-living material Sand, mud, gravel
Rock cycle The ongoing processes that change rock from one type into another Granite weathering into sand that becomes sandstone

Guided Notes

Rock is not permanent — it changes continuously through the rock cycle, driven by plate tectonics and solar energy.

Three rock families:

IGNEOUS ROCK — formed when magma (underground) or lava (surface) cools and solidifies

Type Cooling rate Crystal size Example
Intrusive (plutonic) slow (underground) large granite, gabbro
Extrusive (volcanic) fast (at surface) small (or glassy) basalt, obsidian, pumice

Why crystal size differs: Slow cooling gives crystals time to grow. Fast cooling freezes atoms before they can arrange into large crystals.

SEDIMENTARY ROCK — formed from layers of sediment of weathered rock and organic material, compressed and cemented over time

Three types of sedimentary rock:

  1. clastic sedimentary: formed from rock fragments (sandstone, shale, conglomerate)
  2. chemical sedimentary: formed from mineral precipitation from water (rock salt, gypsum)
  3. organic (biological) sedimentary: formed from organic remains (coal, chalk, some limestone)

Why sedimentary rock is scientifically important:

  • Contains fossils — preserved remains of ancient life
  • Rock layers (strata) record Earth's history: oldest layers at the bottom (younger layers on top)
  • Widespread in Alberta — oil sands, fossils, Alberta Badlands are all sedimentary

METAMORPHIC ROCK — existing rock transformed by heat and/or pressure (but not melted — if it melted, it would become igneous)

Parent rock Becomes Change
Limestone marble heat + pressure
Shale slate heat + pressure
Granite gneiss extreme pressure

Foliated metamorphic rocks: have visible layers or bands (slate (or gneiss), schist)

Non-foliated: no distinct layers (marble, quartzite)

The Rock Cycle:

All rock types can be converted to any other through the appropriate processes:

Process What happens Produces
Melting + cooling rock melts to magma, then solidifies igneous rock
Weathering + erosion + deposition + compaction rock breaks down, transported, deposited, buried sedimentary rock
Heat + pressure (no melting) rock transformed metamorphic rock

Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. What is the difference between intrusive and extrusive igneous rock? Why do they have different crystal sizes?
    Answer Intrusive rock cools slowly underground and has large crystals; extrusive rock cools quickly at the surface and has small crystals or is glassy. Slow cooling gives crystals time to grow.
  2. Why do sedimentary rocks have layers? What do these layers tell geologists?
    Answer Sediment settles in flat layers, one on top of another, over long periods. The layers show the order of events (oldest at the bottom) and what the environment was like when each layer formed.
  3. What conditions create metamorphic rock? What conditions DON'T occur during metamorphism?
    Answer Heat and/or pressure create metamorphic rock. Melting does not occur — if the rock melted and cooled it would be igneous.
  4. In which rock type would you most likely find a fossil? Why?
    Answer Sedimentary rock. Organisms are buried in sediment and preserved as it hardens. The heat of magma or of metamorphism would destroy the remains.
  5. Stretch: A marble statue (metamorphic) is eroding in an acidic urban environment. Trace the marble through the rock cycle: where did the original rock come from, and where might the eroded marble end up in 10 million years?
    Answer Stretch: The marble began as limestone, a sedimentary rock formed from the shells of sea organisms; heat and pressure changed it to marble. Acid rain now weathers it; the dissolved and broken material washes into rivers and is deposited in a lake or sea. Over millions of years it could be buried and cemented into new sedimentary rock — or carried deeper and changed again.

Section 3: Plate Tectonics

Learning target: I can explain plate tectonics and describe what happens at the three types of plate boundaries.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Tectonic plate A large, rigid piece of Earth's outer shell (lithosphere) that moves slowly North American Plate, Pacific Plate
Plate boundary The edge where two plates meet The west coast of North America
Divergent boundary A boundary where two plates move apart Mid-Atlantic Ridge
Convergent boundary A boundary where two plates move together India and Eurasia — the Himalayas
Transform boundary A boundary where two plates slide sideways past each other San Andreas Fault
Subduction A denser plate sinking beneath another plate The Juan de Fuca Plate under North America

Guided Notes

The theory of plate tectonics: Earth's outer shell (lithosphere) is broken into approximately 7 (accept 7–8) major plates and several smaller ones. These plates move at a rate of 1–10 cm per year (roughly the rate your fingernails grow). The driving force is convection currents in the mantle below.

MISCONCEPTION

"Tectonic plates are the same as continents." Why this is wrong: Plates include both continental crust and oceanic crust. Many plate boundaries are underwater (in the middle of oceans). The North American Plate, for example, includes most of North America AND the western half of the Atlantic Ocean. Some plates are almost entirely oceanic (Pacific Plate). Continents ride on plates; they are not the same thing as plates.

Evidence for plate tectonics (how we know):

Evidence type What it shows
Continental fit Coastlines of Africa and South America fit together like puzzle pieces
Matching fossils Same species fossils found on different continents now separated by thousands of km of ocean
Paleomagnetic striping Magnetic reversal patterns are symmetrical (matching / mirror images) on both sides of mid-ocean ridges — like a tape recording of spreading
Earthquake/volcano distribution Most earthquakes and volcanoes occur in narrow bands (zones) — along plate boundaries
GPS measurements Modern technology directly measures plate movement (speed and direction)

Three boundary types:

DIVERGENT BOUNDARY — plates move (pull) apart

Features: new seafloor created; mid-ocean ridges; volcanic activity (gentle, fluid lava); rift valleys on land

Example: Mid-Atlantic Ridge (North America + Europe separating) — Iceland sits on this ridge

Alberta connection: none (there are no divergent boundaries near Alberta)

CONVERGENT BOUNDARY — plates move (push / collide) together

Three subtypes:

  1. Ocean + continent: denser oceanic plate sinks (slides under) (subducts) under continental plate → trench + volcanic arc on continent

    Example: Juan de Fuca plate + North American plate → Cascade volcanoes

  2. Ocean + ocean: older, denser plate subducts → deep trench + volcanic island arc

    Example: Pacific plate + Eurasian plate → Japan, Marianas Trench

  3. Continent + continent: neither subducts; both crumple upward → major mountain chain

    Example: India + Eurasia → Himalaya Mountains

Alberta connection: The Rocky Mountains formed ~100 million years ago when oceanic plates pushed under North America, and later continental collision crumpled up the layers. Alberta itself was pushed and folded. Alberta is safe from active tectonics today, but its rocks still record that ancient collision.

TRANSFORM BOUNDARY — plates slide sideways past each other

Features: earthquakes; no new crust, no subduction; the two plates grind and lock

Example: San Andreas Fault, California — North American plate grinding past Pacific plate

Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. What drives the movement of tectonic plates?
    Answer Convection currents in the mantle — hot rock rises, spreads, cools, and sinks, slowly carrying the plates.
  2. Name the three boundary types and identify ONE feature produced at each.
    Answer Divergent: plates move apart — mid-ocean ridges, new seafloor, rift valleys. Convergent: plates move together — mountains, trenches, volcanoes. Transform: plates slide past each other — earthquakes.
  3. Why do mountains form at continent-continent convergent boundaries?
    Answer Continental crust is too light to sink into the mantle, so neither plate subducts. The rock crumples and is pushed upward.
  4. Most earthquakes and volcanoes occur in predictable locations. What does this tell us about where plate boundaries are?
    Answer Plate boundaries lie where the earthquakes and volcanoes are. Mapping them outlines the edges of the plates.
  5. Stretch: Iceland has active volcanoes and is slowly splitting apart. Scientists predict it will eventually divide into two islands. Explain what type of boundary runs through Iceland and what geological process is occurring.
    Answer Stretch: A divergent boundary — the Mid-Atlantic Ridge runs through Iceland. The North American and Eurasian plates are moving apart, magma rises into the gap, and it cools into new crust. This is the same process that makes new seafloor.

Section 4: Earthquakes and Volcanoes

Learning target: I can explain how earthquakes occur and describe two types of volcanoes in terms of their eruption style.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Fault A break in Earth's crust along which rock moves San Andreas Fault
Focus (hypocenter) The point underground where the rock first breaks 10 km below the surface
Epicenter The point on the surface directly above the focus The location named in news reports
Richter scale A scale that measures the energy an earthquake releases A magnitude 7 earthquake
Mercalli scale A scale that rates an earthquake by its observed effects and damage Level VI: felt by everyone, objects fall
Shield volcano A wide, gently sloping volcano built by runny lava Mauna Loa, Kilauea
Composite (stratovolcano) A steep, cone-shaped volcano with thick lava and explosive eruptions Mt. St. Helens

Guided Notes

Earthquakes:

Plates are locked together at their boundaries by friction. As plates try to move, stress (pressure / stored energy) builds. When the stress exceeds the strength of the rock, the rock breaks and slips suddenly — seismic waves radiate outward in all directions.

  • Focus (hypocenter): the point where the rock breaks (underground)
  • Epicenter: the point on the surface directly above the focus; where shaking is usually strongest

Measuring earthquakes:

Scale What it measures Type of measurement
Richter Energy released at the earthquake's focus Instrument-based; logarithmic (magnitude 6 = 10× more energy than magnitude 5)
Mercalli observed (damage) effects — what people experience and see Observational; I (not felt) to XII (total destruction)

An earthquake of magnitude 7.0 on the Richter scale releases 10× more energy than one of magnitude 6.0.

Volcanoes:

All volcanoes form where magma reaches the surface. Location: mostly along plate boundaries and hot spots (plumes of hot mantle material below the crust).

Type Lava viscosity Eruption style Shape Example
Shield volcano low (fluid) gentle, flows broad, low-sloping dome Kilauea, Mauna Loa
Composite (stratovolcano) high (thick) explosive, violent steep, cone-shaped Mt. Pinatubo, Mt. St. Helens (also Mt. Fuji, Mt. Rainier)

Why does lava viscosity affect eruption style? Fluid lava lets gas escape gradually → gentle flow. Thick lava traps gas → pressure builds until an explosive release.

Alberta and geological hazards:

  • Alberta is geologically stable — far from active plate boundaries
  • Occasional small earthquakes in Alberta from hydraulic fracturing (fracking) and waste-water injection (human-caused: fracking, waste water injection)
  • No volcanoes in Alberta; however, Alberta residents experience smoke from BC/Washington volcanic region
Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. What causes an earthquake at the particle level? (What is actually happening in the rock?)
    Answer Rock along a fault is locked by friction while the plates keep trying to move, so stress builds. When the stress is greater than the rock's strength, the rock breaks and slips suddenly, releasing energy as seismic waves.
  2. What is the difference between the focus and epicenter of an earthquake?
    Answer The focus is the point underground where the rock breaks. The epicenter is the point on the surface directly above it.
  3. Compare the Richter scale and Mercalli scale — what does each measure?
    Answer Richter measures the energy released, using instruments. Mercalli describes the effects — what people feel and the damage observed.
  4. A volcano in Hawaii has gentle lava flows that tourists can watch from nearby. What type is it and why is its lava so fluid?
    Answer A shield volcano. Its lava has low viscosity (it is runny), so gas escapes easily and the lava flows gently instead of exploding.
  5. Stretch: The 1980 eruption of Mt. St. Helens was highly explosive and killed 57 people. The 1984 eruption of Mauna Loa in Hawaii lasted several weeks and caused almost no casualties despite being a larger eruption in terms of lava volume. Explain this difference using lava viscosity.
    Answer Stretch: Mauna Loa's low-viscosity lava lets gas escape gradually, so it erupts as slow flows that people can move away from. Mt. St. Helens has thick, high-viscosity magma that traps gas; pressure built up and was released in a sudden explosion. The eruption style, not the volume of lava, determines the danger.

Section 5: Weathering, Erosion, and Alberta Landforms

Learning target: I can describe mechanical and chemical weathering, explain erosion and deposition, and identify Alberta landforms they have produced.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Weathering The breaking down of rock at or near the surface A cliff face crumbling
Mechanical weathering Breaking rock into smaller pieces without changing what it is made of Freeze-thaw cracking
Chemical weathering Breaking down rock by changing what it is made of Rusting; acid dissolving limestone
Erosion The moving of weathered material by water, wind, ice, or gravity A river carrying silt
Deposition The dropping of eroded material in a new place A sandbar; a river delta
Glacial till The unsorted mix of clay, sand, gravel, and boulders left by a glacier The soil under much of the prairies

Guided Notes

Weathering = the breaking down of rock at or near the surface. Important: weathering does NOT move material — it only breaks it.

Two types:

MECHANICAL WEATHERING — rock is broken into smaller pieces but composition (what the rock is made of) does not change

Process How it works Alberta example
Freeze-thaw Water enters cracks, freezes, expands, widens crack Rocky Mountain trails crumble every spring
Root growth Plant roots grow into cracks, pry rock apart tree roots splitting rock on mountain slopes (or lifting sidewalks)
Abrasion Particles carried by wind/water/ice scratch rock glaciers scraping bedrock in the Rockies; wind-blown sand in the Badlands

CHEMICAL WEATHERING — composition of rock changes; minerals react with water, oxygen, or acids

Process How it works Example
Acid rain CO₂ + water → carbonic acid dissolves limestone limestone caves in the Rockies; worn limestone gravestones and statues
Oxidation Oxygen reacts with iron in rocks → rust red/orange rocks in Alberta Badlands
Dissolution Some minerals dissolve directly in water rock salt or gypsum dissolving (sinkholes in northern Alberta)

Erosion = transport of weathered material by water, wind, ice (glaciers), or gravity. The agent determines what size of material is moved and where it goes.

Deposition = material is dropped (settles) when the agent slows down. Larger/heavier particles settle first; finer particles travel farther.

Alberta landforms — what made them:

Landform Location How it formed
Rocky Mountains SW Alberta plate collision — rock layers folded and pushed up (plate tectonics) + glacial erosion
Hoodoos Drumheller / Badlands differential (uneven) erosion of soft and hard rock layers
Prairies central/southern Alberta glacial deposition of glacial till after the last ice age
River valleys (Bow, Red Deer) throughout Alberta water (river) erosion cutting downward over thousands of years
Athabasca Sand Dunes NE Alberta wind erosion of exposed sandstone + wind transport
Glacial erratics throughout prairies boulders transported hundreds of km by glaciers (ice sheets) during last ice age; dropped when glacier melted
MISCONCEPTION

"Geological change is always dramatic and fast — like earthquakes and eruptions." Why this is wrong: Most geological change is slow (gradual). The Rocky Mountains grew at the rate of centimetres per year over millions of years. River valleys cut downward millimetre by millimetre per year. The prairies were shaped by glaciers that moved at the speed of a few metres per year (slower than a snail). Dramatic events like floods and earthquakes exist but represent a tiny fraction of Earth's geological history.

Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. What is the difference between weathering and erosion?
    Answer Weathering breaks rock down where it sits. Erosion carries the broken material away.
  2. Give one example of mechanical weathering and one of chemical weathering. Explain the difference.
    Answer Mechanical, e.g., freeze-thaw — water freezes in cracks, expands, and splits the rock. Chemical, e.g., oxidation — iron in the rock reacts with oxygen and rusts. Mechanical changes only the size of the pieces; chemical changes what the rock is made of.
  3. Explain what hoodoos are and how they form in the Alberta Badlands.
    Answer Hoodoos are tall pillars of soft rock topped by a harder cap. The cap protects the rock directly beneath it while wind and water wear away the softer rock around it (differential erosion).
  4. What left the large boulders (glacial erratics) scattered across the Alberta prairies, far from any mountains?
    Answer Glaciers. During the last ice age, ice sheets carried boulders hundreds of kilometres and dropped them when the ice melted.
  5. Stretch: A student says Alberta's flat prairies look that way because "nothing has ever happened there geologically." Using what you know about glaciation, deposition, and time, explain why this is wrong.
    Answer Stretch: A great deal happened. Ice sheets up to kilometres thick covered Alberta, scraped the land, and left a thick, even layer of glacial till when they melted; meltwater lakes left flat beds of fine sediment. The flatness is the result of that deposition. It happened slowly and long ago, not "never."

Section 6: Fossils and Geological Time

Learning target: I can explain how fossils form, use relative dating to interpret rock layers, and describe Alberta's fossil significance.

Vocabulary

Term Definition in your own words One example
Fossil Any preserved evidence of past life Bone, shell, leaf imprint, footprint
Index fossil A fossil of an organism that was common, widespread, and lived for only a short time; used to match rock ages Ammonites, trilobites
Relative dating Working out whether a rock or fossil is older or younger than another, without an age in years "This layer is older than that one"
Law of superposition In undisturbed rock, lower layers are older and upper layers are younger Layers in the Badlands
Geological time scale The timeline of Earth's history, divided into eras Mesozoic era — the age of dinosaurs

Guided Notes

What is a fossil? Any evidence (trace) of past life preserved in rock or sediment. Not just bones.

Types of fossils:

Type How it forms Example
Replacement minerals replace original material atom by atom petrified wood, many dinosaur bones
Mould organism dissolves, leaving a hollow impression (cavity) in rock shell impression
Cast mould fills with minerals, creating a 3D copy (replica) cast of a shell
Compression organism flattened under sediment pressure leaf fossils, fern imprints
Amber preservation organism trapped in tree resin, now hardened insects in amber
Trace fossil preserved activity (behaviour) of an organism, not the organism itself footprints, burrows, coprolites (fossil dung)
MISCONCEPTION

"Fossils are mostly large dinosaur bones." Why this is wrong: Most fossils are small (microscopic / microfossils) — microscopic algae, pollen, bacteria. Large bones are rare because they require very specific conditions to preserve (rapid burial, mineral-rich water, no scavengers). The vast majority of the fossil record consists of shells, marine invertebrates, plant fragments, and microfossils. Alberta's Badlands fossil sites are remarkable precisely because large dinosaur fossils ARE found there — they are the exception.

How fossils form (conditions needed):

  1. Organism must be buried quickly (before decomposition or scavenging)
  2. Burial must occur in sediment (mud, sand, silt) — the only environment where sedimentary rock forms
  3. Mineral-rich groundwater slowly replaces or fills the hard parts over thousands to millions of years

Dating rock layers:

Law of superposition: In undisturbed rock layers, lower (deeper) layers are older and upper (top) layers are younger. This allows relative dating — we can say layer A is older than layer B without knowing the actual age in years.

Index fossils: fossils of organisms that were abundant (common) (found in many locations), widespread (spread across different rock types), and existed for a short time period. If you find the same index fossil in rocks in two different places, those rocks are the same age.

Relative vs. absolute dating:

Method What it tells you Examples
Relative dating Which layers are older or younger than others Law of superposition, index fossils
Absolute dating Actual age in years of the rock or fossil Radioactive decay (not required at Grade 7 level)

Geological time scale — broad eras:

Era Approximate time range Life present
Precambrian 4.6 billion – 540 million years ago simple single-celled life; late period: first multicellular life
Paleozoic 540 – 252 million years ago marine invertebrates, fish, first land plants, first reptiles
Mesozoic 252 – 66 million years ago dinosaur ("Age of Reptiles") era; mammals appear; Alberta's oil sands deposit
Cenozoic 66 million years ago – present mammals diversify; humans appear in last 300,000 years

Alberta's fossil record:

Alberta is one of the world's most significant fossil locations because:

  1. The Mesozoic (Cretaceous period) era rock layers are exposed at the surface in the Badlands (erosion removed overlying rock)
  2. The Cretaceous interior seaway covered Alberta ~75 million years ago → preserved marine fossils AND nearby dinosaur fossils
  3. Royal Tyrrell Museum (Drumheller): world's largest collection of dinosaur fossils; one of Alberta's major scientific assets
  4. Burgess Shale (BC border, UNESCO World Heritage Site): Cambrian marine life (~505 million years ago) — some of the oldest animal fossils ever found
Retrieval Check

    Cover your notes. Answer from memory.

  1. What conditions are needed for fossilization? Why are fossils rare compared to the number of organisms that ever lived?
    Answer Conditions: quick burial in sediment before the organism decays or is eaten; mineral-rich groundwater; a very long time. Fossils are rare because most organisms decay or are eaten first, soft parts seldom preserve, and the right conditions are uncommon.
  2. What is the law of superposition? How does it allow relative dating?
    Answer In undisturbed rock layers, lower layers are older and upper layers are younger. It lets geologists put layers and the fossils in them in order from oldest to youngest without knowing their age in years.
  3. Why is Alberta such a significant location for Cretaceous fossils specifically?
    Answer Alberta's Cretaceous sedimentary layers are exposed at the surface in the Badlands, where erosion has removed the rock above. An inland sea covered Alberta about 75 million years ago, and its coastal sediments buried and preserved both marine life and dinosaurs.
  4. Name two types of fossils other than bones. Explain how each forms.
    Answer Any two with how they form, e.g., mould — the organism dissolves and leaves a hollow impression; cast — a mould fills with minerals to make a 3D copy; trace fossil — a footprint or burrow is preserved; compression — a leaf is flattened under sediment; amber — an insect is trapped in hardened tree resin.
  5. Stretch: You find two rock outcrops 200 km apart. Both contain the same index fossil. What can you conclude about these rocks? What else would you need to know to determine the actual age of the rocks in years?
    Answer Stretch: The two rock layers are the same age — they formed during the short period when that organism lived. To find the age in years she would need absolute dating (measuring radioactive decay in the rock, usually a volcanic ash layer).

4. A geologist finds a rock layer in the Alberta Badlands containing large dinosaur bones, small marine shells, and a thin layer of dark material that might be from an impact event. Using relative dating, what can she conclude about the sequence of events? What would she need to determine the actual date in years?

Answer By relative dating she can put the events in order: what lies lower formed first, so the bones and shells were buried before the dark layer above them was laid down. Marine shells and dinosaur bones together suggest a coastline or sea-edge environment. Known index fossils would tell her which period the layer belongs to. For an age in years she needs absolute dating — measuring radioactive decay in the dark layer or a volcanic ash layer.

More answers for this section
  • Law of superposition In undisturbed rock layers, lower layers are older and upper layers are younger
  • Why oceanic crust subducts It is thinner and denser than continental crust, so it sinks beneath it where they meet
  • Three plate boundary types Divergent (plates move apart); convergent (plates move together); transform (plates slide past each other)
  • Why crystal size differs in igneous rocks Slow cooling underground gives crystals time to grow large; fast cooling at the surface leaves small crystals
  • How Alberta's mountains formed Plates collided along the west coast of North America about 100 million years ago; rock layers were folded and pushed up, then carved by glaciers
  • What Alberta's oil sands are made of Sand and clay (sedimentary deposits from the Mesozoic) mixed with water and bitumen, a thick heavy oil formed from ancient organisms

Four labelled layers with a key property each: crust (thin, solid rock; oceanic is thin and dense, continental is thick and lighter); mantle (thickest layer; hot solid rock that flows slowly; convection currents); outer core (liquid iron and nickel; makes the magnetic field); inner core (solid iron and nickel; solid because of pressure).
