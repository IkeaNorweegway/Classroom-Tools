Part A: Earth's Interior (16 marks)

Q1 — Interior layers: (8 marks — 2 per row).

Label Layer name One key property
a Crust Thin, solid, outer layer. Oceanic crust (basalt) is thin and dense. Continental crust (granite) is thicker and less dense.
b Mantle The thickest layer. Solid rock that flows very slowly over long time spans. Has convection currents.
c Outer core Liquid iron and nickel. Convection here makes Earth's magnetic field.
d Inner core Solid iron and nickel. The hottest layer. Huge pressure keeps it solid even though it's very hot.

Q2 — How scientists know Earth's interior: (4 marks).

Seismic waves, made by earthquakes. When an earthquake hits, seismic waves travel through Earth in all directions. P-waves travel through solids and liquids. S-waves travel only through solids. Scientists at seismograph stations around the world detect these waves. They look at three things. First, how fast the waves travel — speed changes with the material. Second, where S-waves disappear — this "shadow zone" shows the outer core is liquid. Third, where waves bend at layer boundaries. Using all this, scientists can map the depth, makeup, and state of each layer without ever drilling down.

Q3 — Inner core solid despite high temperature: (2 marks).

Huge pressure from the weight of every layer above stops the iron-nickel atoms from spreading apart into a liquid. To melt, particles need enough energy to break free AND enough space to move. The massive pressure at Earth's centre (about 360 GPa) removes that space. So the material stays solid, no matter how hot it is.

Q4 — Liquid outer core importance: (2 marks).

The moving liquid iron-nickel outer core makes Earth's magnetic field. This field pushes the solar wind — fast, charged particles from the Sun — away from Earth's surface. Without it, this radiation would strip away our atmosphere and hit the surface directly. Life as we know it could not exist.

Part B: The Rock Cycle (20 marks)

Q5 — Rock classification: (6 marks — 1 per row).

Rock Type Conditions that formed it
Granite Igneous (intrusive) Magma cooled slowly underground. Large crystals formed over millions of years.
Sandstone Sedimentary (clastic) Sand grains carried by water or wind. Deposited in layers, then packed and cemented together.
Marble Metamorphic Limestone under high heat and pressure, without melting. Its calcite crystals reform.
Obsidian Igneous (extrusive) Volcanic lava cooled very fast at the surface. No time for crystals to form, so it looks like glass.
Coal Sedimentary (organic) Plant material (peat) squeezed and buried under layers for millions of years. Carbon builds up.
Quartzite Metamorphic Sandstone under high heat and pressure. Quartz grains fuse together. Very hard rock.

Q6 — Basalt vs. granite crystal size: (3 + 1 = 4 marks).

a. Basalt cooled fast at Earth's surface, as lava. Atoms had only a short time to line up into crystals before it hardened. So its crystals are small or too tiny to see. Granite cooled slowly underground, over millions of years. Its atoms had lots of time to line up into crystals. So its crystals are large and easy to see. Crystal size shows cooling speed: fast cooling makes small crystals, slow cooling makes large ones.

b. Both are igneous rock. Both hardened from molten rock — magma or lava.

Q7 — Rock cycle triangle: (6 marks).

Three-point triangle diagram must show:

  • Igneous, Sedimentary, and Metamorphic at corners.
  • At least five labelled processes on arrows between them.
  • Igneous → Sedimentary: weathering + erosion + deposition + compaction/cementation.
  • Sedimentary → Metamorphic: heat + pressure (burial/subduction).
  • Metamorphic → Igneous: melting (magma).
  • Any rock → Metamorphic: heat + pressure.
  • Any rock → Igneous: melting.
  • Any rock → Sedimentary: weathering + erosion.

Q8 — Fossils in rock types: (5 marks).

Fossils need two things. First, an organism must be buried fast, before decay destroys its soft parts. Second, low heat and low pressure, so the material is preserved or replaced by minerals. Igneous rock forms from molten magma — that extreme heat would destroy any organism. Metamorphic rock forms under intense heat and pressure, which would also destroy organic material and crush any fossil shape beyond recognition. Sedimentary rock is different. Sand, mud, and silt build up layer by layer in calm places, like river deltas, lake floors, or shallow seas. Organisms can be buried quickly there and preserved before they decay. Minerals slowly replace the organic material over time. That is why fossils are found almost only in sedimentary rock.

Part C: Plate Tectonics (20 marks)

Q9 — Boundary types: (9 marks — 3 per row).

Boundary Definition Feature Real-world example
Divergent Two plates moving apart Mid-ocean ridge. Rift valley. New oceanic crust forms. Mid-Atlantic Ridge; East African Rift
Convergent Two plates moving toward each other Subduction trench (ocean meets continent). Mountain chain (continent meets continent). Volcanic arc. Cascadia Subduction Zone; Rocky Mountains; Himalayas
Transform Two plates sliding horizontally past each other Fault line. Earthquakes. No new crust is made or destroyed. San Andreas Fault (California); Queen Charlotte Fault (BC coast)

Q10 — Three lines of evidence: (6 marks — 2 per piece of evidence).

  1. Continental fit (jigsaw puzzle): the coastlines of Africa and South America fit together like puzzle pieces. This suggests they were once joined, then drifted apart over hundreds of millions of years.
  2. Matching fossils across continents: the same extinct species (Mesosaurus, Glossopteris) show up as fossils on continents that are now far apart. Examples are South America and Africa, or Antarctica and India. These organisms could not swim across open ocean. So the continents must once have been joined.
  3. Seafloor spreading / paleomagnetic striping: magnetic minerals in seafloor basalt record Earth's magnetic field at the time they formed. Stripes of normal and reversed magnetism mirror each other on both sides of mid-ocean ridges. This proves new crust forms at the ridge and spreads outward.

Q11 — Rocky Mountains: (3 marks).

A convergent plate boundary. An oceanic plate (the Juan de Fuca/Farallon plate) subducted under the North American continental plate. As the oceanic plate sank, its front edge scraped up and crumpled the edge of the continent. Huge squeezing forces folded and pushed the rock layers upward. This built the fold-and-thrust belt we call the Rocky Mountains. It began about 100 Ma and continued through the Cretaceous period.

Q12 — Alberta's tectonic past: (2 marks).

Give any two. (1) The Rocky Mountains — a fold-and-thrust belt from a Cretaceous convergent boundary. (2) Old limestone beds from marine seas — Alberta was under ocean during the Paleozoic and Cretaceous. (3) The oil sands — marine material from the Cretaceous, buried and changed by heat and pressure. (4) Precambrian basement rock exposed in northern Alberta — the roots of an ancient mountain belt.

Part D: Earthquakes and Volcanoes (16 marks)

Q13 — What causes an earthquake: (6 marks).

Tectonic plates move all the time, driven by convection in the mantle. Where plates meet, rock along fault lines gets stuck by friction. The plates keep trying to move, so stress builds up in the rock. When that stress gets too strong for the rock to hold, it suddenly breaks and slips. The stored energy releases as seismic waves. The focus is the underground point where the break starts. Seismic waves spread out from the focus in every direction. The epicenter is the point on the surface right above the focus — shaking is usually strongest there. Faults are the cracks where this movement happens. Most earthquakes happen at plate boundaries, where faults are active.

Q14 — Richter vs. Mercalli scales: (4 marks).

Richter Mercalli
What it measures Energy released at the earthquake's source Intensity of shaking felt at a specific location
How it is measured Measured by a seismograph. Each step up is about 10 times more shaking, and about 32 times more energy. Based on reports from people and damage to buildings. Scale runs from I (not felt) to XII (total destruction). Changes with distance from the epicenter.

Q15 — Shield vs. composite volcanoes: (6 marks).

Shield volcano Composite (stratovolcano)
Lava viscosity Low (thin, fluid) High (thick, sticky)
Eruption style Gentle. Lava flows outward. Gas escapes slowly. Explosive. Gas pressure builds, causing a violent eruption. Produces ash and pyroclastic flows.
Shape Low, broad dome (wide base, gentle slopes) Tall, steep-sided cone
Example Mauna Loa, Hawaii Mount St. Helens (Washington); Mount Rainier
Why viscosity determines style Thin lava lets gas bubbles escape easily. No pressure builds up. Thick lava traps gas inside. Pressure builds up until it explodes.

Part E: Weathering, Erosion, and Alberta Landforms (16 marks)

Q16 — Weathering vs. erosion: (3 marks).

Weathering is rock breaking down where it sits. It crumbles, dissolves, or cracks apart, but stays roughly in place. Erosion is moving that broken material somewhere else — carried by water, wind, ice, or gravity. Weathering has to happen first, before erosion can carry anything away. Erosion does not cause the first break-up.

Q17 — Mechanical and chemical weathering: (6 marks).

Mechanical example: freeze-thaw (frost wedging). Water seeps into cracks in rock. When the temperature drops below 0°C, the water freezes and expands by about 9%. This pushes hard on the crack walls. Repeated freezing and thawing widens the crack until the rock splits. This is common in the Rocky Mountain foothills during Alberta's spring and fall. In mechanical weathering, only the rock's shape or size changes — not what it's made of.

Chemical example: oxidation. Iron-bearing minerals in rock react with oxygen and water to form iron oxides — rust and hematite. This breaks down and weakens the original mineral structure. You can see it as orange or red staining on exposed rock faces, common in the Alberta Badlands. In chemical weathering, the rock's mineral makeup actually changes.

Q19 — Geological change is mostly slow: (3 marks).

Most geological processes take millions of years. Even "fast" erosion is far too slow to notice on a human timescale. Here is the math: 2 mm/year × 1,000,000 years = 2,000,000 mm = 2,000 m = 2 km deep. A river eroding just 2 mm a year can carve a valley 2 km deep over a million years. This is why the Red Deer River valley and the Grand Canyon are so impressive. Huge depth comes from tiny changes, added up over geological time.

Part F: Fossils and Geological Time (12 marks)

Q20 — Law of superposition: (3 marks).

In undisturbed, flat rock layers, the oldest layers sit at the bottom and the youngest sit at the top. Each new layer is deposited on top of the ones before it. This helps with relative dating. Scientists read the layers from bottom to top. This way they can build a timeline of which organisms lived before others. They do this without knowing the exact age. Fossils in deeper layers are older than fossils in the layers above them.

Q21 — Two fossil types besides bone replacement: (4 marks).

Any two with correct formation description:

  1. Cast fossil: the original organism dissolves or decays, leaving an empty space (a mould) in the rock. Minerals fill that space over time. This creates a solid 3D copy of the organism's outer shape.
  2. Trace fossil: a record of an organism's activity, not its body — footprints, burrows, feeding marks, or fossilized dung. The organism moves through soft sediment, which later hardens around the mark it left.
  3. Amber fossil: a small organism, often an insect, gets trapped in tree resin. The resin hardens over millions of years. The organism ends up preserved in amazing detail inside the amber.
  4. Carbonized impression: soft body parts are preserved as a thin carbon film on the rock. The organic matter breaks down to leave a flat, 2D outline of the organism.

Q22 — Why Alberta is an important fossil location: (3 marks).

During the Late Cretaceous, about 75 million years ago, Alberta was warm and low, on the coast. It sat next to the Western Interior Seaway. This was a shallow sea that split North America in two. The area supported many kinds of life. Marine organisms lived in the sea. Large groups of dinosaurs (hadrosaurs, ceratopsians, tyrannosaurs) lived in the nearby wetlands and floodplains. Fast burial in river deltas and floodplains preserved many of these organisms. Later, the Rockies rose up and the Red Deer River cut through the land. This exposed these Cretaceous-age layers in the Badlands, making fossils easy to find at the surface. Alberta holds one of the richest collections of Late Cretaceous dinosaur fossils on Earth.

Q23 — Relative dating from layers: (2 marks).

Layer A, with marine invertebrate fossils, is older than Layer B, with dinosaur fossils. That's because Layer A sits beneath Layer B. By the law of superposition, deeper means older in undisturbed layers. So the marine invertebrates in Layer A lived and were preserved before the dinosaur-age organisms in Layer B were laid down.

Part G: Challenge (★) — answer-only

Q24 — Badlands rock column:

a. Oldest to youngest: Layer 1 → Layer 2 → Layer 3 → Layer 4 (law of superposition: deepest = oldest).

b. A shallow marine sea. A warm, saltwater environment. Limestone forms from compressed marine organisms with calcium carbonate shells.

c. Alberta had a coastal environment during the Cretaceous. The Western Interior Seaway bordered the region. Dinosaurs lived in warm forests and floodplains next to the sea. Alberta was warmer and wetter than today, about 75 Ma ago.

d. Scientists think this is the Cretaceous-Paleogene (K-Pg) boundary — the asteroid impact from about 66 Ma ago. Iridium from this moment is found worldwide. The organisms in Layer 3, including the non-avian dinosaurs, went extinct soon after this impact. Huge environmental changes caused it — an impact winter, loss of plant life, and disrupted global temperatures.

e. Alberta had a stable, calm environment throughout this time — first a shallow sea, then rivers and floodplains. No major volcanic activity, tectonic disruption, or erosion broke up the sediment building up. This let layers form one after another, without a break, preserving fossils at each level.
