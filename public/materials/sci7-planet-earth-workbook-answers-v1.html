(c) A student says: "The rock cycle shows that rocks are always changing. It probably takes about 100 years for a rock to go all the way around the cycle." Judge this claim. Why can't a rock cycle diagram show the real time scale?

Answer

(a)

  • Metamorphic → Igneous: melting, then cooling (solidifying). The rock must melt completely into magma, then cool and harden into new igneous rock.
  • Igneous → Sedimentary: weathering, erosion, deposition, then compaction and cementation. The rock is broken into pieces, carried away and laid down in layers, then the layers are squeezed and glued together.
  • Igneous → Metamorphic: heat and pressure. Deep underground, the rock is heated and squeezed until its minerals change, without melting.

(b) Accept any accurate examples:

  • Igneous: granite or other crystalline rock in the Canadian Shield, northeastern Alberta (near Fort Chipewyan); granite boulders (glacial erratics) such as the Okotoks Big Rock.
  • Sedimentary: sandstone and shale in the Drumheller Badlands; limestone in the Rocky Mountains (Banff).
  • Metamorphic: gneiss in the Canadian Shield near Fort Chipewyan; quartzite (often as cobbles in Rocky Mountain rivers).

(c) The claim is wrong. Rock-cycle changes take millions to hundreds of millions of years, not 100 years. Some rocks stay unchanged for billions of years, and rocks do not follow one fixed path around the cycle. A diagram shows the order of possible changes, but it has no time axis and cannot show how slow each step is.

(b) We have never drilled down to the mantle. So how do scientists know what Earth's interior looks like? Name the type of evidence used and explain how it works.

(c) The inner core is under huge pressure and is very hot. Yet the inner core is solid while the outer core is liquid. Explain why pressure matters here. What does this tell you about what makes matter change state inside Earth?

Answer

(a)

  1. Crust: solid rock; thin (about 5–70 km); the coolest layer.
  2. Mantle: hot, mostly solid rock that flows very slowly (convection); the thickest layer.
  3. Outer core: liquid iron and nickel; about 4,000–5,000°C.
  4. Inner core: solid iron and nickel; about 5,000–6,000°C.

(b) Seismic waves from earthquakes. Waves change speed and bend when they pass into different materials, and some waves (S-waves) cannot travel through liquid. By timing when waves arrive at stations around the world, scientists work out where the layers are and whether they are solid or liquid.

(c) The inner core is under such enormous pressure that its particles are squeezed together and cannot spread apart to become a liquid, even at that temperature. The outer core is under less pressure, so it melts. Inside Earth, both temperature and pressure decide the state of matter, not temperature alone.

(b) The marine shale layer was laid down about 75 million years ago. The Badlands are now hundreds of kilometres from any ocean. What does this tell us about what central Alberta looked like during the Cretaceous period? Name two types of organisms you would expect to find as fossils in this layer. Explain your thinking.

(c) The thin volcanic ash layer sits at about 65 Ma. That is the same time as the mass extinction that ended the non-avian dinosaurs. What geological event could spread an ash layer across the whole globe? Why is this layer useful for geologists dating rock layers worldwide, even far from any volcano?

Answer

(a) The marine shale at the bottom (about 75 Ma) is the oldest. In undisturbed layers, the lowest layer was laid down first: this is the law of superposition.

(b) Central Alberta was covered by a shallow inland sea (the Western Interior Seaway). Expected fossils include ammonites (shown), plus marine reptiles such as mosasaurs or plesiosaurs, fish, clams, and other shelled sea animals, because the shale formed from mud on a sea floor.

(c) A huge asteroid impact (or massive volcanic eruptions) threw dust and ash into the atmosphere, which settled worldwide. Because it was laid down at the same moment everywhere, the layer is a time marker: geologists anywhere can match it and know that rock above it is younger than about 65 Ma and rock below it is older.

Answer

  • Panel A: Divergent. Example: Mid-Atlantic Ridge or Iceland. Feature: mid-ocean ridge, rift valley, new crust.

5 ★★ Extended Rock Cycle · LO 7-5-02 · Evaluate
Calgary sits on thick layers of sedimentary rock — mostly limestone and sandstone. Kilometres below those layers lies the Canadian Shield. This is ancient Precambrian igneous and metamorphic rock. It forms the stable core of North America. Calgary is far from any active volcanoes or plate boundaries.

(a) Limestone is a sedimentary rock made mainly of calcium carbonate from marine shells and skeletons. What does the limestone under Calgary tell you about this region's past? Be specific about the environment that must have been here, and about when.

(b) The rock layers under Calgary run, top to bottom: sedimentary layers, then Precambrian igneous/metamorphic basement rock. Explain how igneous and metamorphic rock ended up below sedimentary rock here. What does this order tell you about this region's history?

(c) Rainwater turns slightly acidic as it absorbs CO2 from the air. Predict what happens when this mildly acidic water flows through limestone layers underground near Banff. What type of weathering is this? What landforms could this process create over thousands of years?

Answer

(a) The region was once covered by a warm, shallow sea full of shelled organisms. Their shells and skeletons piled up on the sea floor and hardened into limestone. This happened hundreds of millions of years ago (mainly in the Paleozoic Era, before the dinosaurs).

(b) The igneous and metamorphic basement is much older (Precambrian). It formed first, deep underground, and was later worn down. Then, over millions of years, sediments were laid down on top of it by seas and rivers and hardened into rock. The order shows a long history: ancient rock first, followed by long periods of erosion and then deposition.

(c) The slightly acidic water dissolves the limestone (calcium carbonate). This is chemical weathering. Over thousands of years it can carve caves, sinkholes, and underground channels (karst), and leave mineral deposits such as stalactites. Banff's caves and springs are examples.

Processes to choose from: Glacial deposition  ·  River (fluvial) erosion  ·  Tectonic uplift + glacial carving  ·  Differential erosion by wind and water

(b) Hoodoos are rock pillars with a harder cap rock on top. This cap protects the softer rock below. Explain, step by step, how a hoodoo forms from a flat rock surface. For each step, say whether it is weathering, erosion, or deposition — and whether it is mechanical or chemical.

Answer

(a)

  • Drumheller hoodoos: differential erosion by wind and water. Soft rock wears away faster than the hard cap rock.
  • Flat Alberta prairies: glacial deposition. Melting ice sheets spread a smooth blanket of till.
  • Bow River valley near Banff: river (fluvial) erosion, widened by glaciers. The river cut the valley, and its gravel and terraces show the flowing water. (Accept glacial carving with reasoning; the valley's U-shape shows glaciers.)
  • Rocky Mountain peaks: tectonic uplift + glacial carving. Thrust faulting pushed rock up; glaciers carved sharp peaks and cirques.

(b)

  1. Rain, frost and temperature changes crack the rock (mechanical weathering); some minerals dissolve (chemical weathering).
  2. Water running down cracks and wind carry away the loose pieces (erosion).
  3. Where a hard cap rock sits on top, it shields the softer rock below, while the soft rock around it wears away faster.
  4. Over thousands of years, a pillar is left standing under its cap.
  5. The eroded sediment is laid down in low areas and river valleys (deposition).

7 ★★ Extended Error Analysis Earthquakes & Volcanoes · LO 7-5-04 · Evaluate
Student Claim
"Earthquakes and volcanoes can happen anywhere on Earth. I've seen news stories about eruptions in different places all over the world. So they must happen randomly, all over."

(a) Is this claim true, partly true, or false? Use evidence from plate tectonic theory to back up your answer. Name at least two specific places, and explain why each place has the geological activity it does.

(b) This claim is based on news coverage. Give one reason why news stories about geological events might mislead us. Where do earthquakes and volcanoes really happen most often?

(c) Alberta rarely has earthquakes or big volcanic activity. Use plate tectonics to explain why. What is Alberta's tectonic setting? What evidence in the Alberta landscape shows this was not always true?

Answer

(a) False (or at best partly true). Earthquakes and volcanoes are not random; most happen along plate boundaries. Examples: Japan, where the Pacific Plate subducts under the Eurasian Plate, causing earthquakes and explosive volcanoes; Iceland, on the divergent Mid-Atlantic Ridge, with eruptions as plates pull apart; California, on the San Andreas transform fault, with earthquakes.

(b) News covers events that are dramatic or affect many people, from all over the world. That makes them seem to happen everywhere. Most activity happens in belts along plate edges, especially the "Ring of Fire" around the Pacific Ocean.

(c) Alberta sits in the stable interior of the North American Plate, far from any active plate boundary. But the landscape shows the past was different: the Rocky Mountains were pushed up by plate collision, the mountains are full of folded and faulted rock layers, and Alberta has old volcanic ash layers.

8 ★ Core Fossils & Geological Time · LO 7-5-06 · Analyze
A geologist finds a fossil ammonite — a spiral-shelled marine invertebrate — in limestone on a mountainside in Banff National Park. This spot is about 2,000 metres above sea level. Ammonites lived in the ocean and went extinct about 66 million years ago.

(a) Explain how a marine fossil ends up 2,000 metres above sea level in a mountain range. Use geological processes. Your answer must name at least three processes, in the right order.

(b) The fossil is in limestone, a sedimentary rock. Why does this matter? Could you find the same ammonite fossil in a granite layer or a marble layer? Explain your thinking for each rock type.

(c) A paleontologist says this layer can work as an index fossil, to help date other rock layers worldwide. What must a fossil have to be a good index fossil? Does the ammonite qualify? Back up your answer with at least two specific properties.

Answer

(a)

  1. Ammonites died and sank to the sea floor; their shells were buried by sediment (deposition).
  2. The sediment hardened into limestone (compaction and cementation / lithification), preserving the fossils.
  3. Plate collision pushed the rock layers up along thrust faults (tectonic uplift / mountain building), lifting them thousands of metres.
  4. Erosion and weathering wore away the layers above and exposed the fossil on the mountainside.

(b) Fossils form in sedimentary rock, because the organism is buried gently in sediment. Granite (igneous) forms from melted rock, which would destroy any fossil, so no. Marble (metamorphic) forms under heat and pressure that usually crush or destroy fossils, so almost never.

(c) A good index fossil must: have lived for a short period of geological time; have been widespread (found in many places around the world); and be common and easy to recognize. Ammonites qualify: each species lived for only a short time, they spread through oceans worldwide, and their spiral shells are easy to identify.

9 ★★ Extended Weathering, Erosion & Deposition · LO 7-5-05 · Evaluate
The Rocky Mountains formed about 80–50 million years ago through convergent plate tectonics. At their highest, they may have stood as tall as, or taller than, today's Himalayas, which are still rising. Alberta's Rocky Mountain foothills are now much lower than the main peaks. The peaks themselves have eroded a lot from their tallest height.

(a) What geological process or processes best explain why the foothills and peaks are now much lower than before? Name at least two different processes that both helped lower the height. Explain how each is different.

(b) Say you are a geologist looking for proof that the Rockies were once much taller. What specific feature or deposit would you look for, and where would you look? Explain why this evidence would support your idea.

(c) The Himalayas are still rising, because the Indian Plate is still colliding with the Eurasian Plate. The Rockies stopped rising about 50 Ma ago, when the tectonic forces driving them stopped. What does this comparison tell you about how tectonic activity relates to mountain height over geological time?

"Mountains stop growing when… but continue to shrink because…"
Answer

(a) Any two:

  • Weathering: frost wedging and chemical breakdown crack and weaken the rock.
  • Erosion by glaciers: ice ground down and carved the mountains, carrying huge amounts of rock away.
  • Erosion by rivers: flowing water carried sediment downhill for millions of years.

Weathering breaks rock down where it sits; erosion moves the broken material away.

(b) Look for thick deposits of sediment (gravel, sand, conglomerate) worn off the mountains, in the foothills and plains east of the Rockies. That huge volume of eroded rock had to come from mountains that were once much taller.

(c) "Mountains stop growing when the tectonic forces pushing them up stop (the plates stop colliding) but continue to shrink because weathering and erosion never stop wearing them down." A mountain's height depends on the balance between uplift and erosion; when uplift ends, erosion wins over time.

10 ★★ Extended Plate Tectonics · LO 7-5-03 · Evaluate
Iceland sits right on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, a divergent plate boundary where the Eurasian and North American plates pull apart. Iceland has frequent, fairly gentle eruptions with runny basaltic lava. Japan sits on a convergent boundary, where the Pacific Plate subducts beneath the Eurasian Plate. Japan has large, explosive eruptions (Mt. Fuji is a composite/stratovolcano).

(a) Use the diagram from Question 4 and what you know about plate tectonic theory. Explain why the type of lava and eruption style differs between Iceland and Japan. Your answer must say where the magma comes from in each case, and why its makeup differs.

(b) Both Iceland and Japan have frequent earthquakes. Explain why. Are the causes the same in both places, or different? Be specific about what is happening to the rock in each place.

(c) Judge this claim: "All volcanoes are equally dangerous." Use the Iceland-Japan comparison to decide if this is true. What factors would a scientist look at to judge how dangerous a specific volcano is?

Answer

(a) Iceland: magma rises straight from the mantle as plates pull apart. It is basaltic: low in silica, hot, and runny, so gas escapes easily and eruptions are gentle with flowing lava. Japan: magma forms where the subducting plate sinks and releases water, melting rock that then mixes with continental crust as it rises. It is higher in silica, thick and sticky, and traps gas, so pressure builds and eruptions are explosive.

(b) The causes are different. In Iceland, plates pull apart: the crust cracks and magma moves, causing many small earthquakes. In Japan, plates collide: the subducting plate sticks and then slips suddenly, releasing large amounts of energy in powerful (often deep) earthquakes.

(c) False. Iceland's runny eruptions are usually less deadly than Japan's explosive ones. Scientists would look at: the eruption style and type of magma; the number of people living nearby; how often it erupts and how long since its last eruption; and hazards such as ash, mudflows (lahars), and pyroclastic flows.

11 ★ Core Earthquakes & Volcanoes · LO 7-5-04 · Analyze
A magnitude 6.0 earthquake strikes with a focus 15 km deep, right beneath a city of 500,000 people. A second earthquake, magnitude 4.0, happens 1,000 km away. Its focus is 200 km deep, beneath a small town of 2,000 people. Emergency managers must decide which event needs more resources.

(a) The Richter scale is logarithmic. Each whole-number step means about 10 times more ground shaking, and about 32 times more energy released. How much more energy did the magnitude 6.0 earthquake release than the magnitude 4.0 earthquake? Show your work.

(b) Explain the difference between the Richter scale and the Modified Mercalli Intensity scale. Which scale would help more when deciding which earthquake caused more damage? Why?

(c) The magnitude 4.0 earthquake is farther away, much weaker, and deeper. But the community it hits may still need a lot of support. What factors, other than magnitude and depth, decide how damaging an earthquake is to a community? Name at least three factors. Explain how each one raises or lowers the actual damage.

Answer

(a) The difference is 6.0 − 4.0 = 2 steps. Energy: 32 × 32 = about 1,000 times more energy (about 1,024).

(b) The Richter (magnitude) scale measures the energy released at the source: one number per earthquake. The Modified Mercalli Intensity scale describes the shaking and damage people actually observe in a place, so it varies from place to place. Mercalli is more useful for judging which earthquake caused more damage.

(c) Any three:

  • Building quality and design: weak or old buildings collapse, raising damage.
  • Ground type: soft or loose soil amplifies shaking.
  • Population density: more people nearby raises damage.
  • Emergency preparation and services: good preparation lowers damage.
  • Time of day.
  • Landslides or tsunamis.

A small town with weak buildings and few services can be badly hurt by a weaker quake.

S1 Cross-cluster · Plate Tectonics + Rock Cycle + Alberta · Evaluate

Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift in 1912. Other scientists rejected the idea. Not because the evidence was weak — but because he could not explain a mechanism. He could not say what force was moving the continents.

Name and explain the mechanism we now use to explain plate movement. Then explain why the rock cycle and plate tectonics are closely connected. Why can't you fully explain the rock cycle without plate tectonics, and the other way around? Use at least two examples from Alberta's geology to support your answer.

Answer

The mechanism is convection currents in the mantle. Hot mantle rock slowly rises, spreads sideways under the plates and sinks as it cools, dragging the plates along (plus the pull of sinking plates at subduction zones).

The rock cycle and plate tectonics depend on each other. Plate movement pushes rock deep down (subduction), where heat and pressure make metamorphic rock or melt it into igneous rock. Collisions uplift mountains, which are then weathered into sediment. In turn, the rocks formed tell geologists how plates moved.

Alberta examples: the Rockies' uplifted limestone with marine fossils (sedimentary rock lifted by plate collision); the Precambrian igneous/metamorphic basement formed in ancient mountain-building; the Badlands' sediments, eroded from the rising Rockies.

S2 Cross-cluster · Earth's Interior + Plate Tectonics + Fossils · Analyze / Evaluate

Earth's magnetic field is made by convection currents in the liquid outer core. This field has flipped its north and south poles hundreds of times in geological history. Evidence of these flips is "frozen" into the ocean floor. It shows up as alternating stripes of magnetized rock on either side of mid-ocean ridges. This is called paleomagnetic striping.

Explain how paleomagnetic striping gives evidence for both sea-floor spreading and magnetic field reversals at the same time. Why is the ocean floor a better record of these flips than continental rock? What does this tell you about the age of oceanic crust compared to continental crust, and why?

Answer

New crust forms at a mid-ocean ridge and records the direction of Earth's magnetic field as it cools. As the plates spread apart, that rock moves away from the ridge and newer rock forms in the middle. When the field flips, the new rock records the opposite direction. The result is matching, mirror-image stripes on both sides of the ridge. The mirror pattern proves the sea floor spreads outward, and the alternating stripes prove the field has reversed many times.

The ocean floor is a better record because it forms in a steady, ordered way at ridges, while continental rock is folded, eroded and changed many times. Oceanic crust is much younger (mostly under 200 million years old) than continental crust (up to about 4 billion years old), because oceanic crust is constantly destroyed at subduction zones while lighter continental crust stays on the surface.

S3 Cross-cluster · Full Unit · Create / Evaluate

Write a "geological biography" of Alberta. This is a summary of what this land has been through, from the Precambrian to today. Your biography must include three things. First, at least four geological eras or time periods. Second, at least three geological processes from different clusters in this unit. Third, at least two pieces of physical evidence a person could visit in Alberta today.

Write in full sentences and paragraphs, not point form. Explain it as if to a smart adult who has never studied geology. Use correct geological vocabulary throughout.

Answer

Look for full paragraphs that include four time periods, three processes from different clusters, two places to visit, and correct vocabulary. A strong answer could cover:

  • Precambrian: igneous and metamorphic rock of the Canadian Shield forms deep in the crust, still visible near Fort Chipewyan.
  • Paleozoic: warm, shallow seas lay down limestone full of shelled organisms, now seen in the Rockies near Banff.
  • Mesozoic (Cretaceous): an inland sea covers central Alberta, with ammonites and marine reptiles, while dinosaurs live on the coastal plains (Drumheller and Dinosaur Provincial Park). Late in the period, plate collision begins pushing up the Rockies (thrust faulting), and organic material is buried and becomes the oil sands.
  • Cenozoic: erosion wears down the mountains; the ice ages bury Alberta under glaciers that leave till, erratics (Big Rock near Okotoks) and carved valleys; meltwater rivers carve the Badlands.

Processes named should include, for example, deposition and lithification, tectonic uplift, glacial erosion, and fossil formation.
