## Concept 1: A Working LED Circuit

**I can:** build a basic LED circuit that lights reliably.

A **circuit** is a complete loop a current can flow around — break the loop anywhere and nothing lights. An **LED** needs current flowing the correct direction through it, usually with a resistor to protect it from too much current. This is the foundation everything else in this unit sits on top of: a sensor trigger and a mechanism are both useless if the basic circuit underneath isn't reliable.

## Concept 2: Sensor-Triggered Behavior

**I can:** wire and program a light so that it turns on because of a sensor, not because it's always on.

An always-on light is not the same as a **sensor-triggered** light. A sensor-triggered behavior means a sensor (light, motion, or proximity) reports a reading, and a conditional — the exact same logic you've used since Grade 5, and again in Java this year — decides whether the light should turn on. This unit's constraint requires the *triggered* version specifically: a scene's lighting has to respond to something, not just sit there switched on.

> **Example:**
> ```
> if motion sensor detects movement:
>     turn on window LED
> else:
>     keep window LED off
> ```

## Concept 3: Simple Mechanical Motion (Stretch)

**I can:** describe how a hinge or cam mechanism could create simple motion in a scene.

If you attempt the motion stretch goal, a **hinge** lets a part (a lid, a door) swing open along a fixed pivot, and a **cam** is a shaped piece that turns rotational motion into a different kind of motion (like a push or a lift) as it spins. Either mechanism should be prototyped and tested on its own — separate from the finished scene — before it's built into your final piece, the same "test the sub-system before integrating" habit you used with motors and mechanisms in Grade 8.

## Concept 4: Design Is One Job, Not Two

**I can:** explain why sensor placement and light choice are compositional decisions, not just technical ones.

It's tempting to treat a build like this in two separate stages: get the circuit working, *then* make it look good. That order produces scenes where the light feels bolted on, or a sensor sits somewhere convenient to wire rather than somewhere that makes sense in the story.

> **MISCONCEPTION:** "The art and the engineering are two separate parts of this project — build the technical part, then decorate it."
>
> Why this fails: a light or sensor placed purely for wiring convenience, after the scene's composition is already decided, usually looks stuck-on and can also end up in a spot that doesn't work well technically (a sensor blocked by scenery, a light awkwardly aimed). Composition and circuitry are making claims about the same physical space at the same time.
>
> **Correct understanding:** Where the light sits, what it lights, and what triggers it are compositional decisions — they're part of what the scene is *about* — exactly as much as they're technical decisions about wiring. Plan them together, from your first sketches, not in sequence.
