## Concept 1: Simple Machines, Reviewed

**I can:** identify at least two simple machines working together in one mechanism, and explain the force/motion trade-off each one provides.

You've met all six simple machines before this year: **lever**, **pulley**, **inclined plane**, **wheel-and-axle**, **wedge**, and **screw**. Every one of them does the same basic job — it changes the _____________ or the _____________ needed to move something, but it never creates energy out of nothing.

> 📌 **New word: mechanical advantage**
> What you get when a machine lets you trade one of those for the other — less force for more distance, or more force for less distance. Your apparatus stage almost certainly needs at least two simple machines working together, not just one, to do its job.

## Concept 2: Energy Transfer Between Stages

**I can:** describe, specifically, what form of energy or motion my apparatus hands off to the next one.

Every stage in the chain reaction has to do two jobs: receive some kind of triggering motion from the apparatus before it, and produce a triggering motion for the apparatus after it. > 📌 **New word: hand-off**
> The specific physical form that motion or energy takes when it passes from one stage to the next — a rolling ball, a falling weight, a pushed lever, a released spring. "It sends energy to the next one" is too vague to build against; "a marble arrives at the edge of this ramp moving fast enough to knock over a domino" is specific enough to design for.

**Connection:** Think about the last apparatus you saw in a video or in class. What specific form did the energy take right at the moment it triggered the next thing?

## Concept 3: Reliability & Tolerance

**I can:** explain why "it worked once" is not the same as "it works."

A chain reaction is only as strong as its weakest link. If your apparatus triggers the next one 9 times out of 10, that 1 failure is exactly the run that will happen in front of the audience — reliability across *multiple* runs is the actual bar, not a single lucky success.

> 📌 **New word: tolerance**
> How much a hand-off can vary — a little too slow, a little off-angle, a little short — and still work. A design with tight tolerance (it only works if everything is nearly perfect) is fragile. A design with wider tolerance (it still works even if the ball arrives a bit slower than expected) is much more reliable in a class-wide system where you don't fully control what happens upstream.

## Concept 4: The Hand-off Is Not Someone Else's Problem

**I can:** explain why an apparatus that works perfectly alone is not automatically "done."

> **MISCONCEPTION:** "My apparatus works every single time by itself, so my part of the project is finished."
>
> Why this fails: no other unit this year works this way, so it's an easy trap. Your apparatus doesn't exist by itself in this project — it exists to receive a trigger from one neighbor and reliably produce a trigger for another. An apparatus that is flawless in isolation but never reliably sets off its downstream neighbor has not actually done its job in the chain, because the *system's* success — not your apparatus's success alone — is what the class is building toward.
>
> **Correct understanding:** "Done" means your apparatus reliably triggers its downstream neighbor, across multiple consecutive runs, using a hand-off both groups agreed on *before* either group finalized their build. Isolation success is a necessary first step, not the finish line.
