## Part 1: A Story Needs a Shape

**Goal:** Plan a story with a beginning, problem, solution, and end — before you build anything.

Every story in this unit needs four parts, in this order:

**beginning → problem → solution → end**

> 📌 **New word: storyboard**
> A set of drawn boxes showing what happens in each part of your story, in order. It's your plan on paper, before any code exists.

A story doesn't get better just because *more happens* — more sprites, more effects, more scenes. It gets better when someone else can follow the beginning, problem, solution, and end clearly.

## Part 2: Events & Broadcasts

**Goal:** Use events and broadcasts to control the order things happen in your story.

> 📌 **New word: event**
> A block that says "when this happens, do this." Example: "when this sprite is clicked." This is the same idea as a conditional from Unit 1 — now used in a story instead of a robot.

> 📌 **New word: broadcast**
> A message one sprite sends that other sprites can listen for. This is how you make something happen for *everyone* at once — like a backdrop changing or a new character appearing.

> **Example:**
> ```
> when this sprite clicked
> broadcast [door opens]
>
> when I receive [door opens]
> switch backdrop to [inside house]
> ```

**Common mistake:** "Coding is the boring math part, and art is the fun part — two separate jobs."

**Why that's wrong:** The same sequence → loop → conditional ideas that moved Dash through a maze are what move your story from scene to scene. A beautifully drawn character with no working events is just a picture, not a story. A perfectly coded story with no character design gives the audience nothing to connect with. Neither one works alone.

**What's actually true:** Code and art do the *same job* here — they both control what the audience sees and when. Designing your character and planning your broadcasts are both design decisions about the same story.

## Part 3: Designing a Character on Purpose

**Goal:** Explain one specific design choice behind how your character or backdrop looks.

Every choice in a character's design — shape, color, size, expression — sends a message before the character says a word. Sketching on paper first, before building the sprite on the computer, lets you try out shapes and looks quickly.

## Part 4: This Story Has a Real Job to Do

**Goal:** Explain what a branch point is, and why a real audience changes how you build the story.

This is the capstone — the biggest project of the year. Every story needs at least one branch.

> 📌 **New word: branch**
> A moment where the story splits based on a choice, instead of always running the same path. A branch is built from the same event/broadcast tools you already know — just used to send the story down one of two paths.

Your story is also being shared with a **real audience beyond your class** — another class, or a family/school screening. This isn't just a nicer ending. It's a rule you have to build for, the same way a materials budget is a rule in other units. It changes your decisions, because someone who doesn't already know your story has to be able to follow it.

> **Example:**
> ```
> when [choice] flag clicked
> ask "Help the fox or run away?" and wait
> if <answer = [help]> then
>     broadcast [brave ending]
> else
>     broadcast [safe ending]
> ```
