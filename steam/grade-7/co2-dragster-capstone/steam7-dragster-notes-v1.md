## Concept 1: Propulsion — Action and Reaction

**I can:** explain, using Newton's third law, why a CO2 cartridge makes a dragster move.

Newton's third law says every action has an equal and opposite reaction. When the CO2 cartridge releases gas rapidly out the back of the car, that gas rushing backward is the **action** — and the car being pushed forward is the **reaction**.

> 📌 **New word: propulsion**
> The force that pushes something forward. Here, it comes from gas shooting backward out of the cartridge.

The car isn't "pulled" forward by anything. It's *pushed* forward by the same force that's shooting gas the other way.

## Concept 2: Friction and Drag — Working Against Speed

**I can:** name friction and aerodynamic drag as two forces working against a moving dragster, and explain what each one is.

> 📌 **New word: friction**
> A force between two surfaces in contact — here, your wheels and axles, and the car's contact with the track.

> 📌 **New word: aerodynamic drag**
> Air resistance pushing back against the car's shape as it moves through the air. A separate problem from friction.

A car can have low wheel friction but a boxy, high-drag shape — or the reverse. They're two separate problems, and a fast dragster has to manage both.

## Concept 3: Weight vs. Shape — Letting the Data Decide

**I can:** explain why weight alone doesn't determine how fast a fixed-propulsion car accelerates.

**Common Misconception:** "Lighter is always faster."

**Why this fails:** The CO2 cartridge delivers a **fixed** amount of propulsion force, no matter how the car is shaped or how much it weighs. A lighter car does accelerate faster for the *same* force — but if shaving weight means a rougher, draggier shape, the extra drag can cancel out (or even outweigh) the benefit of being lighter. The reverse mistake is just as common: assuming shape barely matters compared to weight. Neither claim holds up until you actually test it.

> 📌 **New word: acceleration**
> How quickly something speeds up. A car with more acceleration reaches top speed faster.

**What's actually true:** With a fixed propulsion force, weight *and* shape (which controls drag) both affect the result — and how much each one matters is something your own time-trial data will show you, not something to assume in advance. That's why this unit's redesign step exists: to let a real test settle the question instead of guessing.

## Concept 4: Subtractive Shaping — A New Kind of Design Space

**I can:** explain how shaping a fixed wood blank is a different design space than building something up from parts.

Every earlier build in this program — robots, marble runs, filters, boxes — has been **additive**: you start with nothing (or a kit of separate pieces) and add or assemble parts together.

> 📌 **New word: blank (wood)**
> A single solid block of wood you start with, before any shaping.

> 📌 **New word: subtractive shaping**
> Removing material to reveal the shape you planned, instead of adding anything to it. This unit is different from every earlier one because of this.

This matters for how you plan. Once wood is removed, you can't put it back — so the "sketch before you build" habit matters more here than almost anywhere else in the program, because a mistake in shaping usually can't be undone the way a bad code block or a wrong glue joint can.
