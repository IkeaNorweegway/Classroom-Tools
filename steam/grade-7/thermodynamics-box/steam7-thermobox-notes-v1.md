## Concept 1: Heat Transfer — Three Ways Heat Moves

**I can:** name conduction, convection, and radiation, and give an everyday example of each.

Heat moves in three ways.

> 📌 **New word: conduction**
> Heat moving through direct contact. Touch a hot pan, and your hand feels it.

> 📌 **New word: convection**
> Heat moving through a moving fluid — air or liquid. Warm air rising off a radiator is convection.

> 📌 **New word: radiation**
> Heat moving as waves, with no contact needed at all — the warmth you feel from the sun or a campfire without touching either one.

A good **insulator** doesn't stop heat completely — it *slows* one or more of these three transfers.

## Concept 2: What Actually Makes a Good Insulator

**I can:** explain why a material insulates well, using air pockets and material type — not just thickness.

**Common Misconception:** "Thicker automatically means better insulation."

**Why this fails:** Two materials of the exact same thickness can perform completely differently. A solid slab of one material can transfer heat faster than a thin layer of a material full of trapped air pockets. Trapped air barely moves, so it's a poor conductor and a poor path for convection — that's why fluffy or foamy materials, with lots of tiny air pockets, often outperform solid, dense materials of the same thickness.

**What's actually true:** What matters is the *material type* (how well it conducts heat) and how many air pockets it traps — not just how thick it is. Your comparative material tests this unit are designed to let the data show you this directly. Don't assume the thickest option wins before you test it.

## Concept 3: Light — Opacity, Reflectivity, and Gaps

**I can:** explain how opacity, reflectivity, and gaps each affect how much light enters an enclosed space.

*(This concept applies if your box is tackling the Light Challenge — read it even if you're doing Insulation, since your class will share design-review vocabulary either way.)*

> 📌 **New word: opacity**
> How much a material blocks light from passing through it. Fully opaque blocks all of it; fully transparent blocks none.

> 📌 **New word: reflectivity**
> How much light a surface bounces away, rather than absorbing or letting it through.

Even a highly opaque material can let in far more light than expected if it has small gaps — seams, joints, or holes light can sneak through.

A related mistake to the insulation one: "more material always blocks more light" is the same logic error as "thicker always insulates better." A material with high opacity but poor edge-sealing can leak more light through its gaps than a thinner, well-sealed material with modest opacity.

## Concept 4: Data Logging — Reading a Trend, Not Just One Number

**I can:** explain why readings at consistent intervals matter, and read a trend from a data log.

A single temperature or light reading tells you almost nothing on its own.

> 📌 **New word: data log**
> A set of readings taken at consistent intervals — the same amount of time apart, every time — so you can compare results fairly across different box designs.

If one test logs every 30 seconds and another logs every 5 minutes, you can't honestly compare them.

> 📌 **New word: trend**
> The pattern the readings show over time — rising, falling, leveling off — not any single number by itself.

Reading a trend correctly is what lets you say something real about your design, instead of just "the number was 24."
