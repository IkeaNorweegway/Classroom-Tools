## Part 1: Shape Resists Load — Triangulation

**Goal:** Explain how shape, especially triangulation, affects a structure's ability to resist load. Use the vocabulary of forces.

Before designing anything, this unit starts with quick shape tests: a braced triangle versus an unbraced square, and a flat paper strip versus a folded or corrugated one.

> 📌 **New word: load**
> The weight or force pushing on a structure.

An unbraced square can twist and collapse sideways under load, because its corners can change angle. A triangle can't do that — its three fixed side lengths lock its angles in place.

> 📌 **New word: triangulation**
> Using triangle shapes to lock a structure's angles in place, so it can't twist or collapse.

> 📌 **New word: truss**
> A structure built from a repeated pattern of triangles, for exactly this reason.

A flat **beam** sags because all its material is doing the same job in the same flat plane. Fold or corrugate that same amount of material, and it resists bending far better — the shape is spreading the load differently, not adding strength from nowhere.

## Part 2: More Material Isn't the Same as Stronger

**Goal:** Explain why shape matters more than how much material you use. Design inside a stated materials budget.

**Common mistake:** "More material = stronger."

**Why that's wrong:** A thick, unbraced beam can still bend or **buckle** under load, while a thinner structure built as a well-triangulated truss can hold far more. The difference is how the material is arranged, not how much of it there is. Unlimited material lets you brute-force strength without ever thinking about shape — which is exactly why this unit gives you a fixed budget.

> 📌 **New word: budget (constraint)**
> A limit on how much material you can use — a set number of craft sticks/straws and a limited amount of tape.

**What's actually true:** Strength comes from how load travels through a shape — triangulated, spread across multiple parts, carried in the direction a material resists best — not from piling on more of the same material. A smart shape beats a heavy shape almost every time.

A structure that **looks** sturdy doesn't always hold weight. That's exactly why this unit tests with real, added weight instead of guessing from appearance.

## Part 3: Failure Is Data, Not a Mistake

**Goal:** Predict a likely failure point before testing. Use the test result to guide a redesign.

Your bridge will be tested by adding weight a little at a time, until it fails or reaches a target load. Before that test, you predict **where** the structure will fail first (a specific beam, joint, or **span** section).

Failing at that point — or somewhere else entirely — isn't a bad result. It's real information about where your structure was actually weakest, which tells you exactly what to redesign.

**Think about it:** Has something you built before (blocks, cardboard, Lego) ever failed at a spot you didn't expect? What did that spot have in common with other weak points?
