A grain elevator — one of the most recognizable structures on the Alberta prairie — is not a single simple shape. It's a rectangular prism with a pyramid on top, and finding how much grain it can hold or how much material it takes to build means breaking it into pieces you already know how to measure.

This unit builds the skills to measure curved and combined 3-D shapes, then puts them together to handle real composite objects.

## What you will explore

You will extend surface area and volume formulas beyond prisms and cylinders to include **cones**, **pyramids**, and **spheres** — each with its own formula, but each also connected back to simpler shapes. A cone's volume, for instance, is exactly one-third the volume of a cylinder with the same base and height, which is a relationship worth understanding, not just memorizing.

You will then work with **composite objects**: shapes built from two or more simpler solids combined, like a cylinder topped with a cone, or a prism with a pyramid roof. The key skill is deciding which surfaces are actually on the *outside* of the combined shape (a face where two solids join is not part of the surface area) and adding or subtracting volumes correctly depending on whether a piece is added on or removed.

## By the end of this unit

You will be able to calculate the surface area and volume of cylinders, cones, spheres, and pyramids individually, and combine them correctly to find the surface area and volume of a composite object — identifying exactly which faces count and which don't.
