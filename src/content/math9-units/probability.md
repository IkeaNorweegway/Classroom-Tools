A hockey draft lottery, a card game at a Métis social, and a weather forecast all rely on the same underlying math — probability — even though they feel completely different. Understanding how likely something is, and whether one event changes the odds of another, turns out to matter in games, forecasting, and decision-making alike.

This unit builds probability from first principles and applies it to real, sometimes unexpected, situations.

## What you will explore

You will calculate **theoretical probability** (what should happen, based on counting outcomes) and compare it against **experimental probability** (what actually happens when you run a trial or simulation), and explore why the two don't always match exactly — especially with a small number of trials.

The core distinction of the unit is between **independent events** (one event's outcome has no effect on the next, like separate coin flips) and **dependent events** (where the outcome of one event changes the probability of the next, like drawing cards from a deck without replacement). You will learn to calculate the combined probability of multiple events happening in sequence, using multiplication for both types — but recognizing why the individual probabilities themselves need to be recalculated at each step for dependent events.

## By the end of this unit

You will be able to calculate theoretical and experimental probability for single and combined events, correctly identify whether a sequence of events is independent or dependent, and calculate the combined probability accordingly — including in real contexts like games, draws, and everyday chance situations.
