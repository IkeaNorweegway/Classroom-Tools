A cell phone plan that charges $15 a month plus 5 cents a minute is not just a bill — it is a linear relation, and once you can see it that way, you can predict the cost for any number of minutes without a single phone call.

This unit is about recognizing that pattern wherever it shows up, and moving fluently between the different ways of describing it.

## What you will explore

You will work with the same relationship represented three ways: as a **table of values**, as a **graph**, and as an **equation** — and learn to translate between them in either direction. You will pay close attention to **slope** (the rate of change — how fast one quantity changes relative to the other) and the **y-intercept** (the starting value, before any change has happened), and connect both to real, physical meaning: a steeper slope means a faster rate; a larger y-intercept means a higher starting cost.

You will also distinguish between **direct variation** (a relationship that starts at zero, like distance and time at a constant speed) and **partial variation** (a relationship with a fixed starting amount, like the phone plan's base fee) — a distinction that shows up constantly in real pricing and rate problems.

## By the end of this unit

You will be able to take a real situation — a rental cost, a savings plan, a rate of fill — and model it as a linear relation in whichever form is most useful, then use that model to answer questions like "when do these two plans cost the same?"
