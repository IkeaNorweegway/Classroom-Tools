Grab a metal spoon and a wooden spoon that have both been sitting on the same counter for an hour. The metal one *feels* colder. It isn't — they are exactly the same temperature. What is actually different is how quickly each material moves heat away from your hand. That single experiment is the whole unit in miniature: temperature and the feeling of hot or cold are not the same thing.

This unit untangles heat from temperature, and builds a model — using particles — for what is actually happening at a scale too small to see.

## What you will explore

You will define **temperature** as a measure of the average kinetic energy of particles, and **heat** as energy that transfers from a warmer object to a cooler one. That distinction explains why a swimming pool holds more heat energy than a cup of boiling water even though the cup is at a much higher temperature — there is simply far more matter (and far more moving particles) in the pool.

From there, you will study the three modes of heat transfer: **conduction** (through direct contact, particle to particle — why metal feels cold), **convection** (through the movement of fluids like air or water), and **radiation** (through electromagnetic waves, which needs no matter at all — how the Sun's heat crosses empty space to reach Earth). You will also explore thermal expansion, why bridges and railway tracks need expansion gaps, and why insulation works by slowing conduction and convection rather than "keeping cold out."

Specific heat capacity rounds out the unit: some materials (like water) require a huge amount of energy to change temperature, which is exactly why Alberta's lakes stay cool long into summer and why coastal climates are milder than inland ones.

## By the end of this unit

You will be able to explain, using particles, why the metal spoon feels colder without actually being colder, identify which mode of heat transfer is at work in a given situation, and explain how insulation, expansion gaps, and specific heat capacity show up in the buildings, vehicles, and climate around you.
