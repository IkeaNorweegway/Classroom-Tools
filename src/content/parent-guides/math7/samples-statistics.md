title: Samples & Statistics
plainSummary: "Your child is learning how we find out about a big group without asking everyone. First they name the population: everyone a question is about. Asking all of them is a census. When that costs too much or takes too long, we ask a sample, a smaller part of the group. A fair sample is picked at random, like names drawn from a hat, so everyone has the same chance. Then they sum up the data with four numbers: the mean (add and share equally), the median (the middle value once the numbers are in order), the mode (the most common value) and the range (biggest minus smallest). Last, they compare two samples and make a careful conclusion about the whole group, using words like \"about\"."

### Example 1: Picking a fair sample

A class list has 30 names. Pick a sample of 6.

1. **Find the jump.** 30 ÷ 6 = 5, so pick every 5th name.
2. **Pick a random start** from 1 to 5. Roll a die: say 2.
3. **Count on by 5.** 2, 7, 12, 17, 22, 27.
   *Say:* "Did every student have the same chance to be picked?"

**Common slip:** asking 6 friends instead. Friends are alike, so the sample is biased.

### Example 2: The four statistics

Eight students read for 20, 30, 15, 30, 45, 20, 30 and 10 minutes.

1. **Mean.** Add: 200. Divide by 8: 25 minutes.
2. **Median.** In order: 10, 15, 20, 20, 30, 30, 30, 45. The middle two are 20 and 30, so the median is 25.
3. **Mode.** 30 comes up 3 times. **Range.** 45 − 10 = 35.
   *Say:* "Which number comes up most often? Is that the same as the biggest?"

**Common slip:** saying the mode is 45 because it is the biggest.

### Example 3: Two samples, one conclusion

Two samples of Grade 7 students give screen time. Sample A has a mean of 3 hours. Sample B has a mean of 3.6 hours.

1. **Compare.** The means are close but not the same.
2. **Conclude.** A Grade 7 student gets about 3 to 4 hours of screen time.
   *Say:* "Why might a second sample give a different mean?"

**Common slip:** saying every student watches exactly 3 hours. A sample gives an estimate.
