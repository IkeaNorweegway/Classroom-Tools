### Example 1: Describing a trend

**Hours studied and test scores: (1, 55), (2, 62), (3, 70), (4, 74), (5, 83). Describe the trend and predict the score for 3.5 hours.**

1. **Plot the points.** Hours across the bottom (they come first); score up the side (it depends on hours).
2. **Describe the trend.** As hours go up, scores go up: a strong positive correlation.
   *Say:* "When one goes up, what does the other do?"
3. **Draw a line of best fit** through the middle of the dots, then read it at 3.5 hours: about 72.
4. **This prediction is inside the data range** (interpolation), so it's reasonable.

### Example 2: Does it prove cause?

**A headline says "Students who eat breakfast get better grades." Does breakfast cause better grades?**

1. **The data shows a correlation:** the two things go together.
2. **Look for other explanations.** Students who eat breakfast may also sleep more or have more regular routines.
   *Say:* "What else could explain this?"
3. **Conclusion:** the data suggests a link, but it doesn't prove that breakfast causes the grades.

### Example 3: Spotting a biased sample

**A gym surveys its own members: 95% say people should exercise more. Is this fair evidence about everyone in town?**

1. **Who was asked?** Only gym members, who already like exercise.
2. **That's a biased sample.** It doesn't represent the whole town.
3. **A better survey** would ask a random selection of people from the whole town.
