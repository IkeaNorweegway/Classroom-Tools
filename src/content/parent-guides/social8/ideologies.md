### Example 1: The person or the group?

**Should everyone in a town pay for a new hockey rink, even people who won't use it?**

1. **An individualist view:** people should pay for what they choose to use. Each person decides how to spend their own money.
2. **A collectivist view:** a rink helps the whole community, so everyone chips in.
   *Say:* "What would someone who puts the person first say? What about someone who puts the group first?"
3. **Your child's view:** they can pick either side, as long as they give a reason.

### Example 2: Placing ideas on the spectrum

**Where do these ideas go on the political spectrum? (a) "The government should own the big industries so everyone shares the profits." (b) "Government should stay small and let people make their own choices."**

1. **(a)** puts the group first and gives the government more control: **left**.
2. **(b)** puts the individual first: **right**.
   *Say:* "Who is making the decisions in each idea?"
3. **Remember:** the spectrum is a rough guide, and where the middle sits can change from country to country.

### Example 3: Reading a primary source

**Your child is shown a 1930s government poster. How should they read it?**

1. **Who made it?** The government.
2. **Why?** To persuade people to support the government's ideas.
3. **Who was it for?** Ordinary citizens.
4. **What's left out?** Whose voices and experiences are missing, especially people the government excluded or persecuted.
   *Say:* "What did the people who made this want you to think?"

**Common slip:** treating a source as plain fact instead of asking who made it and why.
