### Example 1: How Canada gets a prime minister

**Walk through a federal election.**

1. **Canada is split into ridings.** Each riding elects one Member of Parliament (MP).
2. **Count the seats.** The party that wins the most ridings usually forms the government.
3. **That party's leader becomes prime minister.** The party with the second-most seats becomes the Official Opposition.
   *Say:* "Did anyone's ballot have the prime minister's name on it?" (Only in the leader's own riding.)

**Common slip:** saying Canadians "vote for the prime minister".

### Example 2: Canada and the U.S.

**Compare how each country chooses and keeps its leader.**

1. **Canada:** the prime minister leads the party with the most support in the House of Commons, and must keep that support to stay in power. Elections usually happen every four years.
2. **United States:** the president is elected separately from Congress for a fixed four-year term, up to two terms, and can veto laws Congress passes.
   *Say:* "In which country could the leader lose their job by losing a vote in the legislature?" (Canada.)

### Example 3: Party systems

**Match each country to its party system: China, the United States, Canada.**

1. **China: one-party.** Only one party holds power.
2. **United States: two-party.** Two main parties win almost all seats.
3. **Canada: multiparty.** Several parties win seats, like the Liberals, Conservatives, NDP, Bloc Québécois and Greens.
