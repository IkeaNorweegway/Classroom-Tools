After the Second World War, Canada changed faster than at almost any other point in its history. Soldiers came home, refugees and displaced persons arrived from a shattered Europe, and cities that had been small before the war suddenly needed housing, roads, schools, and sewers for thousands of new families. This unit asks a simple question with a complicated answer: what happens to a country when its cities grow faster than anyone planned for?

## What you will explore

You will trace the **push and pull factors** that brought immigrants to Canada after 1945, including Holocaust survivors and other displaced persons, and see how this reshaped Canada's cities into more diverse, pluralistic places. From there you will follow the money and the pipelines: the growth of **oil and gas exploration**, **hydroelectric power**, and **automobile and paper production** that both fed and fed off the boom in urban population.

Growth is never free. You will weigh the benefits of urbanization — jobs, infrastructure, new industries — against its costs: **pollution**, **land consumption**, the division of farmland and wilderness into subdivisions, and the disruption of wildlife migration routes. By the end, you should be able to argue, with evidence, whether the postwar urban boom was worth what it cost.

## By the end of this unit

You will be able to relate patterns of immigration to push and pull factors, explain how Canada's demographics changed after the Second World War, and evaluate the social, economic, and environmental consequences of urbanization and industrial growth — including the emergence of the petroleum industry.
