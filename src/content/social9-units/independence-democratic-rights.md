In 1919, Canada still signed its treaties under Britain's shadow. By 1931, it didn't have to. This unit is about the quiet paperwork of becoming a country — the declarations and statutes that turned a self-governing colony into a fully sovereign nation — and about the much louder fight to decide who actually counted as a full citizen once that sovereignty existed.

## What you will explore

You will trace how the **1926 Balfour Declaration** and the **1931 Statute of Westminster** transformed Canada's political independence, and consider why Britain let go: a mix of post-war exhaustion, pressure from the Dominions, and a pragmatic bet that cooperation would keep more influence than coercion ever could. You will look at what full control over **foreign policy** actually changed for a country that, on paper, had always looked independent.

At the same time, you will examine the **Persons Case**, in which the **Famous Five** — five prominent Alberta women — launched a legal challenge that redefined the status of women in Canadian law. By establishing that women were legal "persons," the case affirmed their right to hold appointed or elected office, reinforcing the democratic principle of equality decades before it was fully realized in practice.

## By the end of this unit

You will be able to analyze how the Balfour Declaration and the Statute of Westminster transformed Canada's political independence, explain how gaining control over foreign policy contributed to Canada's sovereignty, explain how the Persons Case changed the distribution of political power and participation, and compare how independence and expanded rights together reshaped Canadian identity.
