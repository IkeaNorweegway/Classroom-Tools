When Britain declared war on Germany in September 1939, Canada did not automatically follow — for the first time, its own Parliament debated and declared war independently, a full week later. That one-week delay was small in military terms and enormous in symbolic ones: it was proof that the sovereignty won on paper in 1931 was now being exercised in practice, in the middle of the century's largest conflict.

## What you will explore

You will follow Canada's war from the **Defence of Hong Kong (1941)** and the costly **Dieppe Raid (1942)**, through the **Italian Campaign (1943)**, to **D-Day and the Battle of Normandy (1944)** and the **liberation of France, Belgium, and the Netherlands (1944–45)**. More than one million Canadians served, and by 1945 the country had built one of the largest navies and air forces in the world — a staggering feat for a nation of just over 11 million people.

You will also look past the official narrative to the people often left out of it: **Chinese Canadians** who served despite being denied full citizenship rights at home, and the **Cree code talkers** — First Nations and Métis soldiers who developed a military coding system based on the Cree language that proved invaluable to Allied communications. Their contributions raise a pointed question about who gets to be a "full" citizen of a country they're risking their life for.

## By the end of this unit

You will be able to analyze how Canada's independent declaration of war demonstrated its growing sovereignty, use evidence from key battles to explain Canada's contribution to Allied success, explain the significance of contributions from groups such as Chinese Canadians and the Cree code talkers, and evaluate the extent to which the Second World War was a turning point in Canada's development as an independent, influential nation.
