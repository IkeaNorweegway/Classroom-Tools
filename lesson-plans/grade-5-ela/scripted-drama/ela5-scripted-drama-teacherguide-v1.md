## At a glance

Students read, rehearse and perform three short plays (a lunchroom mystery, a fable rematch, and *Under the Dome* adapted from the Science Fiction unit). They learn how scripts work, how characters are revealed through dialogue and action, and how actors use **verbal, non-verbal and paraverbal** language and project their voices. Then, in groups of four or five, they **adapt a story from this year** (or create their own) into a short play, each student writing at least one scene, and perform it at a **Performance Festival**. Lesson 30 closes the year with a reflection across all six units.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Reading** | Script features; characters and events in scripts; comparing a fable with its rematch; story vs. script |
| **Writing** | POW + script planner; adapting narration into dialogue and stage directions; lines that show character; script punctuation |
| **Word work** | -tion, -ance, -ive, -ment, ex-, trans-; verbs of speaking; punctuation for delivery; whole-year review |
| **Fluency** | Rehearsal as repeated reading; vocal warm-ups; cue cards |
| **Oral language** | Verbal, non-verbal, paraverbal; projection; collaboration and consensus |
| **Assessment** | Six exit tickets; cold reads in Lessons 14 and 27; rubric (scene, performance, teamwork) |

**Why it is built this way:** rehearsing a script is repeated reading with a real reason, one of the most motivating fluency routines (research §5, reader's theatre). Adapting a story they already know means students spend their thinking on the script form and character, not on inventing a plot (research §2: knowledge frees up working memory). The script planner extends POW + WWW from earlier units; it is this workspace's adaptation, not a published SRSD strategy. Group work follows principle 8: roles, a product, and a feedback routine.

## The daily block, groups and editions

Warm-ups replace word work on rehearsal days. **Groups:** fluency (rehearse lines with repeated reading and feedback), comprehension (story-to-script with heavy support), and the play groups themselves. **Edition B:** word box per anchor page, ✎ model rows, starters (including script-line frames). **Edition C:** shorter scripts with the same characters; the Edition C student can perform those lines inside their group's play; write a part by drawing a storyboard and dictating lines.

**Group roles** (rotate if time allows): writer (keeps the master script), director (watches the whole stage, gives notes), stage manager (props, sound, cues). Everyone acts. Set norms: every idea gets tried before it's rejected; disagreements are solved by trying both versions.

**Error patterns:** reading stage directions aloud; quotation marks in scripts; lines that tell feelings ("I am angry"); facing away from the audience; one student writing the whole script; characters who all sound the same.

## Week 4 · Write

::: lesson 16 | Model a scene | WR12, WR16
SHOW: Write a scene aloud from a tall tale (script below).
DO: Notebook L16.
:::

::: teacher Think-aloud script for Lesson 16 (adapt freely)
"I'm adapting *Big Joe Mufferaw*: the night he left boot prints on the ceiling. **Setting first:** where are we? 'An inn in Bytown, 1830s. Raftsmen at long tables. A low wooden ceiling.' What will the audience see first? Joe, ducking through the door. **First line:** who speaks first? Someone who doesn't believe the stories. 'RAFTSMAN 1: (laughing) So you're the famous Big Joe. You don't look so big.' **Stage directions:** how would Joe react? Not angry; he's confident. '(smiling, setting down his hat)'. Can his line *show* that instead of telling it? 'JOE: Stories grow. Boots don't.' Good. **Ending:** what's the last line? After the jump: 'RAFTSMAN 1: (staring up at the boot prints) …Those are going to be there a long time.' Strong. That scene's done."
:::
