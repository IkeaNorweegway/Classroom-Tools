## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **Outcome** | 9-EX: Students relate experiences and employment opportunities to career pathways. |
| **Understandings** | Experiences can spark interest in career pathways. Employment opportunities can contribute to career pathways. |
| **Driving question** | How can an experience I create move me toward my career? |
| **Student materials** | Mini-workbook `cefl9-experiences-workbook-v1` · project brief `cefl9-experiences-project-v1` |
| **Teams** | 4–5. One venture per team; one cover letter and digital-identity check per student. |
| **Audience** | A pitch panel (staff, community members, local business owners), Session 7 |
| **Game** | *Pitch It* (CEFL Town), on the unit page, optional: one chapter after each of Sessions 1–4. See "The Pitch It game" below. |
| **Assessment** | Single-point rubric on the report-card scale. No unit test. |

::: teacher
**Before Session 1:**
- **Book the panel** for Session 7; local business owners or a school administrator make it feel real.
- **Hook examples:** find three real youth ventures (local news stories, the school store, a student club) or describe composite ones.
- **Ask your school** what work experience, RAP or dual-credit options exist for Grade 10, so Moment 1 points to real next steps.
- **If ventures will run for real**, check school policy on selling, handling money and permission forms.
:::

## Why it is built this way

- **Driving question first.** Real youth ventures in the hook show that 15-year-olds can create experiences, not just wait for them.
- **Experience as a career tool.** Volunteering, work-integrated learning and small ventures are linked to better adult work outcomes (OECD career readiness indicators).
- **Two products.** The team venture practises entrepreneurship; each student's cover letter and digital-identity check covers attaining employment.
- **A panel with real questions.** It tests whether the plan is realistic.

## The Pitch It game (CEFL Town)

A companion game on the unit page, one mission in **CEFL Town**, the top-down town every CEFL unit shares. Each student makes a character once, or keeps an earlier one. Mr. Osei at the Community Centre helps them run a small stall at the Saturday Market (bike tune-ups, custom buttons or plant starts) and pitch it to a panel.

| Chapter | Play after | What happens in town | Then, in the project |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 · Experiences that open doors | Session 1 (Moment 1) | Sort volunteering, work-integrated learning and entrepreneurship, and pick a venture. Theo is packing up his sticker table: it always loses money. | Step 1 |
| 2 · A simple business plan | Session 2 (Moment 2) | Zoe's bike business, then their own costs, price and break-even, and risks. Market Day 1: the Stall Rush game (making change) sets how many customers they serve. Then Theo's numbers. | Step 2 |
| 3 · Skills | Session 3 (Moment 3) | Sort formal and informal skills, job-specific and employability skills, plan roles for the venture, and inspect Theo's notebook of "rules." | Step 3 |
| 4 · Getting hired | Session 4 (Moment 4) | Digital identity, a short cover letter, clean up Theo's public profile, Market Day 2, then the pitch panel. | Step 4 |

- **When:** sessions are full, so set each chapter as homework, early-finisher work, or a device day before the next session. Chapters play in order. Chapter length has not been timed with students yet.
- **Saving:** the game saves in that browser, and the character carries over to every CEFL Town mission. To move to another computer, use Menu → **Take your town home** (a save code or a file).
- **Controls:** arrow keys or tap to walk, Enter or tap to talk. Tapping the gold goal line at the top walks the student there, which suits students who find moving around hard.
- **The side story:** Theo never counted his fixed costs, priced below break-even, and his helpers quit. His "rules" are the cause, and his public posts cost him the summer market spot. At the pitch panel he presents a real plan with the student, and the market's new-stall plan template is named after the student. The story never changes the goal.
- **The goal:** after both market days, profit (sales − variable costs − fixed costs) reaches the stall's target. The middle price wins in every venture even with poor Stall Rush rounds; the highest and lowest prices can lose. Unlike the other missions' games, Stall Rush changes customers that day (great +20%, poor −20%, skipped −30%).
- **Gentle and Real (Menu):** Gentle gives a hint after a wrong try and shows the answer after 3 tries; Real gives no hints and shows it after 2. Real gives no hints while students work the numbers.
- **Between lessons (optional):** after a chapter, students can choose "Not yet: help around town." Townspeople then ask for help with their own version of what the chapter taught, for a little play money in a wallet that never touches the mission, and one Town Mail letter arrives each day.
- **Report (Menu → Report for my teacher):** first-try answers by chapter, what the student wrote and chose, game results, how far they got in the side story, and a Between lessons table. It prints. First-try answers make a quick look-for before each step's checkpoint.
- **Simplifications** to tell students if asked: venture prices and costs are samples; a higher price brings fewer customers on a set schedule. The bilingualism figure (about 18%) is the 2021 Census.


## Sample products (show all three in Session 5)

1. **Pitch slideshow** (6 slides): problem → idea → customers → numbers and break-even → risks and roles → career connections.
2. **Prototype + live pitch:** a sample product, flyer or mock-up on the table while the team pitches.
3. **One-page business plan:** headings for each part, a small break-even table, and a roles chart.

## When a team gets stuck

| What you see | What to do |
|---|---|
| Idea is too big ("open a restaurant") | Shrink it: a one-day bake sale or a weekly service at school. |
| Volunteer project with no numbers | Use costs and how they'll be covered instead of break-even. |
| Cover letter is generic | Ask: why *this* role in *your* venture? What evidence do you have? |
| Unsafe venture | Checker reviews safety and school rules; redirect early. |

## Access supports (UDL)

- Break-even card (fixed cost ÷ profit per item); word cards in the brief.
- Product choice; a prototype suits hands-on learners.
- Students who can't run a real venture still complete the full plan and pitch.

## Background for the teacher

- **RAP (Registered Apprenticeship Program):** high school students can start an apprenticeship and earn high school credits; ask your school or check alberta.ca.
- **Bilingualism:** about 18% of Canadians could conduct a conversation in both English and French (2021 Census).
- **Employment services:** Alberta has employment services for newcomers, Francophones and under-represented groups (for example, through alis.alberta.ca and community agencies).
