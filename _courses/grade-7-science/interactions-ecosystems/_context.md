## Learning Outcomes (Alberta 2007 PoS)

Students who have achieved this outcome will be able to:

**LO 7-1-01** Describe the characteristics and components of local ecosystems, including biotic and abiotic factors.
**LO 7-1-02** Describe the roles of producers, consumers, and decomposers in energy flow through ecosystems; construct and interpret food chains and food webs.
**LO 7-1-03** Describe the concept of biodiversity and explain why biodiversity is important to ecosystem health.
**LO 7-1-04** Analyze the effects of natural and human-caused changes on ecosystems; evaluate strategies for maintaining ecosystem health.
**LO 7-1-05** Identify local Alberta ecosystems and describe the organisms that inhabit them.

## Knowledge Clusters (Conceptual Sequence)

Build in this order — each cluster depends on the one before it:

1. **Ecosystems and their components** — biotic (living) and abiotic (non-living) factors; what makes a complete ecosystem; Alberta ecosystem types (boreal forest, grassland, wetland, mountain, parkland, foothills)
2. **Energy flow** — producers (autotrophs; photosynthesis); consumers: primary (herbivores), secondary (carnivores/omnivores), tertiary; decomposers (bacteria, fungi — essential, not optional); food chains (linear) → food webs (realistic, networked)
3. **Energy pyramid** — only ~10% of energy transfers to the next trophic level; why there are fewer top predators; why food webs collapse if a mid-level species disappears
4. **Biodiversity** — definition: variety of species in an ecosystem; why it matters (stability, resilience, ecosystem services); keystone species; habitat; ecological niches (no two species occupy the same niche)
5. **Environmental change** — natural disturbances: fire, drought, flood, disease, succession; human-caused: habitat destruction, pollution, invasive species, overharvesting, climate change; cumulative effects
6. **Sustainability and stewardship** — environmental ethics; FNMI land-based knowledge and relationships; species at risk in Alberta; ecosystem services (clean water, clean air, food, carbon storage)

## Misconceptions Inventory

| Misconception | Correct understanding | Where to address |
|---|---|---|
| Decomposers are not really part of the ecosystem — they're just "cleaners" | Decomposers are essential members of the ecosystem; they return nutrients to the soil that producers need; without them, nutrients would be locked in dead matter permanently | Section 2 (energy flow) — callout box |
| Energy increases as you go up the food chain | Energy decreases (~90% lost as heat at each trophic level); top predators have the least energy available | Section 3 (energy pyramid) — callout box + diagram |
| Nature always recovers from disturbance | Ecosystems can cross tipping points after which they do not return to their original state; this is particularly true of deforestation, coral reef bleaching, and permafrost loss | Section 5 (environmental change) — callout box |
| Humans are separate from/external to ecosystems | Humans are a species within ecosystems; we depend on ecosystem services; our actions create cascading effects | Section 1 opener + Section 6 |
| Bigger organisms have more energy | Energy is determined by trophic level, not organism size; a field of grass contains more energy than all the deer it feeds | Section 3 |
| Food chains are linear in nature | Real ecosystems have complex, networked food webs; removing one species rarely affects only its immediate predator or prey | Section 2 (food webs) |

## Scope Boundary

| In scope | Out of scope |
|---|---|
| Biotic and abiotic factors in Alberta ecosystems | Formal ecological survey methods |
| Producers, consumers (primary/secondary/tertiary), decomposers | Population dynamics equations (Lotka-Volterra) |
| Food chains, food webs, energy pyramids (10% rule conceptually) | Detailed biochemistry of decomposition |
| Biodiversity as species richness; keystone species concept | Genetic diversity, species diversity indices |
| Natural and human-caused environmental change | Detailed climate change mechanisms (covered in Grade 6 Climate) |
| FNMI relationships to land and knowledge of local ecosystems | Formal ethnobotany or land management protocols |

## Local Alberta Content (required)

Every lesson should draw on Alberta ecosystems specifically:

- **Boreal forest** — largest land biome in Alberta; wolves, moose, black bears, beavers; white spruce, aspen
- **Grasslands** — southern Alberta; burrowing owls, pronghorn, prairie dogs, ferruginous hawks; short-grass prairie
- **Wetlands** — beaver ponds, potholes; waterfowl critical habitat; ducks, herons, muskrat
- **Mountain ecosystems** — Rockies; grizzly bears as keystone species; whitebark pine/Clark's nutcracker relationship
- **Foothills/parkland** — transition zone; highest biodiversity in the province
- **Species at risk in Alberta:** grizzly bear, woodland caribou, swift fox, burrowing owl, piping plover, bull trout, northern leopard frog
- **Invasive species in Alberta:** zebra mussels (prevention, not yet established), purple loosestrife, European starling, common carp, rainbow smelt

## FNMI Integration

- Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK) as a valid and scientifically complementary source of information about ecosystem change
- Relationship to land — stewardship as a responsibility, not just a management problem
- Examples: Blackfoot burning practices for grassland health; Cree and Métis knowledge of boreal forest; Stoney Nakoda knowledge of mountain ecosystems
- Fire as a tool for ecosystem management — parallels between Indigenous burning and modern prescribed burning
