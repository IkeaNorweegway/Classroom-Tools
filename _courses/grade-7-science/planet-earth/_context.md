**LO 7-5-02** Describe the rock cycle and classify rocks as igneous, sedimentary, or metamorphic; relate rock type to the conditions that formed it.
**LO 7-5-03** Explain the theory of plate tectonics; describe the three types of plate boundaries and the geological features each produces.

**LO 7-5-06** Explain how fossils form and how they are used to interpret Earth's geological past; describe the significance of Alberta's fossil record.

## Knowledge Clusters (Conceptual Sequence)

1. **Earth's interior** — four layers: crust (outermost, 5–70 km thick; oceanic crust is thin/dense basalt; continental crust is thick/less dense granite), mantle (largest layer; solid rock but flows over millions of years; convection currents drive plate motion), outer core (liquid iron-nickel; convection here generates Earth's magnetic field), inner core (solid iron-nickel; extreme pressure despite high temperature); how we know: seismic waves bend and reflect differently through different materials → we have never drilled to the mantle
2. **Rock cycle** — three rock families: igneous (cooled from magma/lava; intrusive = cooled slowly underground, large crystals; extrusive = cooled quickly at surface, small crystals or glassy), sedimentary (weathered bits of rock compressed and cemented; layers reveal history; most fossils found here; limestone, sandstone, shale — all found in Alberta), metamorphic (existing rock transformed by heat + pressure; no melting; foliated vs. non-foliated); all rock types can become any other through the cycle; rock cycle is driven by plate tectonics and solar energy (weathering)
3. **Plate tectonics** — theory: Earth's crust + upper mantle (lithosphere) is broken into ~15 major plates; plates move at 1–10 cm/year (about the rate fingernails grow); driving force: mantle convection currents; three boundary types: divergent (plates separate; mid-ocean ridges; new crust formed; example: Mid-Atlantic Ridge), convergent (plates collide; ocean floor subducts under continental crust → volcanoes and mountains; two continental plates → mountain chain; example: Himalayas, Rockies), transform (plates slide sideways past each other; earthquakes; no new crust; example: San Andreas Fault); evidence: continental drift (coastlines fit, same fossils on separated continents, paleomagnetic striping)
4. **Earthquakes and volcanoes** — earthquakes: tectonic plates locked by friction; stress builds until rocks break → seismic waves; focus (underground origin point), epicenter (surface point above focus); Richter scale (energy logarithmic — each step = 10× more energy); Mercalli scale (observational — what people feel and see); volcanoes: magma reaches surface through conduits; shield volcanoes (gentle, fluid lava; Hawaii), composite/stratovolcanoes (violent, explosive, thick lava; Ring of Fire); calderas; Alberta: geologically quiet but Rockies are evidence of past tectonic collision
5. **Weathering, erosion, deposition** — weathering: breaking rock into smaller pieces without moving it; mechanical (freeze-thaw, root growth, abrasion) vs. chemical (acid rain, oxidation, dissolution of limestone); erosion: weathered material transported by water, wind, ice, gravity; deposition: material dropped when agent slows; Alberta landforms: Badlands (river erosion, Hoodoos), Rocky Mountains (tectonic uplift + glacial erosion), prairie (glacial till deposition), river valleys (ongoing fluvial erosion)
6. **Fossils and geological time** — fossil formation: hard parts preserved in sedimentary rock through replacement, mould, cast, compression, or amber preservation; index fossils: abundant, widespread, short-lived species used to date rock layers; relative dating: law of superposition (older layers deeper); absolute dating (brief mention — uses radioactive decay, not expected at grade level); geological time scale: Precambrian, Paleozoic, Mesozoic (dinosaurs), Cenozoic (current); Alberta fossil record: Cretaceous marine fossils in badlands (Tyrannosaurus, Triceratops, Edmontosaurus), Burgess Shale (Cambrian marine life in BC), dinosaur footprints in Peace Country

## Misconceptions Inventory

| Misconception | Correct understanding | Where to address |
|---|---|---|
| Tectonic plates are the continents above the water | Plates include both continental and oceanic crust; most plate boundaries are underwater; oceanic plates are more dense and different in composition | Section 3 (plate tectonics) — callout box with diagram |
| Geological change is always rapid and catastrophic (volcanism, earthquakes) | Most geological change is imperceptibly slow; continents moved to their current position over hundreds of millions of years; students see headlines about earthquakes and over-generalise | Section 3 and Section 5 |
| Alberta has no geological history or activity | Alberta has major geological significance: oil sands (ancient marine sediment), the Rockies (tectonic collision), Badlands (fossil-rich Cretaceous sediments), ongoing river erosion | Section 5 and Section 6 — Alberta examples throughout |
| Fossils are mostly large dinosaur bones | Fossils include any preserved evidence of past life: marine invertebrate shells, plant imprints, fish scales, footprints, insects in amber, microfossils | Section 6 — callout box |
| The Earth's interior is hollow or has a world inside | Earth's interior is solid (inner core) and semi-solid/liquid (outer core, mantle); we know from seismic wave data | Section 1 |
| Sedimentary layers are always horizontal | Although deposited horizontally, tectonic forces can tilt, fold, and fault layers dramatically | Section 3/5 connection |

## Alberta Focus (Required Content)

- **Oil sands:** located in northeastern Alberta (Athabasca region); formed from ancient marine organisms (Cretaceous period) buried under sediment; bitumen — very thick oil mixed with sand; geological formation context, not extraction techniques
- **Rockies:** formed by convergent plate tectonics; oceanic plate subducted under North American plate; thrust faulting pushed layers up; subsequent glaciation carved current shapes
- **Badlands (Drumheller):** exposed Cretaceous sedimentary rock; river erosion reveals layers; hoodoos formed by differential erosion; one of the world's richest fossil sites
- **Glaciation:** most of Alberta was covered by glaciers during the last ice age (~20,000 years ago); glacial till (unsorted sediment) deposited across prairies; glacial erratics (boulders transported hundreds of km)
- **Alberta geological timeline:** Precambrian basement rock → Paleozoic marine sediments (central Alberta) → Cretaceous inland sea (oil sands, dinosaurs) → Tertiary Rockies uplift → Quaternary glaciation → present
