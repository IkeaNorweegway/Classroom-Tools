## Core Competencies Emphasis

Per the draft curriculum's subject introduction, Social Studies 7–9 develops Alberta's eight core competencies: critical thinking, problem solving, research and managing information, creativity and innovation, communication, citizenship, collaboration, and personal growth and well-being. Grade 8 leans hardest on **critical thinking** (comparing ideologies and systems) and **citizenship** (civic engagement unit) — worth foregrounding in workbook framing and inquiry projects.

## Learning Outcomes by Unit

### Foundations and Influences of Ideologies
**Organizing Idea:** Time and Place — *"Exploring the dynamic relationships between people, place, and time supports understanding of perspectives and events to make meaning of the world."*
**Guiding Question:** How have ideologies formed over time?
**LO (verbatim):** Students examine the foundations and influences of ideologies.

Key knowledge clusters (from the curriculum document):
1. Historical roots — Plato (leadership through knowledge/wisdom) vs. Aristotle (leadership through law); Ancient Athens aristocrats (tradition, elite rule) vs. democratic reformers (expanding political rights)
2. Individualism vs. collectivism — individualism prioritizes the individual's moral worth, autonomy, and self-reliance; collectivism prioritizes group goals over individual desires
3. The political spectrum — left tends toward collectivism, right tends toward individualism
4. Four ideologies — communism (classless, stateless, common ownership), socialism (means of production owned/regulated by the community), liberalism (progress through reform, active state role for equal opportunity), conservatism (minimal state involvement, gradual change, respect for tradition)
5. Ideology taken to extremes — totalitarian government; Hitler and the Nazi Party's complete political control leading to the Holocaust (systematic persecution and mass murder of Jewish people and other targeted groups)

Skills & procedures (verbatim from curriculum): compare/contrast Plato and Aristotle's philosophies and argue which is better; compare individualism to collectivism; explain why individuals/groups are drawn to one or the other; connect individualism/collectivism to the four ideologies; reflect on Holocaust victim accounts using primary sources.

Major misconceptions to address *(this workspace's addition — confirm)*:
- "Left" and "right" are fixed, universal labels rather than a relative spectrum that shifts by country and era
- Communism and socialism are interchangeable terms
- Totalitarianism is only a historical phenomenon (1930s–40s Germany), not a category that can recur
- An ideology is the same thing as a political party — ideologies are the ideas; parties are organizations built around them

### Economic Systems
**Organizing Idea:** Systems — *"Evaluating processes and structures of organizations builds understanding of decision making in the world."*
**Guiding Question:** How should governments use spending, taxation, and economic systems to meet the needs of society?
**LO (verbatim):** Students compare the role of individuals and governments in economic systems.

Key knowledge clusters (from the curriculum document):
1. Scarcity and the three basic economic questions — what to produce, how to produce it, for whom
2. Four economic systems — traditional, command, market, mixed — each answering the three questions differently
3. The economic spectrum — based on who decides (individuals vs. government) and how much control government holds
4. Market economies (competition, private ownership, limited government) vs. command economies (government-controlled, collective goals, public ownership) vs. mixed economies (blend — e.g., government-run healthcare/education alongside private business)
5. Measuring economies — production, prices, employment (GDP, CPI, unemployment rate)
6. Government revenue and spending — taxes fund spending on healthcare, education, infrastructure, social programs
7. Surplus, deficit, and debt — surplus (revenue > spending), deficit (spending > revenue), debt (accumulated deficits over time); governments may run deficits deliberately to respond to challenges or invest long-term

Skills & procedures (verbatim from curriculum): analyze how different systems make resource decisions; evaluate advantages/challenges of each system; analyze the balance of individual choice vs. government involvement; analyze causes/consequences of government spending and intervention using real-world or historical examples; analyze how and why deficits occur; analyze how sustained deficits/debt affect future decisions; evaluate spending trade-offs (citizen benefit vs. financial risk).

Major misconceptions to address *(this workspace's addition — confirm)*:
- Deficit and debt are the same thing (deficit is one year's gap; debt is the accumulated total)
- A deficit is always bad economic management, never a deliberate choice
- Real-world economies are "pure" market or command systems rather than mixed
- Taxes only fund one visible thing (e.g., "roads") rather than a broad basket of services

### Political Systems
**Organizing Idea:** Systems — *"Evaluating processes and structures of organizations builds understanding of decision making in the world."*
**Guiding Question:** In what ways can society be organized?
**LO (verbatim):** Students compare government control and individual rights within political systems.

Key knowledge clusters (from the curriculum document):
1. Types of political systems — democracy, monarchy, dictatorship, oligarchy; a spectrum from total government control to little/no centralized authority (anarchy)
2. Models of democracy — direct vs. representative
3. Parliamentary (Westminster) vs. presidential democracies — compared on leader title/selection, term length, and policy-making control; parliamentary terms (caucus, backbencher, frontbencher, whip, Leader of the Opposition, shadow cabinet, parliamentary secretaries) vs. presidential terms (congress, veto, elected senate, cabinet, elected judiciary)
4. Canada's political system — parliamentary democracy; three branches (executive, legislative, judicial) with executive and legislative closely connected; Parliament's structure (Governor General, PM and Cabinet, Senate, elected MPs); ridings/constituencies
5. Political parties — form around shared ideology; goal is electing members to turn ideas into law; originated with the Whigs and Tories in 18th-century England; parties form, merge, or dissolve over time; government vs. opposition roles
6. Party systems — one-party (China), two-party (United States), multiparty (Alberta, Canada, Europe)
7. Canada's and Alberta's parties — federal: Liberal, Conservative, NDP, Green, Bloc Québécois; Alberta: United Conservative Party, New Democratic Party
8. Party membership and organization — open public membership in most major parties; members vote on local candidates, party priorities, and leadership; riding/constituency associations each with a President and Board of Directors

Skills & procedures (verbatim from curriculum): describe attributes of political systems/government structures; differentiate control levels (totalitarian → anarchy); describe democratic features; compare Canada's parliamentary system to America's presidential system, including the three branches in each; explain how candidates/politicians organize into parties; explain government vs. opposition roles; explain how citizens participate in and influence parties; use Canadian/Alberta examples; explore party platforms; identify party systems across Canada, Alberta, and the world; consider how political systems affect citizens' lives globally.

Major misconceptions to address *(this workspace's addition — confirm)*:
- Canadians directly elect the Prime Minister the way Americans elect a president (they elect a local MP; the PM is the leader of the party that wins the most seats)
- The Governor General holds real independent political power
- "Democracy" means one single system everywhere, rather than a spectrum of models (direct/representative, parliamentary/presidential)
- More political parties automatically means a healthier democracy (ignores trade-offs of multiparty vs. two-party systems)

### Civic Engagement
**Organizing Idea:** Citizenship — *"Understanding local, national, and global issues empowers individual and collective action toward an inclusive society."*
**Guiding Question:** In what ways can citizens influence issues and events?
**LO (verbatim):** Students examine civic engagement to understand and influence issues and events.

Key knowledge clusters (from the curriculum document):
1. Voting as a responsibility of democratic citizenship; voter eligibility — Canadian citizen, at least 18, valid proof of residence in the riding/constituency, valid proof of identity
2. Global issues affecting life in Canada — economic disruption, poverty, drought and famine, human migration, civil unrest, war
3. Engaged citizenship — awareness, decision-making, and taking action on local, national, and global issues
4. Forms of civic engagement — volunteering and joining service groups, participating in community initiatives, attending rallies/protests, advocacy, ethical purchasing decisions, political party membership and joining riding/constituency associations, election volunteering (door knocking, delivering flyers, phone calls, mailers, data entry)

Skills & procedures (verbatim from curriculum): investigate contemporary local/national/global issues; identify local civic engagement opportunities; discuss how global issues affect Canadians; identify democratic participation opportunities beyond party involvement; determine ways to get involved in parties; explain how parties are organized locally.

Major misconceptions to address *(this workspace's addition — confirm)*:
- Voting is the only meaningful form of civic engagement
- Civic engagement requires being 18+ (most listed forms — volunteering, advocacy, ethical purchasing, campaign volunteering — don't)
- Global issues (migration, drought, conflict) are disconnected from daily life in Canada

## Inquiry Project Options (per unit) *(this workspace's addition — proposed, confirm or replace)*

### Foundations and Influences of Ideologies
1. Ideology profile — research one ideology in depth (communism, socialism, liberalism, or conservatism); present its core beliefs, a real historical or current example, and a critique
2. Plato vs. Aristotle debate — structured class debate on "best person" vs. "best laws" governance, using original arguments
3. Holocaust primary source study — examine survivor testimony; connect it back to how the ideology of the Nazi regime enabled the persecution

### Economic Systems
1. Design your own economy — in groups, choose a point on the market/command spectrum and design a small economy; justify trade-offs
2. Alberta budget investigation — research a real recent Alberta or federal budget; identify surplus/deficit, major spending categories, and a policy trade-off
3. Economic systems comparison chart — compare two real countries with different economic systems on the same indicators (GDP, unemployment, government role)

### Political Systems
1. Mock Parliament — simulate a Westminster-style debate and vote on a student-proposed bill, using real party-system roles (government, opposition, whip)
2. Compare-a-country — research a country's political system and government structure; present how it compares to Canada's
3. Design a political party — create an original party: name, ideology, platform, and explain what party system it would function in

### Civic Engagement
1. Real civic action project — plan and (where feasible) carry out an actual act of civic engagement (letter to a representative, volunteering, awareness campaign) and reflect on its impact
2. Global issue case study — pick one global issue from the list (migration, drought, conflict, etc.) and trace its real impact on a Canadian community
3. Campaign simulation — run a mock election campaign for a local issue or mock candidate, using real campaign tactics (canvassing script, flyer, phone bank pitch)
