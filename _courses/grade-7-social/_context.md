# Grade 7 Social Studies — Alberta (Draft 2026 Curriculum)
*Source: Alberta Education, "Draft Social Studies (7–9) Curriculum," June 2026 (Optional Classroom Piloting 2026–27), Grade 7 column. Read alongside `_meta-context.md`.*

**⚠ DRAFT STATUS:** This course is built on Alberta's *draft* curriculum, released for optional field piloting in 2026–27, not the final program of studies. Knowledge/Understanding/Skills text below is quoted or closely paraphrased from the official document. Misconceptions, importance scores, weeks allocation, unit order and inquiry project ideas are this workspace's own additions and need your review.

**French:** Skipped for now, matching Social 8 and 9.

**Reading level:** Grade 7 is a historical narrative year (Canada from Confederation to the First World War). Target a Grade 5–6 reading level in notes, workbooks and prompts: short sentences, a concrete story or image before an abstract idea, vocabulary glossed on first use. Dates are anchors, not the point: every date in a material should carry a "so what".

**Sensitive content (teacher review before use):** the *Indian Act*, the numbered treaties, scrip, the Red River Conflict and Northwest Resistance, the trial of Louis Riel, the Chinese Head Tax, the Continuous Journey Regulation, the 1907 Anti-Asian Riots, Order-in-Council P.C. 1911-1324 and the Komagata Maru. Materials state what happened plainly, name whose perspective a source shows, and give First Nations, Métis, Francophone and immigrant perspectives alongside the government's. No role-play of discrimination or of treaty negotiation from one side only.

## Core Competencies Emphasis

The draft names eight core competencies. Grade 7 leans on **critical thinking** (interpreting evidence, determining significance, recognizing perspectives, fact vs. opinion) and **research and managing information** (maps, timelines, primary sources). Every unit includes at least one source-reading task and one timeline or map task.

## Learning Outcomes by Unit

### 1. Building the Canadian State
**Organizing Idea:** Time and Place — *"Exploring the dynamic relationships between people, place, and time supports understanding of perspectives and events to make meaning of the world."*
**Guiding Question:** How was the Canadian state built after Confederation?
**LO (verbatim):** Students examine the deliberate acts of state building.
**Understanding:** Canada was built through deliberate acts.

Knowledge clusters (from the curriculum):
1. **Before Confederation** — British–French competition and conflict changed boundaries and gave Britain control of New France and Acadia, contributing to British North America. As negotiations began, BNA included Rupert's Land, the North-Western Territory, PEI, Nova Scotia, New Brunswick (the Maritimes), Newfoundland, the Province of Canada (Upper and Lower Canada, which became Canada West and Canada East) and British Columbia.
2. **Why Confederation** — concern about U.S. expansion northward, shaped by events such as the American Civil War, built support for Confederation to strengthen political unity. The Fathers of Confederation (Province of Canada, New Brunswick, Nova Scotia) met at Charlottetown and Quebec (1864) and London (1866–67) to create a unified state with more control over territory, governance and defence. On July 1, 1867, the *British North America Act* created the Dominion of Canada: Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Quebec and Ontario.
3. **Expansion west** — growth west was part of a strategy to strengthen the state: territory, resources, national unity. Acquiring Rupert's Land and the North-Western Territory from the Hudson's Bay Company extended Canada's authority over a vast region.
4. **Red River and the Northwest Resistance** — Louis Riel formed a provisional government in 1869 during the Red River Conflict. The government used the scrip system for Métis land claims (1870). The Northwest Resistance led to Riel's trial and conviction.
5. **First Nations, treaties and the *Indian Act*** — after Confederation, federal decisions placed First Nations people and lands under federal authority. Canada and First Nations signed 11 numbered treaties (1871–1921) setting terms for land use and governance; treaties were agreements between First Nations and the Crown (later represented by the Canadian government) that enabled expansion by transferring land and setting up new systems of governance. The 1876 *Indian Act* is a federal law affecting First Nations governance, land management and cultural practices; its legacy remains a subject of legal and political debate.
6. **Settlement and new provinces** — Macdonald's National Policy promoted western settlement and a labour source and market for the Prairies. The CPR improved transportation and enabled settlement by diverse groups (immigrants from Britain and Ukraine; from the U.S., including Black settlers; Francophones from Quebec). Immigration strengthened Canada's claim to new territory. Boundaries changed as provinces and territories joined: Manitoba 1870, BC 1871, PEI 1873, Yukon 1898, Alberta and Saskatchewan 1905, Newfoundland and Labrador 1949, Nunavut 1999 (the NWT divided several times).

Skills & procedures (verbatim, with local codes):
- **SB1** Construct maps and timelines that highlight Canada's shifting political boundaries.
- **SB2** Justify concerns about United States territorial expansion to reasons for supporting Confederation.
- **SB3** Explain the goals of the Fathers of Confederation and how they contributed to the creation of a unified Canadian state.
- **SB4** Evaluate how and why the Canadian government expanded west and established authority over new territories. / Describe benefits gained by the Canadian government from acquiring Rupert's Land and the North-Western Territory.
- **SB5** Investigate the Red River Conflict as a response to Canadian expansion and assess how the government responded to maintain control.
- **SB6** Explain how expansion of the Dominion of Canada west changed control over land and affected First Nations and Métis communities.
- **SB7** Research how the Canadian government encouraged immigration to settle the West and how different groups experienced this expansion. / Identify key decisions and actions taken by the Canadian government that contributed to building the Canadian state after Confederation.

Misconceptions *(workspace addition — confirm)*:
- Canada became fully independent in 1867 (it became a self-governing Dominion; foreign affairs stayed with Britain until 1931 — Grade 9 picks this up).
- The West was empty land before settlement (First Nations and Métis lived, governed and traded there).
- Treaties were sales of land, understood the same way by both sides (written and oral understandings differed; many First Nations understood them as agreements to share land).
- Rupert's Land was bought from the people who lived there (it was transferred from the Hudson's Bay Company).
- All ten provinces joined in 1867.

### 2. Creating Canada's Government
**Organizing Idea:** Systems — *"Evaluating processes and structures of organizations builds understanding of decision making in the world."*
**Guiding Question:** How was Canada's system of government created at Confederation?
**LO (verbatim):** Students examine how the Quebec Conference, London Conference, and the *British North America Act* (1867) led to the creation of Canada.
**Understanding:** Canadian sovereignty was developed gradually.

Knowledge clusters:
1. **The conferences** — at Quebec (1864) colonial representatives negotiated how the new country would be governed and power shared: how laws would be made, how regions represented and how powers divided. These agreements formed the basis of the *BNA Act* (1867), which turned the plans into law. London (1866–67) finalized them.
2. **The *BNA Act*** — created the Dominion (NS, NB, Quebec, Ontario) on July 1, 1867 and defined how political power would be organized and exercised.
3. **Three branches** — executive power formally vested in the Crown, exercised by the Governor General on the advice of the Privy Council; legislative branch is a bicameral Parliament (House of Commons, the lower house and primary legislative body, MPs elected in ridings; Senate, the upper house for "sober second thought", senators appointed by the Governor General on the PM's recommendation); judicial branch authorized through a national court system, with the Act allowing a later General Court of Appeal for Canada.
4. **PM and Cabinet** — the PM is head of government and leader of the party holding the most seats; Cabinet ministers are appointed by the Governor General on the PM's advice, usually from sitting MPs, each responsible for a department.
5. **Democratic features** — representative government, responsible government, representation by population.
6. **Federalism** — powers and responsibilities divided between the federal government and provincial governments.

Skills & procedures (verbatim, with local codes):
- **CG1** Sequence key events leading to Confederation and explain how each contributed to the creation of Canada's system of government.
- **CG2** Analyze how the division of powers between federal and provincial governments affects decision making in Canada.
- **CG3** Explain one benefit and one challenge of dividing power between federal and provincial governments in Canada.
- **CG4** Describe and compare the roles of the executive, legislative, and judicial branches in Canada's government.
- **CG5** Evaluate how establishing a national court system and allowing for a "general court of appeal" supports consistency in law across a country.

Misconceptions *(confirm)*:
- Canadians vote directly for the Prime Minister (they elect an MP in their riding).
- The Senate is elected, like the House of Commons.
- The Governor General makes the government's decisions (acts on the advice of ministers).
- Federal and provincial governments do the same jobs, or the federal government is "the boss" of the provinces in everything.
- Confederation happened in one meeting (it took three conferences over three years).

Depth ceiling: the full seven-step bill process, orders in council, the Charter and the Supreme Court in detail are Grade 9 (System of Government). Grade 7 names the three branches and the 1867 structure.

### 3. Building Canada's Economy
**Organizing Idea:** Systems
**Guiding Question:** How did the Canadian government help shape the economy after Confederation?
**LO (verbatim):** Students examine the Canadian government's role in developing the economy post-Confederation.
**Understanding:** Government decisions can influence how an economy grows and develops. Different regions develop different economies based on their resources and industries.

Knowledge clusters:
1. **Expansion and growth** — the economy changed as Canada expanded west, developed new industries and increased trade; expansion grew agriculture, settlement and resource development, shaping regional economies.
2. **The National Policy** — Macdonald's tariffs on imported goods protected Canadian industries and encouraged buying Canadian-made goods; it also aimed to support growth and connect regions.
3. **The CPR** — supported growth by connecting regions, transporting goods and enabling trade.
4. **Tariffs vs. free trade** — free trade agreements let producers sell without tariffs, giving consumers more choice.
5. **GDP and regions** — GDP measures the total value of goods and services produced in a country. Regional inequalities are minimized through taxation and equalization payments. Regional economies based on extracting and manufacturing natural resources (wheat, oil) show their importance.
6. **Banking** — GDP influences banking through demand for financial services; banks help people and businesses manage money, save and invest. The Bank of Canada (created in the 1930s) regulates banking and the monetary system; Canada's first banks include the Bank of Montreal, Bank of Nova Scotia, Laurentian Bank, National Bank, Royal Bank and CIBC.

Skills & procedures (verbatim, with local codes):
- **BE1** Analyze how expansion west influenced economic development in Canada. / Explain the crucial role of natural resources in Canada's economic growth.
- **BE2** Investigate the short-term and long-term consequences of Sir John A. Macdonald's National Policy on Canada's economy. / Research the contributions of Sir John A. Macdonald.
- **BE3** Compare the opportunities and challenges of tariffs and free trade on the Canadian economy.
- **BE4** Explain gross domestic product. / Explain why governments provide financial support to some regions and how this can affect economic development.
- **BE5** Analyze the reciprocal relationship between gross domestic product and banking. / Explain the role of the Bank of Canada in Canada's economy.

Misconceptions *(confirm)*:
- A tariff is paid by the foreign country (it is collected on imports and usually raises the price the buyer pays).
- GDP measures how much money a country has, or how rich each person is.
- Equalization means Ottawa gives every province the same amount.
- The Bank of Canada is a bank where people open accounts.
- The National Policy helped every region equally (Prairie farmers paid more for protected eastern goods).

Note on Macdonald: the draft asks students to "research the contributions" of Macdonald. Materials present his economic contributions and note, in one line, that his record also includes policies harmful to First Nations covered in State Building, so the picture is balanced without making this unit about it.

### 4. Immigration After Confederation
**Organizing Idea:** Citizenship — *"Understanding local, national, and global issues empowers individual and collective action toward an inclusive society."*
**Guiding Question:** Did post-Confederation immigration shape the development of the Canadian state?
**LO (verbatim):** Students examine immigration in post-Confederation Canada.
**Understanding:** Immigration in post-Confederation Canada created opportunities for settlement and economic growth, while also leading to conflict and discrimination.

Knowledge clusters:
1. **Discrimination** — treating people differently because of their ethnicity, religion, age, race or disability.
2. **Settlement** — the government encouraged some immigrants to develop land and build communities in the West. The *Dominion Lands Act* (1872) offered free land to settlers willing to farm it. Immigrants built towns, developed agriculture such as wheat farming and grew the economy. Ukrainian settlers brought farming knowledge that helped turn prairie land into productive farmland.
3. **Restriction** — past policies restricted immigration from specific groups: the Chinese Head Tax and Exclusion Act; the Continuous Journey Regulation (1908). Order-in-Council P.C. 1911-1324 proposed a one-year prohibition of Black immigrants; Black farmers from Oklahoma were not welcomed on the Prairies.
4. **Conflict** — the 1907 Anti-Asian Riots in Vancouver destroyed property and demanded an end to Asian immigration, driven by rising Asian immigration and growing dissatisfaction along the Pacific coast. In the Komagata Maru incident (1914) passengers from India were not allowed to disembark in Vancouver and the ship was escorted out by the military.
5. **Minority rights** — the Manitoba Schools Question (1890) and Ontario's Regulation 17 (1912) created constitutional crises about Catholic and Francophone minority rights.

Skills & procedures (verbatim, with local codes):
- **IM1** Explain how immigration created both opportunities and conflict in Canada, using historical examples.
- **IM2** Describe how access to land influenced decisions by immigrants to settle in the Prairies.
- **IM3** Describe the causes and consequences of specific events, such as the Komagata Maru incident and the Anti-Asian Riots.
- **IM4** Explain why the Canadian government created policies and laws that restricted immigration from certain groups. / Use examples from historical events to support conclusions about discrimination and government policy.
- **IM5** Assess whether government policies and actions during this period were fair to different groups, using historical examples.
- **IM6** Distinguish between facts and opinions when examining historical accounts of events such as riots or immigration policies.

Misconceptions *(confirm)*:
- Canada welcomed all immigrants equally in this period.
- Discrimination was only individual prejudice, not government law and policy.
- "Free land" was free of cost and free of people (settlers paid a fee and had to farm it; the land was First Nations and Métis land covered by the treaties).
- A source written at the time is automatically a fact (it records someone's view).

### 5. Armed Forces and Sovereignty
**Organizing Idea:** Time and Place
**Guiding Question:** How can defence of peace and security play a role in the development of a nation?
**LO (verbatim):** Students relate the development of armed forces in Canada to sovereignty.
**Understanding:** Development of armed forces advanced Canada's national sovereignty.

Knowledge clusters:
1. **Policing the West** — armed forces and national policing were developed to support peace and security and establish authority across the state. The NWMP (1873) enforced laws and defended territory as Canada expanded west; national police evolved into the RCMP, which oversees national security today.
2. **Building a military** — supported by policy (Militia Acts 1855, 1904), increased independence (British Army withdrawal 1871, Royal Canadian Navy 1910) and international conflict (Boer War 1899–1902, specifically Paardeberg).
3. **The First World War** — Britain controlled Canada's foreign affairs and sent Canadian forces (the CEF and Canadian Corps) to fight as distinct units for the first time. Accomplishments at Vimy Ridge, Beaumont-Hamel, Ypres (Second Ypres and Passchendaele) and Gallipoli.
4. **Diverse contributions** — Black Canadians in the No. 2 Construction Battalion, Indigenous soldiers, Sikh Canadian soldiers, Francophone soldiers in the Royal 22e Régiment.
5. **Remembrance** — Remembrance Day, Indigenous Veterans Day (Nov 8), the Canadian National War Memorial and the Newfoundland National War Memorial.
6. **Sovereignty** — through the *Statute of Westminster* (1931) Canada gained control of its foreign affairs, including decisions on alliances and conflicts.

Skills & procedures (verbatim, with local codes):
- **AF1** Examine the evolution of national police forces in Canada after Confederation.
- **AF2** Examine the evolution of Canada's military forces after Confederation. / Determine how Canada's military changed after Confederation. / Identify steps taken for Canada to begin developing a modern Canadian Armed Forces.
- **AF3** Discuss the significance of Canada's role in the Boer War.
- **AF4** Examine the participation of Canadian military forces in the First World War. / Explore and analyze key battles and military accomplishments that had significance for Canada. / Research stories about heroic and gallant acts carried out by Canadian military forces.
- **AF5** Discuss ways of honouring sacrifices of Canadian military forces. / Identify war memorials in the local community.
- **AF6** Explain the role of the *Statute of Westminster* in Canada's sovereignty.

Misconceptions *(confirm)*:
- Canada chose to enter the First World War (Britain's declaration in 1914 automatically included Canada; Canada chose *how much* to contribute).
- Canadian soldiers only fought at Vimy.
- Beaumont-Hamel was a Canadian battle (it was the Newfoundland Regiment; Newfoundland was a separate Dominion until 1949).
- The RCMP has always existed in its current form.
- Sovereignty arrived in one moment (it grew step by step: 1871 withdrawal, 1910 navy, Vimy, 1931).

Depth ceiling: the Balfour Declaration and Westminster in depth are Grade 9 (Independence). Grade 7 names Westminster as the step that gave Canada its own foreign policy.

## Inquiry Project Options *(workspace addition — confirm)*

- **State Building:** (1) Map-maker: an annotated series of five maps, 1867–1999, with a "why it changed" caption on each. (2) Many voices of Red River: a museum panel set showing Métis, government, Anglophone settler and First Nations perspectives. (3) Treaty case study: one numbered treaty (Treaty 6 or 7 for Alberta), its written terms and the oral understanding, with a local Nation's view if a guest can be invited.
- **Creating Canada's Government:** (1) Confederation conference simulation: colonies negotiate the division of powers. (2) Build-a-Parliament model with role cards. (3) "Who handles it?" campaign: sort 20 real issues into federal or provincial and present a poster.
- **Building the Economy:** (1) National Policy trial: is it good for the Prairies? (2) Regional economy profile of Alberta vs. another province. (3) A bank history poster: one of Canada's first banks and what it did.
- **Immigration:** (1) Settler stories archive: profile one group (Ukrainian, Black Oklahoman, Francophone, Chinese, Sikh). (2) Fair or unfair? A structured assessment of one policy using fact/opinion evidence cards. (3) Local history: who settled your town and why.
- **Armed Forces:** (1) Local memorial study (AF5). (2) "Steps to sovereignty" timeline mural. (3) A story of courage: one soldier's or unit's story from the First World War, including the No. 2 Construction Battalion or an Indigenous soldier.
