# Math 9 — Better Questions, Better Answers
*Plan, 2026-10-21. Builds on `math9-achievement-indicators-map.md` (what skills to ask for) and `_pedagogy-reference.md` (scaffolding, fading, metacognition).*

---

## The idea in one paragraph

The achievement indicators are written as **verbs**: verify, find the error, model, compare, describe a context, explain. Our materials mostly use one verb: *solve*. So students learn to produce answers, not reasoning. The plan:
1. Turn each indicator verb into a small set of **question stems**.
2. Give each stem a **scaffold that fades** over the unit.
3. Show students **what a good answer looks like** for that kind of question, so they can aim at it.

The teacher gets a **hint ladder** for the common wrong answers.

---

## Part 1 — Eight question types (from the indicators)

Each type below has: stems, a Math 9 example, the scaffold ladder (most help → none), and what a strong answer contains.

### 1. Find and fix the error
*Indicators: N2.5, N4.3, N5.3, PR3.4, PR6.7, PR7.6*

**Stems**
- "Sam's work is below. Circle the first wrong step. Explain what Sam did. Fix it."
- "Two students got different answers. Who is right? Show how you know."

**Example:** 3(x − 4) = 15 → 3x − 4 = 15 → 3x = 19 → x = 19/3

**Ladder**
1. The wrong step is marked: "Step 1 is wrong. What should it be?"
2. The step is not marked: "One step is wrong. Find it."
3. The student doesn't know whether there's an error: "Is this correct? If not, fix it."
4. The student writes their own: "Make a mistake a classmate might make on this problem, then explain how to spot it."

**Strong answer:** names the step, names the rule broken ("only multiplied the 3 by x, not by −4"), gives the corrected step, and checks the new answer.

### 2. Check it (verify by substitution)
*Indicators: PR1.5, PR3.2, PR4.9*

**Stems**
- "Is x = −2 a solution? Prove it without solving."
- "Your answer is x > 4. Test one value that should work and one that shouldn't."

**Ladder**
1. A blank substitution table (value | left side | right side | equal?)
2. The table headers only
3. "Check your answer." with no table
4. A habit: every answer box has a small "✓ check" corner

**Strong answer:** shows the substitution, compares both sides, and states the conclusion in words ("Both sides are 7, so x = −2 works").

### 3. Show it another way (model or represent)
*Indicators: N1.1, PR3.1, PR5.1, PR6.1–6.2, PR7.1–7.2*

**Stems**
- "Draw the algebra-tile model, then write each step in symbols beside it."
- "Show 2³ and 3² as pictures. Why are they different?"

**Ladder**
1. The model is drawn; the student writes the symbols
2. The symbols are given; the student draws the model
3. The student chooses any model
4. "Which model made this easiest to see? Why?"

**Strong answer:** the model and the symbols match step for step, with one sentence linking them ("Removing 3 unit tiles from each side is the same as −3").

### 4. Reverse it
*Indicators: PR1.3, PR5.4, N5.4, N6.4*

**Stems**
- "Write a story that this equation could solve: 4x + 15 = 63."
- "Name a number whose square root is between 6 and 7."
- "Describe a situation for the expression 12n + 5."

**Ladder**
1. A story frame with blanks: "___ costs $15, plus $4 for each ___ …"
2. A worked example of a different equation, then "your turn"
3. The stem only
4. "Swap with a partner. Can they write the equation from your story?"

**Strong answer:** every number and the variable has a meaning, and the question asked is the one the equation answers.

### 5. Compare
*Indicators: PR4.6, PR4.8, N1.2*

**Stems**
- "Solve 2x + 1 = 7 and 2x + 1 > 7. How are the steps the same? How are the answers different?"
- "Compare (−3)² and −3². Which rule explains the difference?"

**Ladder**
1. A two-column organizer: "Same | Different"
2. A sentence frame: "Both ___, but ___ because ___."
3. The open stem
4. "Write a rule that would help next year's class remember the difference."

**Strong answer:** names at least one similarity and one difference, and says *why* the difference exists (not just that it does).

### 6. Generalize the rule
*Indicators: PR4.3, PR4.4, N1.5, N2.1*

**Stems**
- "Start with 2 < 5. Multiply both sides by −1. Is it still true? Try three more. What rule do you notice?"
- "Fill in the pattern 3³, 3², 3¹, 3⁰. What must 3⁰ be? Why?"

**Ladder**
1. A guided table that the student fills in, with "What do you notice?"
2. The table without the last row: "Predict, then check."
3. "Find a rule and test it on a case you choose."
4. "Find a case where the rule *doesn't* work, or explain why there isn't one."

**Strong answer:** states the rule in words, shows it working on at least two cases, and names its limits ("only when dividing by a negative").

### 7. Explain with an example
*Indicators: N2.1, SS1.1, SP1.2, SP4.3*

**Stems**
- "Explain why the inscribed angle is half the central angle. Use a diagram with numbers."
- "How could the same 30% chance of rain be used to argue *for* and *against* holding the track meet?"

**Ladder**
1. A frame: "Claim: ___. Example: ___. This shows ___ because ___."
2. "Claim · Example · Because" headings only
3. The open stem
4. "Give a non-example too."

**Strong answer:** claim + specific example with numbers + link back to the claim.

### 8. With and without technology
*Indicators: N4.1–4.2, N6.2–6.3*

**Stems**
- "Estimate √40 first, then use a calculator. How close were you? Why does the calculator show so many digits?"
- "Type −3² into your calculator. Is that what you meant? How do you fix it?"

**Ladder:** estimate box → calculator box → a "Was my estimate reasonable?" check → students choose when a calculator helps.

**Strong answer:** the estimate comes before the calculator, both are compared, and any surprise is explained.

## Part 2 — Scaffolds for better answers

The question types say *what* to ask. These scaffolds help students *answer well*. Each is a cognitive scaffold unless noted, so each must fade (see `_pedagogy-reference.md`, Fading).

| Scaffold | What it looks like | Fades to |
|---|---|---|
| **Answer, Method, Check (AMC)** | A three-part answer box: *Answer · How I know · How I checked*. One shared format across all units, so it becomes a habit. | The headings shrink to "A · M · C", then disappear. Tests keep the expectation, not the box. |
| **Success criteria per question type** | One line under the question on first use: "A strong answer names the step, the rule, and the fix." Taken from Part 1. | Shown on first use, then only in the key, then students write the criteria themselves. |
| **Which answer is better?** | Two student answers, both correct, one with reasoning and one without. "Which would get full marks? What's missing from the other?" | Students improve a weak answer, then mark their own. |
| **Upgrade it** | A weak answer is given ("x = 3 because I solved it"). "Make this a 3-mark answer." | Used early in a unit, then dropped. |
| **Reasoning word bank** | *because, so, therefore, which means, this works when, check: …*, with distractors (per the ELL guidance). | An access scaffold for ELL students: it can stay for them, and fades for others. |
| **Sentence frames** | Only for the language structure (compare, claim-example), never to give away the maths. | Frame → partial frame → none, over 3–4 lessons. |
| **Self-check prompts** | "Does your answer make sense in the story? Is it about the size you expected?" | Moves from every question to one per page, then the end of a section. |
| **Error diagnosis on returned work** | "Was this a don't-know-it error or a slip? How can you tell?" | Stays: this is a metacognitive habit, not a crutch. |

**A simple answer-quality rubric** (used on exit tickets and test explain items, and shown to students):

| Score | What it means |
|---|---|
| 0 | No answer, or only an answer with no work |
| 1 | Correct answer **or** correct method, not both |
| 2 | Correct answer and method |
| 3 | Answer, method, and a reason or check that would convince someone else |

## Part 3 — Where it goes in each material

| Material | Change |
|---|---|
| **Notes packages** | Each section ends with a "Show me" check using one of the eight question types, not just "solve". Worked examples keep the self-explanation prompt; add the success criteria the first time a question type appears. |
| **Workbooks / worksheets** | ★ items: question types 1–3 with ladder levels 1–2. ★★ items: types 4–7 with ladder levels 3–4. Each strand workbook uses all eight types at least once. AMC answer boxes on the first pages, faded by the end. |
| **Quizzes / tests** | Each local code is tested with at least **two question types** (e.g. solve + find the error). One explain item per test is marked with the 0–3 rubric, printed on the test. No ladder scaffolds on tests, except access scaffolds. |
| **Exit tickets** | One question type per ticket, scored 0–3. This gives quick data on whether answers are improving. |
| **Home practice sheets** | The worked example first (as now), then a "check it" item (type 2) at the end. Parents can mark it without knowing the method. |
| **Teacher notes** | A **hint ladder** for each common error, least help first. Example for "flipped the sign in an equation": (1) "Is this an equation or an inequality?" (2) "Test your answer: does it make both sides equal?" (3) "When do we flip the sign?" Written from the misconception tables in `_context.md`. |
| **Thinking prompts** | Turn Part 1 into a reusable stems bank: `prompts/math-reasoning-prompt-v1.md` (follows `prompts/_context.md`: specific anchor, reasoning demand, low floor). |

## Part 4 — Rollout

**Step 1 — Stems bank** (one session)
- Write `prompts/math-reasoning-prompt-v1.md` from Part 1: all eight types, two stems each, plus the AMC box and 0–3 rubric as printable pieces.

**Step 2 — Pilot unit: Linear Equations** (one or two sessions)
It has the clearest gaps (no error items on its test, and no equation-vs-inequality compare, a/x = b or multi-value check).
- Notes v2: a "Show me" check per section; a compare item in S4 (2x + 1 = 7 vs 2x + 1 > 7); a generalize task for the inequality sign rule.
- Core and challenge worksheets v2: all eight types, laddered; AMC boxes fading.
- Quiz, test, exits v2: two question types per LE code; one 0–3 explain item.
- Teacher notes: hint ladders for the five Patterns & Relations misconceptions.

**Step 3 — Teach it, then look** (2–3 weeks)
- Compare exit-ticket scores on the 0–3 rubric from early to late in the unit. The aim is more 3s, not just more correct answers.
- Note which ladder steps students got stuck on. Adjust the fading pace.

**Step 4 — Make it standard** (after the pilot)
- Add to the gates in `CLAUDE.md` and the contexts: workbook "Question types used: [ ]"; test "Each LO tested two ways; explain item with 0–3 rubric".
- Add the AMC box and the rubric to `templates/workbook-template.md`, `quiz-template.md`, `test-template.md`.
- Roll out by gap size (from the indicators map): Polynomials, Square Roots, Powers, then Statistics and Probability (which also need new content: sampling and probability in society).

## Decisions for the teacher

1. **Answer format name:** AMC (Answer · Method · Check) is a placeholder. Use any name the class already knows.
2. **Rubric on tests:** the 0–3 explain rubric on one item per test, or on all short-answer items?
3. **Pilot unit:** Linear Equations is proposed. Pick another if it's already been taught this year.
