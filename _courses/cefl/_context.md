## Course Overview

**Curriculum:** Alberta Career Education and Financial Literacy 7–9, Draft, June 2026
**Grades:** 7, 8, 9 | **Age:** ~12–15
**Schedule:** 1-hour class every 3 days for 24 weeks ≈ **40 sessions a year**. 5 units × 7 sessions = 35, leaving 5 flex sessions (course intro, catch-up, assemblies, a guest who can only come on one day).
**Course shape:** mostly **project-based**. Short teaching moments give students the context and big ideas; the project is where the learning happens. See *CEFL Materials Suite and Unit Rhythm* below.
**Audience:** Students and teachers. Materials are print-ready and structured enough for any teacher to pick up and deliver.
**Reporting:** Outcome-based. Each assessment must map to a specific learning outcome. No blanket grades.

**Where it comes from:** In K–6, career education and financial literacy are part of Physical Education and Wellness. From Grade 7 it is a separate subject.

**Five strands** (subject introduction): Career Exploration, Career Opportunities, Financial Literacy, Decision Making, Planning for the Future. Only the first three are organizing ideas with outcomes. Decision Making and Planning for the Future are threads that run through all units, not units of their own.

## Units

The curriculum has 3 organizing ideas, each with 2 rows of outcomes per grade. This workspace treats each row as a unit, so every grade has the same 5 units. Grade 9 Career Opportunities has an extra block (page 10 of the PDF: attaining employment), folded into the Experiences unit as a second cluster.

| Unit (slug) | Organizing Idea | Grade 7 GQ | Grade 8 GQ | Grade 9 GQ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Careers and the Labour Market** (`labour-market`) | Career Exploration | What factors influence career development? | How does a changing labour market shape the skills, goals, and adaptability individuals need to achieve career success? | How do individuals succeed in a changing labour market? |
| **Post-Secondary Pathways** (`post-secondary`) | Career Exploration | How can decisions be made about post-secondary programming? | How do post-secondary pathways, labour market realities, and financial considerations shape education and career planning? | How do individuals make informed decisions about post-secondary pathways? |
| **Experiences and Employment** (`experiences`) | Career Opportunities | How can experiences guide future choices? | How can one's impression of career possibilities be influenced? | How might experiences inform career development? |
| **Income and Tax** (`income-tax`) | Financial Literacy | How is employment income earned, taxed, and used? | How do taxation and total compensation influence the true value of employment? | How do income, taxation, and deductions shape an individual's financial well-being from employment? |
| **Money, Interest, and Credit** (`money-management`) | Financial Literacy | How does managing money affect financial security? | How do the costs of daily life, the mechanics of interest, and the choice between saving and investing shape personal financial security? | How does the use of credit and debt shape an individual's financial future? |

**Organizing ideas (verbatim):**
- **Career Exploration:** Purposeful exploration of past and present experiences can support a healthy work-life balance, contribute to self-knowledge, and assist in navigating transitions while pursuing career and educational goals.
- **Career Opportunities:** Volunteer or work-integrated learning experiences can influence career development and promote world-of-work and community interactions.
- **Financial Literacy:** Informed financial decision making contributes to the well-being of individuals, groups, and communities.

**Units are silos.** Any unit can be taught in any order, per the unit-silo rule in `_meta-context.md`.

### Sessions per unit

Every unit gets **7 sessions** (about 4–5 weeks at one session every 3 days). Units are not weighted differently: the project rhythm needs all 7 sessions, and content-heavy units (Grade 7 Post-Secondary, Income and Tax) carry their extra content in the moments, not in extra weeks.

---|---|---|---|---|
| Careers and the Labour Market | 15% | 20% | 20% | Gr 8–9 add the career plan, resumé, brand statement, interviews |
| Post-Secondary Pathways | 25% | 20% | 20% | Gr 7 carries the heaviest Knowledge column in the whole document |
| Experiences and Employment | 15% | 15% | 20% | Gr 9 has two clusters |
| Income and Tax | 25% | 20% | 20% | Gr 7 Knowledge column runs four pages; all grades are calculation-heavy |
| Money, Interest, and Credit | 20% | 25% | 20% | Gr 8 introduces both simple and compound interest |

## CEFL Materials Suite and Unit Rhythm

This replaces the generic materials suite in `_meta-context.md` for this course (allowed by "Expanding This Framework"). Evidence: `research/project-based-learning-research.md` (project design) and `research/financial-career-education-research.md` (subject evidence).

### Why this shape

| Research finding | What CEFL does |
|---|---|
| Positive PBL trials (KIA, ML-PBL, Project PLACE) were all **structured**: the project is the spine and content is taught through it | The project *is* the unit. The workbook moments are the mini-lessons inside it, not a phase before it |
| **Driving question first** creates a "need to know" | Session 1 launches the driving question; students write a need-to-know list. Each later moment answers an item on that list |
| **Explicit instruction inside the project** answers the minimal-guidance critique (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006 vs. Hmelo-Silver et al. 2007) | Every work session opens with a 10–15 min moment: worked example, then 2–3 practice problems |
| Financial education decays when taught far from the decision; **just-in-time** teaching is best supported (Fernandes et al. 2014; Kaiser et al. 2022) | Each moment comes right before students use it in the project |
| **Critique → revise at every milestone** (Berger) | A checkpoint every work session: peer protocol + teacher look-for; students revise before the next milestone |
| **Teams of 4–5 with roles** (0.91 vs. 0.44 for 6–7) | Home teams of 4–5 with rotating roles in every project. Personal content (career, budget) → personal product with team critique; shared content → one team product |
| **Public product to a real audience** | Each project names a real audience beyond the class |
| **Don't grade only the final product** | Rubric scores milestones too: **Knowledge at milestones, Understanding in the justification, Skills & Procedures in the product** |
| **Reflection**; students and teachers often disagree on authenticity | Session 7 reflection plus a 2-question authenticity check |
| **Content spirals across projects** (KIA) | A year-long **My Plan** portfolio gets one page from each project (career → pathway → experiences → pay → budget). Each project still stands alone |
| **Implementation fidelity explains most of the variance** (North American subgroup ≈ 0.06) | Teacher guides are concrete: a session script, look-fors, the critique protocol, role cards, what to do when a team stalls |
| **Career readiness comes from contact with real work** (OECD 2021) | Career projects require a real conversation, guest, posting, or workplace |

### Unit rhythm (7 sessions)

| Session | Project cycle step | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Entry event + driving question + need-to-know | Real-life hook scenario, driving question, home teams and roles, need-to-know list, Moment 1 |
| 2 | Mini-lesson → build → checkpoint | Moment 2, milestone 1 work, checkpoint (critique → revise) |
| 3 | Mini-lesson → build → checkpoint | Moment 3, milestone 2 work, checkpoint |
| 4 | Mini-lesson → build → checkpoint | Moment 4, milestone 3 work, checkpoint |
| 5 | Skill workshop → build | Product skills (e.g., making an infographic readable), draft 1 of the product |
| 6 | Final critique + revise | Gallery-walk critique of draft 1, revise to draft 2 |
| 7 | Public product + reflection | Share to the real audience, reflection, authenticity check, My Plan page |

Each session opens with a 3–5 minute retrieval warm-up pulling from earlier moments (listed in the teacher guide). That is where spacing comes from, since the mini-workbook is not interleaved.

### Mini-workbook spec

What changes from `worksheets/workbooks-context.md`:
- **Small:** 4 moments per unit, 1–2 printed pages each.
- **Each moment, in this order:**
  1. **Scenario** — a real Alberta situation with real, dated numbers (no pseudo-context)
  2. **Visual** — an inline SVG explainer (diagram, infographic, pay stub, map) with labels on the visual, no legends, no decoration
  3. **Worked example** — numbered steps with the reason for each
  4. **Practice** — **2–3 problems**, guided → independent; at least one asks students to explain or justify
  5. **Extra practice** — an optional box with 2–3 more problems at the same level, for students who need more
  6. **Self-check** — ☐ Yes ☐ Almost ☐ Not yet, against one "I can" statement
  7. **Use it in your project** — one line linking the moment to the next milestone
- **Cross-curricular:** at most one small "Connects to" tag per moment (e.g., *Connects to Math 7: percent of a number*), only where the link is real. Never a whole task built around another subject.
- **Kept from the main spec:** F&P P–Q reading level (curriculum terms kept), no decorative images, response space sized to the question, one misconception item per unit (from the unit's list above), a forethought prompt at the start and a short reflection at the end.
- **Dropped:** full interleaving and the ★/★★ minimum per cluster. Moments are short, sit in one scenario, and are used just in time.
- **Data:** every real figure has its year printed beside it. Re-check figures each January (see the 2026 list in `research/financial-career-education-research.md`).

### Project brief spec

Every project brief contains, in this order:
1. **Driving question** and the **entry event** (the hook scenario)
2. **Need-to-know list** (students fill it in during Session 1)
3. **Home team roles** for 4–5 people, rotating each session (e.g., Facilitator, Researcher, Calculator/Checker, Designer, Reporter)
4. **Milestone calendar** matched to Sessions 1–7
5. **Choices:** students choose their **scenario** (career, job posting, goal) and their **product** from a menu (poster or infographic, slideshow, comic, short video or podcast, written report)
6. **Organizers** students fill in themselves, a **visual task card**, **vocabulary cards**, and **2–3 deliberately different sample products** described in the teacher guide (never a single model; it narrows what students make)
7. **Critique protocol:** kind / specific / helpful, with sentence frames
8. **Real audience** named
9. **Single-point rubric on the report-card scale** (`templates/rubric-phrase-bank.md`): columns ✗ Has yet to meet · ✓ Meets (the outcome) · ★ Beyond (Proficient or Excellent); each row tagged K, U or S&P and scored 4 Excellent · 3 Proficient · 2 Meets · 1 Has yet to meet. Write Meets from the outcome; describe, don't count. Teamwork and "made 2 changes" go on a hand-in checklist, not in the rubric. Scored at milestones and on the final product
10. **Reflection** + **authenticity check** ("Did this feel like something real people do? What would make it more real?")
11. **My Plan** portfolio page

**UDL (CAST 3.0) at the strategy level:**
- **Engagement:** choice of scenario, real audience, chunked milestones
- **Representation:** visual task card, vocabulary cards, read-aloud-friendly text, varied samples
- **Action and expression:** product menu, organizers, sentence frames as access supports only

**Not allowed:** "go research it" without curated sources and an organizer; projects that depend on another unit (if a project uses a number from an earlier unit, the brief supplies a default); teams larger than 5.

## Threads Across All Units

- **Decision Making:** trade-offs, opportunity cost, aligning choices with goals and values. Every unit has at least one "compare and decide" skill. Build at least one decision task per unit.
- **Planning for the Future:** the course produces a set of plans across grades: Gr 7 personal growth plan → Gr 8 career plan and high school course plan → Gr 9 post-secondary plan, resumé, and credit plan. Consider a single portfolio that carries forward year to year.
- **Numeracy:** the subject introduction names numeracy explicitly. Calculation outcomes live in Income and Tax and in Money, Interest, and Credit.

## Learning Outcomes by Unit

### Careers and the Labour Market

**Grade 7 — LO (verbatim):** Students explore the foundations of careers.
- **Understanding:** Careers are dynamic and are shaped by a variety of factors.
- **Knowledge clusters:**
  1. Experiences, education, personal identity, interests, and values shape career aspirations; interests can shift over time
  2. A job is a specific role; a career is an evolving pathway of learning, work, and personal experiences; people can change careers multiple times
  3. Transferable skills are required across careers and are developed through education and experience
  4. Career decisions weigh preferences, options, education, cost, and access; different careers need different types and levels of education and training
- **Skills (verbatim/close):** explore the difference between jobs and careers; develop a career pathway of several roles or jobs; evaluate how careers align with interests and values; identify careers that share transferable skills; design a personal growth plan to improve transferable skills; explore a personalized future pathway (education, training, experience); talk with people about careers; rank careers by personal interest.

**Grade 8 — LO (verbatim):** Students analyze the dynamic nature of the labour market and develop an understanding of the knowledge, skills, and goals required to build career resilience.
- **Understandings:** The labour market is dynamic, making adaptability a more reliable career strategy than job stability alone. Career resilience is built deliberately through continuous learning, transferable skills, and purposeful networking. The growth of human capital directly influences career opportunities and long-term employability.
- **Knowledge clusters:**
  1. The labour market is shaped by economic cycles, technological advancement, and social change; technology has positive and negative impacts
  2. Career resilience (adapting without losing employment); job security (staying in one role) vs. career security (finding new work quickly)
  3. Emerging industries can be identified through government policy changes and capital investment
  4. Transferable skills enhance employability and are valued differently by the labour market
  5. Human capital: the economic value of a person's combined education, knowledge, skills, and experience
  6. Career goals: short- and long-term with milestones; clear goals help, rigid goals add stress and reduce flexibility
- **Skills:** analyze positive and negative impacts of technology on the labour market; explore factors that change the labour market; build a career plan with short- and long-term milestones; identify and rank transferable skills that improve employability.

**Grade 9 — LO (verbatim):** Students recognize that success in career goals requires proactive measures.
- **Understandings:** Individuals who manage and communicate their human capital are better positioned to navigate labour market disruptions. Career success increasingly depends on identifying where the labour market is moving, not just where it is.
- **Knowledge clusters:**
  1. Automation (technology doing tasks people did) can disrupt labour; labour markets cross national borders (globalization)
  2. Industry sectors are growing, stable, or declining, depending on trends, policy, investment, and technology; labour market information comes from government databases, industry reports, and professional associations
  3. Personal branding; resumés communicate human capital
  4. Job searching: networking, job fairs, employer websites, online searches
  5. Interviews; self-advocacy (articulating one's own skills, value, and needs)
- **Skills:** analyze how automation and globalization reshape industries and roles; identify growing, stable, and declining industries with current data; write a resumé tailored to a specific opportunity; write a personal brand statement; practise interviews; demonstrate self-advocacy in a professional scenario; evaluate job postings for skills and qualifications.

**Misconceptions *(this workspace's addition — confirm)*:**
- Staying in one job for life is the safest plan (the curriculum's point: career security beats job security)
- A career is chosen once, at the end of high school
- Skills only count in the job where you learned them
- Automation only affects factory work
- A resumé is a list of every job you've had, rather than evidence matched to one opportunity

### Post-Secondary Pathways

**Grade 7 — LO (verbatim):** Students will learn that post-secondary pathways require balancing personal goals, financial considerations, and labour market demand.
- **Understandings:** Post-secondary pathways help prepare individuals for careers. Post-secondary pathways require thoughtful consideration of costs and opportunities.
- **Knowledge clusters:**
  1. Post-secondary = education and training after high school; pathways are flexible and you can change direction
  2. Pathways prioritize different learning (academic, theoretical, applied, hands-on); universities, colleges, and polytechnics all lead to meaningful careers; the same career can be reached by different pathways; all pathways are valuable
  3. Costs: tuition, fees, living expenses, plus employment outcomes and the risk of not completing
  4. The labour market: shortages and surpluses; programs that meet labour market needs are more valuable; financial outcomes (income, stability, benefits) vary across careers
  5. Admission requirements are typically based on high school grades
  6. Choosing a pathway means trade-offs between time, energy, money, and outcomes; balancing interests with realistic employment
  7. Ways to learn about careers: optional courses, volunteering, talking with people in a career or pathway
- **Skills:** identify local post-secondary institutions; compare the type of learning each prioritizes; identify high- and low-demand careers; list costs of pathways alongside the income of the matching career; identify labour surplus and shortage; discuss the equal value of at least two pathways (e.g., degree and apprenticeship); research a pathway choice, naming what must be sacrificed and why; analyze the true cost of a pathway (direct: tuition, fees, living; indirect: lost earnings, time).

**Grade 8 — LO (verbatim):** Students evaluate post-secondary pathways and labour market information to develop realistic, informed education and career goals.
- **Understanding:** Career goals are most achievable when grounded in personal aspirations, finances, and an understanding of labour market realities.
- **Knowledge clusters:**
  1. Admission requirements vary by field and include specific courses and grades; competitive programs: meeting the minimum does not guarantee admission
  2. Early planning and course selection keep options open
  3. Labour market information shows what is in demand now and may be in the future
  4. Tuition varies significantly across programs; grants, bursaries, and scholarships help cover costs
  5. Networking: building professional relationships to exchange information, ideas, and support
- **Skills:** develop a high school course plan that meets post-secondary and workforce requirements; compare admission requirements and costs; analyze labour market information for high-demand fields; compare career aspirations with labour market information; compare tuition across pathways; identify networking opportunities; start conversations about careers and pathways.

**Grade 9 — LO (verbatim):** Students evaluate post-secondary options by weighing personal goals, financial costs, and labour market outcomes.
- **Understandings:** Choosing a post-secondary pathway requires balancing personal values and interests against realistic employment outcomes and the full financial cost of the pathway. Post-secondary financial assistance can improve affordability and access to post-secondary pathways.
- **Knowledge clusters:**
  1. Student loans are debt repaid with interest after graduation
  2. True cost: tuition, fees, living expenses, and the opportunity cost of time in school instead of the workforce
  3. Return on investment: total cost vs. expected income and opportunities over time; affected by graduation rates, employment rates in the field, and regional conditions at completion
  4. Competitive admission can include portfolios, interviews, references, and extracurriculars
  5. Credential recognition varies across provinces and countries
  6. Work-integrated learning (co-op, internships, apprenticeships) reduces cost while building human capital
  7. Mature students, career-changers, and people re-entering the labour market access pathways differently
- **Skills:** compare expected ROI of pathways using income data; evaluate student loans and financial aid, including long-term repayment; build a post-secondary plan with a primary pathway, a contingency, and a rationale (financial, personal, labour market); assess how work-integrated learning reduces cost and improves outcomes; analyze how competitive admission shapes access and how to strengthen an application.

**Misconceptions *(this workspace's addition — confirm)*:**
- University is the best or only real post-secondary option (the curriculum states all pathways are valuable)
- Tuition is the whole cost (ignores living expenses and lost earnings)
- Meeting the minimum admission average guarantees a seat
- Student loans and grants are the same thing
- A program choice is permanent

### Experiences and Employment

**Grade 7 — LO (verbatim):** Students examine the influence of experiences on employment preferences.
- **Understanding:** Experiences can inform career choices.
- **Knowledge clusters:**
  1. Experiences can be extracurricular, curricular, or work-related (volunteering, entrepreneurship); volunteering at school (peer mentoring) or in the community (camp counselling)
  2. Work environments: indoor/outdoor, in-person/virtual, near/far from home; independent or team
  3. Employment types: employed or self-employed; full-time or part-time; regular or casual; permanent or temporary (e.g., seasonal)
  4. Work-life balance; employment choices can support it
  5. Belonging and safety: workplace culture that promotes diversity, equity, and inclusion; workplace safety policies and practices
- **Skills:** discuss reasons for choosing experiences; brainstorm volunteering and entrepreneurship options; relate experiences to future work choices; discuss work preferences; relate work-life balance to employment choices; reflect on personal work-life balance; discuss how workplaces promote belonging and safety.

**Grade 8 — LO (verbatim):** Students investigate the role of experiences in the evaluation of career possibilities.
- **Understanding:** Experiences can reveal opportunities and challenges related to career possibilities.
- **Knowledge clusters:**
  1. Equity of representation in careers; representation can be inequitable and shapes motivation and personal vision of career possibilities
  2. Personal skills and a growth mindset influence motivation to engage in experiences
  3. Employability skills: transferable skills that contribute to success in any career (communication, collaboration, problem solving, critical thinking, technology use, time management), built through volunteering, entrepreneurship, and work-integrated learning
  4. Entrepreneurial success is supported by risk management, time management, and decision making
- **Skills:** research equity of representation in careers; reflect on influences on personal engagement; apply a growth mindset to employability skill development; explore experiences that build employability skills; reflect on employability skills developed in or out of school.

**Grade 9 — LO (verbatim):** Students relate experiences and employment opportunities to career pathways.

*Cluster 1 (9-EX1) — experiences*
- **Understanding:** Experiences can spark interest in career pathways.
- **Knowledge:** volunteering and entrepreneurship inform career interest; entrepreneurship exposes students to business operations (a business plan) and responsibilities (managing employees, mitigating financial risk); skills develop through formal (school courses, hands-on training) and informal (learning from family) experiences; some skills, such as additional languages and technology, come from both; skills transfer across pathways; some careers in Canada require French and English.
- **Skills:** research volunteer, work-integrated learning, and entrepreneurship opportunities; talk with people about them; explore one such opportunity in or out of school; reflect on skills developed; evaluate employability vs. job-specific skills for careers of interest; explore careers that need bilingualism as an asset or prerequisite.

*Cluster 2 (9-EX2) — attaining employment*
- **Understanding:** Employment opportunities can contribute to career pathways.
- **Knowledge:** resources (online employment tools, personal networks); employment services for newcomers, Francophone individuals, and under-represented groups; actions (resumés, cover letters, online profiles and applications, interview preparation); adjusting communication to the employer; skills, education, experience, and access influence hiring; digital identity (e.g., social media) influences hiring.
- **Skills:** investigate employment resources; take actions toward attaining employment; explain the role of communication; discuss factors that influence hiring; explain risks and benefits of digital identity; reflect on personal digital identity.

**Misconceptions *(this workspace's addition — confirm)*:**
- Only paid jobs count as real work experience
- Being your own boss means no boss and no risk
- What you post online is private and won't affect hiring
- Employability skills are personality traits you either have or don't (the growth-mindset point)

### Income and Tax

**Grade 7 — LO (verbatim):** Students develop an understanding of employment and employment income.
- **Understanding:** Employment income is reduced by taxes and deductions, shaping take-home pay and financial choices.
- **Knowledge clusters:**
  1. Employment: an employer pays an employee for work; agreements vary (permanent, temporary, full-time, part-time, regular, casual); employment brings income, benefits, and a sense of purpose; stable employment is linked to well-being
  2. Income depends on education, training, experience, labour market needs, and skills
  3. Forms of income: wages (hourly, overtime, minimum wage laws), salary (fixed regardless of hours), commission (a percentage of sales), tips and gratuities, bonuses, entrepreneurship profits
  4. Gross income (before taxes and deductions) vs. net income ("take-home pay")
  5. Mandatory deductions: income tax, CPP, EI; in Alberta income tax goes to both the province and Canada; optional deductions: union dues, insurance, retirement savings
  6. Canada's progressive system; a basic personal amount is tax-free; rates are set by the provincial and federal governments
  7. Taxes pay for education, healthcare, police, fire services, national defence
- **Skills:** explore how employment improves well-being; compare employment arrangements; compare advantages and limitations of forms of income; apply current tax rates to calculate tax withheld and net pay; argue whether more or less tax should be deducted.

**Grade 8 — LO (verbatim):** Students analyze income taxation systems and total compensation to evaluate employment options and understand the role taxes play in supporting society.
- **Understandings:** Total compensation and tax implications can be helpful to consider when evaluating a job offer. Taxes are the mechanism through which individuals collectively fund the public services and infrastructure that benefit society as a whole.
- **Knowledge clusters:**
  1. Tax brackets: different portions of income are taxed at different rates
  2. Progressive (higher income, higher rate), regressive (higher rate on low-income earners), and flat (same rate for all) tax systems
  3. Total compensation: income plus bonuses, allowances, and non-monetary benefits (insurance, vehicles, vacation); job conditions, promotion, and hours also matter
  4. Social contract: individuals pay taxes to fund services such as healthcare and education
- **Skills:** calculate income tax across brackets; compare progressive, regressive, and flat systems; calculate total compensation; compare job offers for financial security and fit with goals; explain and evaluate how taxes fund public services.

**Grade 9 — LO (verbatim):** Students recognize how registered accounts and government benefits shape personal finances.
- **Understandings:** Understanding how income is taxed and deducted is essential to making accurate financial plans, as gross income and net pay can differ significantly. The financial value of employment is determined not only by wages but by the combined effect of taxation, deductions, benefits, and employment conditions.
- **Knowledge clusters:**
  1. T4 slip (total employment income and deductions for the year); tax return filed with the CRA, which determines a refund or balance owing
  2. Tax credits reduce tax owed (tuition, charitable donations, disability); tax deductions reduce taxable income
  3. RRSP: contributions reduce taxable income; growth is tax-deferred until withdrawal. TFSA: after-tax contributions; growth and withdrawals are tax-free
  4. Self-employment income: taxed differently; remit your own CPP and, in some cases, EI
  5. All income is taxable and reportable, including freelance work, tips, and gig earnings
  6. Government benefits (Canada Child Benefit, GST/HST credit) are income-tested and delivered through the tax system
- **Skills:** interpret a T4; calculate the effect of credits and deductions on tax owed; compare RRSP and TFSA outcomes in different scenarios; distinguish employment from self-employment income and the tax implications; identify reportable income and the obligation to report it; evaluate how understanding tax improves financial position.

**Depth ceilings *(this workspace's addition — confirm)*:**
- Use the current year's federal and Alberta brackets, basic personal amounts, and CPP/EI rates. Date every material that uses them; rates change every January.
- Gr 7 net pay: a single pay period with simplified percentages or a provided deduction table; no payroll formulas.
- Gr 8 bracket calculations: two or three brackets per level, whole-dollar incomes.
- Gr 9: interpret a T4 and compute tax owed with one or two credits and one deduction; no full return, no capital gains, no contribution-room rules beyond "there is a limit."

**Misconceptions *(this workspace's addition — confirm)*:**
- Moving into a higher bracket means all your income is taxed at the higher rate (only the portion above the threshold is)
- Your wage × hours is what lands in your account
- A tax refund is a bonus from the government (it's your own over-withheld money)
- Tips and gig income don't need to be reported
- A TFSA is just a savings account (it can hold investments)
- Tax credits and tax deductions do the same thing

### Money, Interest, and Credit

**Grade 7 — LO (verbatim):** Students explain how spending, saving, and budgeting decisions affect financial security.
- **Understanding:** Individuals aspire to achieve financial security.
- **Knowledge clusters:**
  1. Money management: daily spending or saving to meet needs, wants, and goals; every dollar involves a trade-off (e.g., buying a book vs. saving to spend later)
  2. Financial security: stable income and savings to cover living expenses, handle emergencies, and plan ahead; reduces stress, improves health, gives freedom of choice
  3. Poor money management leads to debt, financial stress, and reduced flexibility
  4. Budgets: a written plan for how income will be spent over a period; used by businesses, organizations, governments, households, individuals; weekly, monthly, yearly, multi-year
  5. Financial goals: short term (next year's vacation) and long term (retirement); paying off debt, saving, investing, major purchases
- **Skills:** describe and give examples of financial trade-offs; explain why financial security is desirable; discuss why poor money management is not; develop examples of poor and effective decisions; analyze a budget against goals and modify it; compare spending scenarios for financial security; build a budget to reach a goal; apply budgeting, saving, and decision-making skills to real or simulated scenarios.

**Grade 8 — LO (verbatim):** Students investigate strategies to achieve financial security by analyzing how costs, interest, and savings help manage personal finances.
- **Understandings:** Financial security depends on actively managing fixed and variable costs so that money is consistently available for saving and investing. Interest is a force that works both for and against individuals.
- **Knowledge clusters:**
  1. Costs: time, money, or resources to obtain, produce, or keep something; fixed (predictable) vs. variable (change with choices and circumstances)
  2. Interest: the cost of borrowing or the reward for saving; principal; rate (per year); compound interest (interest on principal plus earned interest)
  3. Saving (setting money aside) vs. investing (putting money into something that will grow)
  4. Risk and return: all investment has risk; higher risk may bring higher return and greater chance of loss; low risk, low return, safer principal
- **Skills:** define cost, fixed cost, variable cost, interest, principal, rate, saving, investing, return, risk; calculate simple interest; calculate compound interest; compute total owed or earned; create a personal budget separating fixed and variable costs; compare saving and investing; adjust variable costs to reach goals.

**Grade 9 — LO (verbatim):** Students analyze how debt and credit function and evaluate strategies for borrowing responsibly to protect long-term financial security.
- **Understandings:** Debt is a financial tool that can either accelerate or undermine financial security, depending on how deliberately and responsibly it is used. Compound interest amplifies both the growth of savings and the cost of debt, making time and interest rate the two most consequential variables in any borrowing or saving decision. A strong credit history is a financial asset that expands access to borrowing, reduces interest costs, and influences opportunities well beyond lending.
- **Knowledge clusters:**
  1. Debt (borrowed money repaid with interest over time); credit (borrowing or buying now, paying later)
  2. Consumer credit: credit cards, lines of credit, personal loans, student loans, buy-now-pay-later
  3. Credit score: factors are payment history, utilization, length of history, types of credit, new applications
  4. Minimum payments greatly extend repayment and total interest
  5. Repayment strategies: avalanche (highest interest first), snowball (smallest balance first)
  6. Predatory lending (payday loans, high-interest instalment loans) targets vulnerable people and creates debt cycles
  7. Debt-to-income ratio: monthly debt payments ÷ monthly gross income
- **Skills:** calculate total cost of borrowing with compound interest; compare minimum vs. accelerated repayment; analyze how credit score factors affect access and rates, with real examples; compare a predatory product to a conventional alternative; apply avalanche or snowball to a scenario and calculate the outcome; calculate and interpret a debt-to-income ratio; plan for building positive credit from an early age.

**Depth ceilings *(this workspace's addition — confirm)*:**
- Gr 8 compound interest: annual compounding, by repeated multiplication or a table first, then A = P(1 + r)ⁿ; no logarithms, no solving for n or r.
- Gr 9 borrowing: monthly compounding is fine in a spreadsheet or provided table; students interpret amortization output rather than derive the payment formula.
- Credit scores: factors and their effect only; no scoring formulas (they are proprietary).

**Misconceptions *(this workspace's addition — confirm)*:**
- A budget means cutting out everything fun
- Saving and investing are the same thing
- You can get high returns without high risk
- Paying the minimum on a credit card means you're keeping up
- Avoiding credit entirely gives you a good credit score
- Simple and compound interest give about the same result (true only over short periods)

## Project Ideas *(this workspace's addition)*

All three grades are built: Grade 7 in `worksheets/cefl-7/`, Grade 8 in `worksheets/cefl-8/`, Grade 9 in `worksheets/cefl-9/` (teacher guides in `lesson-plans/cefl-[G]/`).

### Careers and the Labour Market
- Gr 7: **Career Pathway Map** — "What path could take me from here to a career I'd want?" Personal pathway map (roles, education, experience) for a career chosen with CAREERinsite, a growth plan for 2 transferable skills, and one real conversation with an adult in that field. Audience: families / career fair table
- Gr 8: **Future-Proof Me** — "How can I build a career that can survive change?" Personal career plan: how economic cycles, technology and social change could affect the career; human capital and resilience; milestones and a backup. Audience: a Grade 7 class
- Gr 9: **Get Hired** — "How do I show an employer what I can do?" Industry trend analysis, a résumé and brand statement tailored to a real posting, and a mock interview. Audience: volunteer interviewers

### Post-Secondary Pathways
- Gr 7: **Two Roads** — "Is there more than one good way to reach the same career?" Team compares two real pathways to one career on true cost, time, type of learning and demand, then recommends one and names the trade-offs. Audience: school counsellor or a guest panel
- Gr 8: **My High School Map** — "What do I need to do in high school to keep my options open?" Grade 10–12 course plan for two programs, tuition and awards, a networking conversation. Audience: counsellor or high school students
- Gr 9: **Pathway Pitch** — "How do I know if a post-secondary pathway is worth the cost?" True cost, student loans and payback with ALIS income data; work-integrated learning; a contingency and rationale. Audience: a panel playing a family

### Experiences and Employment
- Gr 7: **Experience Plan** — "What do my experiences tell me about the work I'd like?" Personal experience audit plus one planned (ideally done) volunteer or micro-venture experience; team builds a workplace belonging and safety checklist. Audience: a Grade 6 class or school leadership group
- Gr 8: **Open Doors** — "Why do some people think a career is 'not for them,' and how can we open the door?" Team campaign on representation in one career using data and a real story; personal growth-mindset skill plan. Audience: Grade 6–7 classes
- Gr 9: **Pitch It** — "How can an experience I create move me toward my career?" Team venture with costs, break-even and risks; each member applies for a role (cover letter) and checks their digital identity. Audience: a pitch panel

### Income and Tax
- Gr 7: **First Paycheque** — "Where does my paycheque go?" Team picks a real local entry-level job posting, builds a pay stub from gross to net, and explains it to Grade 6 students OR argues for more or less deduction. Audience: a Grade 6 class
- Gr 8: **Job Offer Showdown** — "Which job offer is really worth more?" After-tax pay using 2026 brackets, total compensation, non-money factors, and a view on tax fairness. Audience: a panel playing the job seeker
- Gr 9: **Tax Clinic** — "How can understanding taxes put more money in my pocket?" A client case (T4, tips, gig income, tuition): tax owed, credits, RRSP vs. TFSA, self-employed CPP, refund. Audience: volunteers playing clients

### Money, Interest, and Credit
- Gr 7: **Saving for Something Real** — "How can I reach a money goal without losing control of my spending?" Personal monthly budget toward a chosen goal (default income supplied), a surprise-expense card, two spending scenarios compared. Audience: families
- Gr 8: **Grow It** — "How can I make my money grow without losing control of my costs?" Budget with fixed and variable costs, simple vs. compound interest, saving vs. investing for two goals. Audience: a Money Fair
- Gr 9: **Debt Detective** — "How can borrowing help or hurt my future?" A debt case: interest, credit score factors, debt-to-income, avalanche vs. snowball, a payday loan vs. alternatives; personal credit plan. Audience: a Grade 8 class
