## Q7 ★★ — Gambler's Fallacy

**(a)** P(heads on flip 9) = **1/2**

Because: each coin flip is completely independent — the coin has no memory of previous flips. P(heads) is always 1/2.

**(b) The Gambler's Fallacy** — the belief that past independent events affect future probabilities.

**(c)** Over a very large number of flips, heads and tails will each appear close to 50% of the time (Law of Large Numbers). BUT this happens because future flips collectively balance out — not because any single flip "compensates" for the past. The coin does not adjust to correct an imbalance.

## Q8 ★ — Experimental probability (spinner, 50 spins: R=18, B=14, Y=10, G=8)

**(a)**

| Colour | Frequency | Experimental P |
|---|---|---|
| Red | 18 | **18/50 = 9/25 = 0.36** |
| Blue | 14 | **14/50 = 7/25 = 0.28** |
| Yellow | 10 | **10/50 = 1/5 = 0.20** |

**(b)** Theoretical = **1/4 = 0.25** each (equal sections)

**(c)** Red is furthest above theoretical (0.36 vs 0.25); Green is furthest below (0.16 vs 0.25).

**(d)** Yes — with 5000 spins, the Law of Large Numbers brings experimental probabilities closer to theoretical. The larger the sample, the less chance variation dominates.

## Q9 ★ — Interpolation and extrapolation (y = 3.5x + 48)

**(a) x = 6:** y = 3.5(6)+48 = 21+48 = **69%**
**Interpolation** — 6 hours is within the data range (2–12 h). Reliable.

**(b) x = 20:** y = 3.5(20)+48 = 70+48 = **118%**
**Extrapolation** — 20 hours is beyond the data range.

**(c)** 118% is impossible (scores max at 100%). The linear model breaks down — students studying 20 hours/week likely experience diminishing returns, fatigue, or stress. The trend observed in 2–12 hours cannot be assumed to continue indefinitely.

## Q10 ★ — Dependent events without replacement (5G, 3Y, 8 total)

**(a)** P(G draw 1) = **5/8**
**(b)** After drawing green: **4 green** remain, **7 total** remain. P(G draw 2|G draw 1) = **4/7**
**(c)** P(G,G) = (5/8)(4/7) = **20/56 = 5/14 ≈ 0.357**
**(d)** With replacement: (5/8)(5/8) = **25/64 ≈ 0.391**. **With replacement is larger.** Replacing restores P(G) to 5/8 for draw 2; without replacement, it drops to 4/7 because one green is gone.

## Q11 ★★ — Correlation ≠ causation (hospitals and deaths)

> (a) No — **city population size** causes both. Larger cities have more hospitals (needed to serve more people) AND more deaths (more people total). Neither hospitals nor deaths cause each other.
>
> (b) The third factor is called a **confounding variable** (confounder).
>
> (c) Example: More swimming pools in a neighbourhood correlates with higher home values. Third variable = **wealth** — affluent areas have more private pools AND higher property values.

## Q13 ★ — Canola equation y = 0.012x + 1.5

> (a) Slope: each additional **1 kg/ha** of fertilizer increases yield by approximately **0.012 tonnes/ha**.
> (b) y-intercept: baseline yield of **1.5 t/ha** with zero fertilizer.
> (c) x=150: y=1.8+1.5=**3.3 t/ha** — **interpolation** (within 0–200 range). Reliable.
> (d) x=500: y=6+1.5=7.5 t/ha — **extrapolation**, unreliable because excess fertilizer causes soil damage, runoff, toxicity, and declining yields — the linear trend does not hold at extreme amounts.

## Q16 ★★ — Outlier: 8h exercise, 105 bpm

> (a) Yes, outlier. It would pull the line of best fit **upward on the right side**, appearing to weaken the negative correlation. The line may have a less steep negative slope than the other 9 points suggest.
>
> **Keep:** The data point is valid — this person genuinely exercises 8 hours/week with high heart rate. Removing it hides real variation and may reflect an underlying health condition worth studying.
>
> **Remove:** If the person is known to have a heart arrhythmia (a medical condition, not exercise-related), they belong to a different population. Including them contaminates a model meant for healthy adults.
>
> (c) Possible explanations: underlying cardiac condition; overtraining syndrome (extreme exercise can paradoxically elevate resting HR); measurement taken immediately after exercise (error); high caffeine or stress on measurement day.

## Q19 ★ — Independent vs. dependent

| Scenario | Answer | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Roll die, flip coin | **Independent** | Physically separate events; neither affects the other |
| Draw card, keep, draw again | **Dependent** | Deck shrinks; first draw changes what's available |
| Draw card, replace, draw again | **Independent** | Deck restored to original composition |
| Select student, then another (different) | **Dependent** | Class shrinks; first student no longer available |
| Marble from bag A, then from bag B | **Independent** | Separate bags; first draw doesn't affect second bag |

**S3** — Ice cream + crime

> (a) **Correlation ≠ causation.** No mechanism connects ice cream to crime.
> (b) Third variable: **population density / summer heat.** Warm, densely populated areas have more outdoor activity → more ice cream purchases AND more opportunity for crime.
> (c) Test: compare cities that **opened ice cream shops without other changes** (same population, same season, same economic conditions) and measure whether crime actually changed. Or compare the same city with and without ice cream shops. A controlled study with matched confounders over time would help separate correlation from causation.
