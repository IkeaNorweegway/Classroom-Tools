# Number — Inquiry Project

[Rational Numbers · Powers & Exponents · Square Roots]{.subtitle}

**The big question:** *How can you trust a number you can't count or measure directly?*

Your team picks **one of three options**. Each one asks for a number that's too big, too awkward or too far away to measure. You get there by measuring what you *can*, estimating first, calculating carefully, and checking. Then you show your answer to a real audience.

**5 class periods** · **Teams of 4–5** · Keep everything in your **Project Journal**.
:::

::: {.section-heading}
How the project works
:::

| Period | What your team does | Milestone (teacher checks) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pick roles. Make a gut-feeling guess. Plan what you need to know and how you will find it. Start measuring. | **M1 — Plan:** guess, need-to-know list, plan |
| 2 | Short lesson, then build your model: measurements, a table or diagram, and the calculation rule. | **M2 — Model:** model built and checked |
| 3 | Short lesson, then estimate, calculate and check the answer. Write a first draft. | **M3 — Draft:** answer, with the estimate and the calculation shown |
| 4 | Gallery walk: give and get feedback. Revise. | **M4 — Revised:** revision log filled in |
| 5 | Show your answer to your audience. Write your own reflection. | **M5 — Final:** product + individual reflection |

::: {.hint}
[Rule]{.hint-label} Estimate before you calculate, every time. If your calculator answer is nowhere near your estimate, one of them is wrong. Find out which before you move on.
:::

### Team roles

Everyone does math. Roles decide who is responsible for *checking* each part.

| Role | You are responsible for… |
|---|---|
| **Facilitator** | Keeping the team on the milestone. Making sure everyone has a turn to explain. |
| **Recorder** | The team's copy of the measurements, table and calculations. Neat, labelled, with units. |
| **Checker** | Asking "how do we know?" Comparing every calculation with its estimate. |
| **Data / Materials** | Measurements and sources. Getting and returning materials. |
| **Skeptic** *(team of 5)* | Finding the weakest part of the work before the gallery walk does. |

## Fold to the Moon
:::

**Guiding question:** *How many times would you have to fold a sheet of paper in half for it to reach the Moon?*

**Context.** Each fold doubles the thickness, so the paper grows faster than most people expect. Nobody can actually fold paper that many times: in 2002, a high school student named Britney Gallivan set a record by folding paper in half 12 times. But powers let you find the answer without folding at all.

::: {.data-card}
**Average distance from Earth to the Moon:** about **384 400 km**\
**Materials:** a full ream of copy paper (500 sheets), a ruler, a few loose sheets to fold\
*Distance: NASA, average Earth–Moon distance.*
:::

**What you will do**

1. **Guess (M1).** Each person guesses the number of folds: 50? 1000? a million? Plan: how will you find the thickness of **one** sheet when it's too thin to measure?
2. **Model (M2).** Measure the ream and divide to find the thickness of one sheet. Fold a sheet 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 times and count the layers. Make a table of folds → layers → thickness, and write a rule: **thickness = (one sheet) × 2^n^**.

   Then answer: *Why does 0 folds give 2^0^ layers, and what does 2^0^ equal?*
3. **Calculate (M3).** Write the Moon's distance in millimetres as a number × a power of 10. Work out how many layers you need. Then find the number of folds *n* that gives at least that many layers. **Estimate first:** 2^10^ ≈ 1000, so what is 2^20^ roughly? 2^40^?

   **Then:** after 32 folds, what fraction as thick is the stack as the one that reaches the Moon? Write it as a power of 2 and as a fraction. How many kilometres thick is it? (The International Space Station orbits about 400 km up.)
4. **Critique and revise (M4).** Gallery walk. Fix anything the feedback finds.
5. **Myth-buster (M5).** Make a 1-page "Fold to the Moon" explainer or a 60-second video script for another class: the answer, the rule, and why doubling gets so big so fast.

**What to hand in** (in the Project Journal)

- Ream measurement and one-sheet thickness, with the division shown
- Folds → layers → thickness table (0–5 folds measured, the rest calculated)
- The rule, and an explanation of 2^0^
- The Moon calculation: estimate, powers-of-10 work, number of folds
- The 32-fold fraction as a power of 2 and as a fraction, and its thickness in km

::: {.stretch-card}
**★★ Extended.** How many folds would it take to reach the top of Mount Everest (8849 m)? Your answer to the Moon question is *n*. Explain how you could get the Everest answer from it using the quotient of powers, without starting over.
:::

*Math you will use: PE1, PE2, PE3, PE4, PE5, PE7, RN1, RN4*
:::

## Will It Fit?
:::

**Guiding question:** *What is the longest thing that will fit in your locker, and will a hockey stick make it?*

**Context.** Movers, carpenters and anyone packing a car face this problem: the longest thing that fits in a box doesn't run along an edge. It runs corner to corner, through the middle. Finding that length takes the Pythagorean theorem twice, and the answer is almost never a whole number.

::: {.data-card}
**Measure:** the inside of a school locker (or a cupboard your teacher chooses): width, depth and height\
**Test objects:** a hockey stick (an adult stick is about 150 cm), a broom, a metre stick, something of your choice\
**Materials:** tape measure, string
:::

**What you will do**

1. **Guess (M1).** Which test objects will fit? Guess the longest length that fits. Plan: what do you need to measure?
2. **Model (M2).** Measure the inside of the locker. Draw two right triangles: one across the **floor** (width, depth, floor diagonal), and one standing **inside** the locker (floor diagonal, height, long diagonal). Label every side.

   Then answer: *Why does the longest object go corner to corner and not along an edge?*
3. **Calculate (M3).** Use the Pythagorean theorem to find the floor diagonal, then the long diagonal. Write each one exactly as √(*number*) first. **Estimate it between two perfect squares** before you use a calculator. Then calculate to 0.1 cm.

   **Then:** is your long diagonal a rational or an irrational number? How do you know? Decide which test objects fit, then check with a piece of string cut to your answer.
4. **Critique and revise (M4).** Gallery walk. Fix anything the feedback finds.
5. **Locker guide (M5).** Make a "Will it fit?" poster for the hallway: the locker diagram, the longest length, which objects fit, and one "it depends" (shelves, hooks, the object's thickness).

**What to hand in** (in the Project Journal)

- Locker measurements with units
- Both labelled right triangles
- Both Pythagorean calculations: exact √, estimate between perfect squares, decimal answer
- Rational or irrational, with a reason
- Fit results for every test object, and the string test

::: {.stretch-card}
**★★ Extended.** TVs are sold by their diagonal. A "65-inch" TV has a 16 : 9 screen. Find its width and height to 0.1 inch. (Hint: call the width 16*k* and the height 9*k*.)
:::

*Math you will use: SR1, SR2, SR3, RN1, RN2*
:::

## Chinook!
:::

**Guiding question:** *How wild can Alberta weather get in one day, and how would you explain a chinook to someone who has never lived through one?*

**Context.** A chinook is a warm, dry wind that comes down the Rocky Mountains. It can turn a −20 °C morning into a melting afternoon. Measuring how big a swing is means subtracting negatives, averaging negatives and working out rates. Those are exactly the calculations people get wrong.

::: {.data-card}
**Record swing:** In January 1962, the temperature in **Pincher Creek** went from **−19 °C to 22 °C**. Most sources say it took about one hour, but some say a few hours. The record was kept in °F: from **−2 °F to 72 °F**.\
**Your week:** your teacher gives you real daily highs and lows for one winter week in Calgary or Lethbridge, from Environment and Climate Change Canada's historical weather data.\
**Conversion:** °C = 5/9 × (°F − 32)
:::

**What you will do**

1. **Guess (M1).** Guess the biggest temperature change ever recorded in Alberta in one day. Plan: what calculations will tell you how "wild" a week was?
2. **Model (M2).** Make a table for your week: high, low and daily swing (high − low). Plot the lows on a number line. Find the mean high and the mean low.

   Then answer: *Why is a swing from −15 °C to 4 °C a change of 19 degrees, not 11?*
3. **Calculate (M3).** Find the Pincher Creek change in °C. Find the rate in °C per hour if it took 1 hour, and if it took 4 hours (as a mixed number). Convert −2 °F and 72 °F to °C using the formula, showing every step of the order of operations. Do you get the same change?

   **Then:** which day in your week had the biggest swing? How does it compare with Pincher Creek?
4. **Critique and revise (M4).** Gallery walk. Fix anything the feedback finds.
5. **Weather report (M5).** Write and deliver a 60-second "wild weather" segment for the morning announcements, or make a one-page infographic. Include the record, your week, and what a chinook is.

**What to hand in** (in the Project Journal)

- Week table with highs, lows and swings, and the number line
- Mean high and mean low, with the calculation shown
- Pincher Creek change, both rates, and both °F → °C conversions
- Comparison of your week with the record
- Script or infographic

::: {.stretch-card}
**★★ Extended.** The reverse formula is °F = 9/5 × °C + 32. Is there a temperature where the °C number and the °F number are the same? Find it with an equation.
:::

*Math you will use: RN1, RN2, RN3, RN4, RN5*
:::

::: {.section-heading}
How it is assessed — all options
:::

Every option uses the same rubric: 4 criteria, 3 marks each, 12 marks. You get no extra marks for picking a harder option, because all three are built to be the same difficulty. **Criteria 1, 2 and 4** are marked on your team's work. **Criterion 3** is marked on *your own* explanation in your journal.

| Criterion | Full (3) | Partial (2) | Beginning (1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Model** — measurements, table or diagram, and the rule *(Skills & Procedures · RN1, PE1, SR3)* | Complete, labelled with units, and the rule matches the measured values | Present, with one error or missing label | Missing pieces, or the rule doesn't match the measurements |
| **2. Calculate and check** — estimate, calculate, check *(Skills & Procedures · RN2–RN5, PE3–PE7, SR1–SR2)* | Estimate shown first, calculation correct with every step shown, answer checked against the estimate and rounded sensibly | Correct method with a calculation error, or no estimate | Answer with no method, or the method doesn't fit the question |
| **3. Explain the meaning** — why the math works in this situation *(Understanding · PE2, SR2, RN2)* | Explains *why* (2^0^ = 1 layer, why the diagonal is irrational, why subtracting a negative adds), using the situation and units | Explains what was done but not why, or leaves out units | Restates the answer without explaining |
| **4. Communicate** — product for the audience *(Understanding · all)* | Audience could understand it without asking a question. Numbers support the claim. Revisions from feedback are visible | Clear answer, but support is thin, or feedback wasn't used | Answer is unclear or unsupported |
