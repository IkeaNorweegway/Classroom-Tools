## Moment 1 — Student loans

::: scenario
Maya plans a 4-year nursing degree. She'll need to borrow **$20,000** in total. In Alberta, a student loan comes in two parts: a **federal** part (from the Government of Canada) and a **provincial** part (from Alberta). The federal part is **interest-free**. The Alberta part charges interest at the **prime rate**.
:::

::: visual
<svg viewBox="0 0 720 250" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" role="img" aria-label="A 20,000 dollar student loan split into a 12,000 federal interest-free part and an 8,000 Alberta part with interest" font-family="inherit">

  <rect x="10" y="40" width="420" height="64" fill="#16a34a"/>

  <text x="220" y="90" font-size="13" fill="#fff" text-anchor="middle">0% interest</text>
  <text x="570" y="68" font-size="16" font-weight="700" fill="#fff" text-anchor="middle">Alberta part: $8,000</text>
  <text x="570" y="90" font-size="13" fill="#fff" text-anchor="middle">prime rate (sample 4.5%)</text>

  <text x="10" y="244" font-size="12" fill="#334155">The real split and prime rate vary. Check studentaid.alberta.ca.</text>
</svg>
:::

::: example
**How much does Maya repay in total?**

1. **Federal part:** $12,000, no interest. She repays exactly **$12,000**.
2. **Alberta part:** $8,000 at 4.5% over 10 years. Monthly payment **$82.91** (from a loan calculator). Total: 82.91 × 120 = **$9,949.29**, so **$1,949.29** is interest.
3. **Total repaid:** $12,000 + $9,949.29 = **$21,949.29**.
4. **Compare:** if the whole $20,000 were charged 4.5%, she'd pay **$4,873.22** in interest. The interest-free federal part saves her almost $3,000.
:::

Math: multiplying and comparing money; percent
:::

## Moment 2 — Return on investment

::: scenario
**Return on investment (ROI)** compares what a pathway costs with what it gives back over time. One simple way: how many years of higher pay does it take to "pay back" the true cost? For comparison, full-time work at Alberta's $15 minimum wage earns about **$31,200** a year.
:::

::: visual
<svg viewBox="0 0 720 270" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" role="img" aria-label="Payback comparison: registered nurse about 2.5 years, vet tech about 3 years, electrician apprenticeship well under one year" font-family="inherit">
  <text x="10" y="20" font-size="14" font-weight="700" fill="#1e293b">Years to pay back the true cost (simple estimate)</text>
  <line x1="200" y1="36" x2="200" y2="220" stroke="#1e293b" stroke-width="2"/>

    <rect x="200" y="52" width="250" height="28" fill="#94631a"/><text x="458" y="71" font-weight="700">about 2.5 years</text>
    <text x="10" y="126" font-weight="700">Vet tech</text><text x="10" y="142" font-size="11" fill="#334155">2-yr diploma · $47,812/yr</text>

:::

::: example
**How long does a registered nursing degree take to pay back?**

1. **True cost:** tuition about 4 × $7,293.60 = $29,174.40, plus lost earnings 4 × (8 months × $2,400) = $76,800. Total: **$105,974.40**.
2. **Extra pay each year:** average RN salary $73,823 − $31,200 = **$42,623** more than minimum wage.
3. **Payback:** $105,974.40 ÷ $42,623 ≈ **2.5 years**.
4. **But:** ROI also depends on **graduating**, **finding work** in the field, and **regional conditions** (Alberta may be short of nurses, which helps).
:::

**1.** A vet tech diploma costs $6,000 a year in tuition (sample) for 2 years, plus 2 years of lost earnings ($19,200 a year). What is the true cost?

**2.** Use the average vet tech salary ($47,812) and the minimum-wage baseline ($31,200). About how many years does it take to pay back your answer to Question 1?

(work 3)

**3.** Name **two** things that could make a pathway's real ROI worse than the estimate.

(work 3)

::: extra
**E1.** Why does the electrician apprenticeship pay back so quickly?

(work 2)

**E2.** Is ROI the only thing that matters when choosing a pathway? Explain.

## Moment 3 — Learning while you earn

::: scenario
**Work-integrated learning** mixes school with real work: co-op programs, internships and apprenticeships. In a **co-op**, students take turns between school terms and paid work terms. It builds human capital and lowers the cost of school.
:::

::: example
**How does a co-op change the true cost of a degree?**

1. **Without co-op:** true cost includes about $76,800 in lost earnings over 4 years.
2. **With co-op:** three paid terms earn about **$38,400** (sample wage).
3. **Effect:** that money pays tuition and living costs, so Maya borrows less.
4. **Bonus:** she graduates with **experience** and **references**, which helps her get hired.
:::

## Moment 4 — Getting in, and having a Plan B

::: scenario
Competitive programs often look at more than grades. Some ask for a **portfolio** (art, design), an **interview**, **references**, or **extracurricular** experience. Because seats are limited, a smart plan has a **primary pathway**, a **contingency** (backup), and a **rationale** (reasons).
:::

::: example
**Why is Maya's plan strong?**

1. **Primary pathway:** RN degree, matching her goal of working in health care.
2. **Strengthen the application:** volunteering at a care home shows commitment and gives her references.
3. **Contingency:** a practical nursing diploma keeps her in health care if she doesn't get a seat, and she can bridge to RN later.
4. **Rationale:** both pathways fit her interests, Alberta needs nurses, and the contingency costs less.
:::
