## Moment 1 — Debt and credit

::: scenario
**Debt** is money you borrowed and must pay back, usually with interest. **Credit** is the ability to borrow, or to buy now and pay later. At 18, most people start getting offers: credit cards, phone financing, "buy now, pay later," and loans.
:::

::: example
**Is debt a good or bad financial tool?**

1. **It depends on how it's used.** A student loan for a pathway that leads to good jobs can **speed up** financial security.
2. A credit card used for things you can't afford, and not paid off, can **undermine** it, because interest piles up.
3. **Ask before borrowing:** Do I need this? Can I pay it back on time? What will it cost in total?
4. **So:** debt is a tool. Used deliberately and responsibly, it helps; used carelessly, it hurts.
:::

## Moment 2 — The minimum-payment trap

::: scenario
Sam has a **$1,000** credit card balance at **20.99%** a year. The minimum payment is **2.5% of the balance or $10**, whichever is more. Sam stops using the card. How long to pay it off?
:::

::: example
**Why does paying the minimum take so long?**

1. **First month's interest:** $1,000 × 0.2099 ÷ 12 ≈ **$17.49**.
2. **First minimum payment:** 2.5% of the balance ≈ **$25.44**. Only about $8 actually pays down the debt.
3. **As the balance shrinks, so does the minimum,** so payments stay small for years.
4. **Result:** about **15 years** and **$1,614 in interest**, more than the original $1,000. Paying $100 a month instead takes **1 year** and costs **$109**.
:::

## Moment 3 — Your credit score

::: scenario
A **credit score** is a number from **300 to 900** that shows how you've handled borrowing. Lenders use it to decide whether to lend to you and what interest rate to charge. Landlords and some employers may check credit too.

::: example
**Alex has a $1,000 credit limit and owes $900. Why could this hurt Alex's score?**

1. **Credit utilization** = balance ÷ limit = 900 ÷ 1,000 = **90%**.
2. Using most of your limit looks risky to lenders, even if you pay on time.
3. **A better habit:** keep the balance low compared with the limit, and pay in full each month.
4. **Why it matters:** a strong credit score can mean lower interest on a car loan or mortgage later, saving thousands.
:::

## Moment 4 — Getting out of debt, and avoiding traps

::: scenario
Jordan owes money on three things and can pay **$400 a month** in total. Should Jordan pay off the **highest interest first** (the **avalanche** method) or the **smallest balance first** (the **snowball** method)?
:::

::: example
**Avalanche or snowball for Jordan?**

1. **Both:** pay every minimum, then put all extra money on **one** debt until it's gone, then move to the next.
2. **Avalanche** (credit card first, the highest rate): **17 months, $509** in interest. It costs the least.
3. **Snowball** (phone first, the smallest balance): **17 months, $566**. A quick win can keep people motivated.
4. **Minimums only:** **46 months, $1,712**. Either plan saves over $1,100.
:::

**Two more tools:**
- **Payday loans** are small, short loans with very high costs. In Alberta they can cost up to **$14 per $100** borrowed for about two weeks. Borrowing $300 costs $42, about **364%** a year. (Most other loans in Canada can't legally charge more than **35%** a year.)
- **Debt-to-income ratio** = monthly debt payments ÷ monthly gross income. It shows how much of your pay goes to debt. Lenders look at it.

**1.** Jordan's minimums total $170 a month. Jordan's gross income is $3,000 a month. What is Jordan's debt-to-income ratio?
