# Planet Earth — Practice Workbook Answer Key
Grade 7 Science · Teacher copy. Matches the published workbook (12 questions + 3 stretch). Model answers; accept any answer that shows the same science in the student's own words.

## Q1
**(a)**
- **Metamorphic → Igneous:** **melting, then cooling (solidifying).** The rock must melt completely into magma, then cool and harden into new igneous rock.
- **Igneous → Sedimentary:** **weathering, erosion, deposition, then compaction and cementation.** The rock is broken into pieces, carried away and laid down in layers, then the layers are squeezed and glued together.
- **Igneous → Metamorphic:** **heat and pressure.** Deep underground, the rock is heated and squeezed until its minerals change, without melting.

**(b)** Accept any accurate examples:
- **Igneous:** granite or other crystalline rock in the Canadian Shield, northeastern Alberta (near Fort Chipewyan); granite boulders (glacial erratics) such as the Okotoks Big Rock.
- **Sedimentary:** sandstone and shale in the Drumheller Badlands; limestone in the Rocky Mountains (Banff).
- **Metamorphic:** gneiss in the Canadian Shield near Fort Chipewyan; quartzite (often as cobbles in Rocky Mountain rivers).

**(c)** The claim is **wrong**. Rock-cycle changes take **millions to hundreds of millions of years**, not 100 years. Some rocks stay unchanged for billions of years, and rocks do not follow one fixed path around the cycle. A diagram shows the *order* of possible changes, but it has no time axis and cannot show how slow each step is.

## Q2
**(a)**
1. **Crust:** solid rock; thin (about 5–70 km); the coolest layer.
2. **Mantle:** hot, mostly solid rock that flows very slowly (convection); the thickest layer.
3. **Outer core:** **liquid** iron and nickel; about 4,000–5,000°C.
4. **Inner core:** **solid** iron and nickel; about 5,000–6,000°C.

**(b)** **Seismic waves** from earthquakes. Waves change speed and bend when they pass into different materials, and some waves (S-waves) cannot travel through liquid. By timing when waves arrive at stations around the world, scientists work out where the layers are and whether they are solid or liquid.

**(c)** The inner core is under such enormous **pressure** that its particles are squeezed together and cannot spread apart to become a liquid, even at that temperature. The outer core is under less pressure, so it melts. Inside Earth, **both temperature and pressure** decide the state of matter, not temperature alone.

## Q3
**(a)** The **marine shale** at the bottom (about 75 Ma) is the oldest. In undisturbed layers, the lowest layer was laid down first: this is the **law of superposition**.

**(b)** Central Alberta was covered by a **shallow inland sea** (the Western Interior Seaway). Expected fossils include **ammonites** (shown), plus marine reptiles such as **mosasaurs or plesiosaurs**, fish, clams, and other shelled sea animals, because the shale formed from mud on a sea floor.

**(c)** A **huge asteroid impact** (or massive volcanic eruptions) threw dust and ash into the atmosphere, which settled worldwide. Because it was laid down **at the same moment everywhere**, the layer is a **time marker**: geologists anywhere can match it and know that rock above it is younger than about 65 Ma and rock below it is older.

## Q4
**(a)**
- **Panel A:** **Divergent.** Example: Mid-Atlantic Ridge or Iceland. Feature: mid-ocean ridge, rift valley, new crust.
- **Panel B:** **Convergent (subduction).** Example: Japan, the Andes, or Cascadia (BC coast). Features: ocean trench, volcanoes, mountains.
- **Panel C:** **Transform.** Example: San Andreas Fault, California. Feature: fault line with earthquakes.

**(b)** At a subduction boundary, one plate **sinks into the hot mantle**. The sinking plate releases water, which helps the rock above it **melt into magma** that rises to form volcanoes, while the grinding plates cause earthquakes. At a transform boundary the plates only **slide past** each other: they grind and get stuck, causing earthquakes, but no rock sinks or melts, so little magma forms.

## Q5
**(a)** The region was once covered by a **warm, shallow sea** full of shelled organisms. Their shells and skeletons piled up on the sea floor and hardened into limestone. This happened hundreds of millions of years ago (mainly in the **Paleozoic Era**, before the dinosaurs).

**(b)** The igneous and metamorphic basement is **much older** (Precambrian). It formed first, deep underground, and was later worn down. Then, over millions of years, **sediments were laid down on top** of it by seas and rivers and hardened into rock. The order shows a long history: ancient rock first, followed by long periods of erosion and then deposition.

**(c)** The slightly acidic water **dissolves** the limestone (calcium carbonate). This is **chemical weathering**. Over thousands of years it can carve **caves**, sinkholes, and underground channels (karst), and leave mineral deposits such as stalactites. Banff's caves and springs are examples.

## Q6
**(a)**
- **Drumheller hoodoos:** differential erosion by wind and water. Soft rock wears away faster than the hard cap rock.
- **Flat Alberta prairies:** glacial deposition. Melting ice sheets spread a smooth blanket of till.
- **Bow River valley near Banff:** river (fluvial) erosion, widened by glaciers. The river cut the valley, and its gravel and terraces show the flowing water. (Accept glacial carving with reasoning; the valley's U-shape shows glaciers.)
- **Rocky Mountain peaks:** tectonic uplift + glacial carving. Thrust faulting pushed rock up; glaciers carved sharp peaks and cirques.

**(b)**
1. Rain, frost and temperature changes crack the rock (**mechanical weathering**); some minerals dissolve (**chemical weathering**).
2. Water running down cracks and wind carry away the loose pieces (**erosion**).
3. Where a hard cap rock sits on top, it shields the softer rock below, while the soft rock around it wears away faster.
4. Over thousands of years, a pillar is left standing under its cap.
5. The eroded sediment is laid down in low areas and river valleys (**deposition**).

## Q7
**(a)** **False** (or at best partly true). Earthquakes and volcanoes are **not random**; most happen along **plate boundaries**. Examples: **Japan**, where the Pacific Plate subducts under the Eurasian Plate, causing earthquakes and explosive volcanoes; **Iceland**, on the divergent Mid-Atlantic Ridge, with eruptions as plates pull apart; **California**, on the San Andreas transform fault, with earthquakes.

**(b)** News covers events that are dramatic or affect many people, from all over the world. That makes them seem to happen everywhere. Most activity happens in belts along plate edges, especially the **"Ring of Fire"** around the Pacific Ocean.

**(c)** Alberta sits in the **stable interior of the North American Plate**, far from any active plate boundary. But the landscape shows the past was different: the **Rocky Mountains** were pushed up by plate collision, the mountains are full of **folded and faulted rock layers**, and Alberta has old volcanic ash layers.

## Q8
**(a)**
1. Ammonites died and sank to the sea floor; their shells were buried by sediment (**deposition**).
2. The sediment hardened into limestone (**compaction and cementation / lithification**), preserving the fossils.
3. Plate collision pushed the rock layers up along thrust faults (**tectonic uplift / mountain building**), lifting them thousands of metres.
4. **Erosion** and weathering wore away the layers above and exposed the fossil on the mountainside.

**(b)** Fossils form in **sedimentary rock**, because the organism is buried gently in sediment. **Granite** (igneous) forms from melted rock, which would destroy any fossil, so no. **Marble** (metamorphic) forms under heat and pressure that usually crush or destroy fossils, so almost never.

**(c)** A good index fossil must: have lived for a **short period** of geological time; have been **widespread** (found in many places around the world); and be **common and easy to recognize**. **Ammonites qualify**: each species lived for only a short time, they spread through oceans worldwide, and their spiral shells are easy to identify.

## Q9
**(a)** Any two:
- **Weathering:** frost wedging and chemical breakdown crack and weaken the rock.
- **Erosion by glaciers:** ice ground down and carved the mountains, carrying huge amounts of rock away.
- **Erosion by rivers:** flowing water carried sediment downhill for millions of years.

Weathering breaks rock down where it sits; erosion moves the broken material away.

**(b)** Look for **thick deposits of sediment** (gravel, sand, conglomerate) worn off the mountains, in the **foothills and plains east of the Rockies**. That huge volume of eroded rock had to come from mountains that were once much taller.

**(c)** "Mountains stop growing when **the tectonic forces pushing them up stop (the plates stop colliding)** but continue to shrink because **weathering and erosion never stop wearing them down**." A mountain's height depends on the balance between uplift and erosion; when uplift ends, erosion wins over time.

## Q10
**(a)** **Iceland:** magma rises straight from the mantle as plates pull apart. It is **basaltic**: low in silica, hot, and runny, so gas escapes easily and eruptions are gentle with flowing lava. **Japan:** magma forms where the subducting plate sinks and releases water, melting rock that then mixes with continental crust as it rises. It is **higher in silica**, thick and sticky, and traps gas, so pressure builds and eruptions are **explosive**.

**(b)** The causes are **different**. In Iceland, plates **pull apart**: the crust cracks and magma moves, causing many small earthquakes. In Japan, plates **collide**: the subducting plate sticks and then slips suddenly, releasing large amounts of energy in powerful (often deep) earthquakes.

**(c)** **False.** Iceland's runny eruptions are usually less deadly than Japan's explosive ones. Scientists would look at: the **eruption style** and type of magma; the **number of people living nearby**; how often it erupts and **how long since its last eruption**; and hazards such as ash, mudflows (lahars), and pyroclastic flows.

**(b)** Accept any well-reasoned choice. Example: **F (oil sands)**. Heat and pressure over millions of years turned buried organic material into bitumen, a non-renewable resource that formed only under special geological conditions. It drives Alberta's economy, and extracting it affects land, water and greenhouse gas emissions.

## S1
The mechanism is **convection currents in the mantle**. Hot mantle rock slowly rises, spreads sideways under the plates and sinks as it cools, dragging the plates along (plus the pull of sinking plates at subduction zones).

The rock cycle and plate tectonics depend on each other. Plate movement **pushes rock deep down** (subduction), where heat and pressure make **metamorphic** rock or melt it into **igneous** rock. Collisions **uplift** mountains, which are then weathered into **sediment**. In turn, the rocks formed tell geologists how plates moved.

Alberta examples: the Rockies' **uplifted limestone** with marine fossils (sedimentary rock lifted by plate collision); the **Precambrian igneous/metamorphic basement** formed in ancient mountain-building; the **Badlands' sediments**, eroded from the rising Rockies.

## S2
New crust forms at a mid-ocean ridge and **records the direction of Earth's magnetic field** as it cools. As the plates spread apart, that rock moves away from the ridge and newer rock forms in the middle. When the field flips, the new rock records the opposite direction. The result is **matching, mirror-image stripes on both sides of the ridge**. The mirror pattern proves the sea floor spreads outward, and the alternating stripes prove the field has reversed many times.

The ocean floor is a better record because it forms in a **steady, ordered way** at ridges, while continental rock is folded, eroded and changed many times. **Oceanic crust is much younger** (mostly under 200 million years old) than continental crust (up to about 4 billion years old), because oceanic crust is **constantly destroyed at subduction zones** while lighter continental crust stays on the surface.
