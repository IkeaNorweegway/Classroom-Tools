# Structures and Forces — Practice Workbook Answer Key
Grade 7 Science · Teacher copy. Matches the published workbook (12 questions + 3 stretch). Model answers; accept any answer that shows the same science in the student's own words.

## Q1
**(a)** **Top surface: compression.** As the beam bends down, the top is squeezed and shortened. **Bottom surface: tension.** The bottom is stretched and lengthened.

**(b)** In a bent beam, the internal forces are **greatest at the top and bottom edges**, and close to zero along the middle. An I-beam puts most of its material in the **top and bottom flanges**, where it is needed, and uses only a thin web in the middle. It is nearly as strong as a solid beam but uses far less material and weighs less.

## Q2
**(a)**
- **Panel 1:** **Solid.** Alberta example: a concrete dam (Bighorn Dam), a stone wall, or a hoodoo. Main force: **compression**.
- **Panel 2:** **Frame.** Example: a bridge truss, a transmission tower, or the frame of a house. Main forces: **tension and compression** in the members.
- **Panel 3:** **Shell.** Example: a domed roof, a grain bin roof, or an igloo. Main force: **compression**, spread over the surface.
- **Panel 4:** **Combination.** Example: a house (frame walls plus a sheathed roof), or the Saddledome. Main forces: **tension and compression**.

**(b)** **Frame:** the triangles share the load. A load at the top is split into **compression** in the sloping members and **tension** in the bottom chord, so every member carries part of it. **Shell:** the curved surface spreads the load **across the whole shell** as compression, down to the base, so no single point carries it all.

## Q3
**(a)** A **live load**. The truck is a temporary, movable weight that comes and goes. A **dead load** is the permanent weight of the structure itself (concrete, steel, fixed parts).

**(b)** Wind is a **dynamic load**. It changes quickly in size and direction and hits suddenly (gusts), which can make the structure shake or sway and add extra force. Another example for an Edmonton parking structure: **cars braking or accelerating**, a heavy **snow slide**, or a minor **earthquake**. (Accept vibration from traffic.)

**(c)** Compression is highest at the **bottom of the column**, which carries the truck plus the whole weight of the column above it. A perfectly vertical load is pure compression. If the column bends a little (from an off-centre load or the wind), one side is in **tension**. Fix: **reinforced concrete with steel rebar**. The steel carries the tension and the concrete carries the compression.

## Q4
**(a)** The heavy grain fills the **bottom** of the silo, which **lowers its centre of gravity**. A lower centre of gravity makes an object more stable and harder to tip over, even though it weighs more.

**(b)** The outward push creates **tension** in the round walls: the grain stretches the wall around its circumference, like a hoop. The pressure is **greatest at the base**, because the grain there is under the weight of all the grain above it. So the base walls must be **thickest** to resist the largest force.

**(c)** An **empty silo topples more easily**. It is light, and its centre of gravity is high in the middle of a tall, narrow shape. A sideways push can move the centre of gravity outside its **base of support**. A full silo has a lower centre of gravity and much more weight holding it down, so the same wind cannot tip it.

## Q5
**(a)** Effort × effort arm = Load × load arm. 30 N × 2 m = Load × 0.5 m, so 60 = Load × 0.5, and Load = 60 ÷ 0.5 = **120 N**.

**(b)** MA = load ÷ effort = 120 ÷ 30 = **4**. (Or effort arm ÷ load arm = 2 ÷ 0.5 = 4.) The lever **multiplies the person's force by 4**.

**(c)** **Not correct.** It does not save energy (work). Work = force × distance. The effort is 4 times smaller, but the person must push **4 times farther** (2 m of movement for every 0.5 m the load moves). The force needed is less; the **work stays the same** (or slightly more, because of friction).

## Q6
**(a)**
- **Main cables:** force **tension**; property **tensile strength**. They hold up the deck by being pulled.
- **Bridge deck:** force **compression and bending** (compression on top, tension underneath); property **stiffness / compressive strength**. It must carry traffic without sagging or cracking.
- **Anchor blocks:** force **compression** (resisting the cables' pull); property **mass / compressive strength**. Their weight holds the cables down against the pull.

**(b)** A heavier deck keeps the cables **tight (in steady tension)** and makes the bridge **more stable**: it is less likely to sway, bounce or twist in wind and under moving traffic (dynamic loads). The extra mass dampens movement.

## Q7
**(a)** **Frame part:** the network of **triangular struts** (beams) joined in a sphere. **Shell part:** the **panels** covering the frame, forming a continuous curved surface. Loads on any panel pass into the triangle struts, then spread around the curved shape as compression, down to the ring foundation.

**(b)**
- **Advantage:** the load is shared by every strut and spread over the whole curve, so the dome is **strong and very light for its size**. It spans a large open space with no inside columns and stands up well to wind and snow.
- **Downside:** it is **hard to build and fit with flat things** (doors, floors, shelves, exhibits). It has many joints that can leak, and curved space can be wasted.
- **For a science centre:** the **dome** is a good choice: a large open space with no columns suits exhibits and a dome theatre, and the striking shape attracts visitors. Accept the box if well argued (easier to fit floors and rooms).

**(c)** A triangle is **rigid**: its shape cannot change unless one of its sides changes length. A sideways force on a triangle is taken by its members as tension or compression, and it holds its shape. A square with the same joints **folds into a parallelogram** (racks) under a sideways force, because its angles can change without any side changing length.

## Q8
**(a)**
- **Dead:** the weight of the bridge itself (deck, beams, railings).
- **Live:** up to 20 hikers, their packs, and **snow piled on the deck**.
- **Dynamic:** hikers walking in step or bouncing; **wind gusts**; **floodwater and debris** striking the supports; snow sliding off.

Dynamic loads are tricky in the mountains because they are **sudden and hard to predict**: spring floods carry logs and ice, wind funnels through valleys, and snow conditions change quickly.

**(b)** Example: a **frame** (truss) bridge, or a frame with a simple deck (combination).
- **Structural:** a truss spans 8 m strongly; its triangles share the loads and stay rigid.
- **Material:** it can be built from **wood or light steel members**, which are strong for their weight.
- **Transport:** a truss is made of **many short, light pieces** (each under 80 kg) that can be carried in by pack horse and bolted together on site. A solid or shell bridge would need large, heavy pieces or poured concrete.

**(c)** Example: **floodwater scouring away the supports** or undermining the footings. Feature: build the abutments **high above the flood level** on rock, with no supports in the creek bed, so floodwater and debris pass underneath. Accept other failure modes with a matching fix (for example, joints loosening, fixed with cross-bracing and bolted joints that are checked regularly).

## Q9
**(a)**
- **A — Overpass:**
  - Weight of the concrete deck and beams (D).
  - Cars and trucks crossing (L).
  - Braking trucks, or wind (Dy).
- **B — Avalanche shed:**
  - Weight of the concrete roof and walls (D).
  - Snow piled on the roof (L).
  - **Avalanche impact** (Dy).

**(b)** Being temporary does not make a load "live". An avalanche hits **suddenly and with great speed**, slamming into the roof and creating a force much larger than the snow's resting weight. That sudden impact makes it **dynamic**. The engineer must design for the **impact force**, not just the weight of the snow, or the shed could collapse.

## Q10
**(a)** **Work = force × distance.** Mechanical advantage changes the **force** you need (it can make it smaller) by making you move a **greater distance**. It cannot change the total work: the work you put in is at least the work done on the load.

**(b)**
1. Load force = 25 N × 4 = **100 N**.
2. Distance the load moves = 2 m ÷ 4 = **0.5 m**.
3. Work done by you = 25 N × 2 m = **50 J**.
4. Work done on the load = 100 N × 0.5 m = **50 J**.

The work in equals the work out. The lever **traded force for distance**; it did not reduce the work.

**(c)**
- **Multiply force:** you can lift or move something too heavy to lift directly (a car jack, a crowbar).
- **Change the direction of the force**, or **increase speed or distance:** a fixed pulley lets you pull down to lift up; a 3rd-class lever such as a fishing rod or broom makes the end move farther and faster.

## Q11
**(a)** MA = number of supporting rope segments = **4**. Effort = 600 N ÷ 4 = **150 N**.

**(b)** **2 m.** With 4 rope segments holding the load, raising the load 1 m means **each** of the 4 segments must shorten by 1 m. The rigger has to pull out 4 m of rope for every 1 m the load rises, so 8 m of rope ÷ 4 = 2 m.

**(c)** **Friction** in the pulleys (or the weight of the movable pulley and rope). Some of the rigger's work is turned into **heat** in the pulley wheels instead of lifting the load. The energy is not destroyed: it becomes **heat and sound**, so total energy is still conserved. Less of it is useful, so efficiency is below 100%.

## Q12
**(a)**

| Property | Pine timber | Steel hollow tube | Cast iron bar |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strength under compression | Medium | High | High |
| Flexibility / ductility | Medium | High | Low |
| Mass (heaviness per unit volume) | Low | Medium | High |
| Suitability for wet outdoor conditions | Low (rots unless treated) | Medium–High (if galvanized or painted) | Low–Medium (rusts) |

**(b)** A brittle material **snaps suddenly with no warning** when it is overloaded, especially under tension or impact. Dynamic loads (a hiker falling against the rail, an impact, sudden gusts) can do exactly that. **Steel is ductile**: it **bends and stretches first**, absorbing energy and giving a visible warning before failure. Cast iron gives no warning and can break all at once.

**(c)** Example: **steel hollow tube.** It has **high strength** and is **ductile**, so it bends rather than snaps under dynamic loads. Its **mass is low for its strength** (it is hollow), so each post is well under the **80 kg pack-horse limit**. With galvanizing or paint, it stands up to **wet outdoor conditions**. Pine is light but weaker and rots; cast iron is brittle, rusts, and is very heavy.

## S1
**(a)** A **frame** (steel trestle) structure. A solid structure 96 m tall across a wide valley would need an enormous amount of material and would be far too heavy. A shell cannot span that far and that high. An open **steel frame of triangles** is strong, rigid and light, lets wind pass through, and can be built from many separate pieces.

**(b)** Any four, including at least one dynamic load:
- **Dead load:** the weight of the steel structure and tracks.
- **Live load:** fully loaded freight trains.
- **Dynamic load:** the train's **vibration and moving force**, and braking and accelerating trains.
- **Dynamic load:** strong **wind gusts** across the open valley (Lethbridge is very windy).
- **Thermal expansion and contraction** from temperature swings, including Chinooks.
- **Snow and ice.**

**(c)** Properties: **tensile and compressive strength**; **ductility / toughness**; **resistance to fatigue** (repeated loading); **corrosion resistance**. **Tension failure:** the member stretches, **necks (thins)** and pulls apart; steel usually shows warning signs first. **Compression failure:** the member **buckles**: it bends sideways or crumples, often suddenly, which can drop the track.

## S2
**(a)** MA (wheel and axle) = wheel radius ÷ axle radius = 0.4 ÷ 0.05 = **8**.

**(b)** MA (pulley) = number of supporting segments = **3**.

**(c)** Overall MA = 8 × 3 = **24**.

**(d)** Load = 20 N × 24 = **480 N**.

**(e)** It actually lifts 0.85 × 480 = **408 N**. The "missing" energy was changed into **heat (and sound) by friction** in the axle, the pulleys and the rope. Energy is conserved: work in = useful work out + energy lost to friction. None of it disappears.

## S3
**(a)** **Correct:** a dome's curved shape spreads loads along its surface as **compression**, down to the base. Concrete is very strong in compression, so it suits a dome.

**(b)** Errors or overstatements (any two):
- Not **all** loads become pure compression. The base of a dome pushes **outward**, creating **tension in the foundation ring** (the "hoop" at the bottom), and wind, uneven snow or point loads cause bending and tension in parts of the shell.
- Concrete is **weak in tension**, so it is not "ideal for all domes" without reinforcement.
- Concrete is very **heavy**, so very large domes carry a huge dead load, and it is a poor choice where weight or transport matters.

**(c)** An **earthquake**, or very strong **uneven loads** (heavy snow drifting on one side, a point load). The shaking or uneven loading creates tension and bending in the shell. Unreinforced concrete **cracks** and can collapse suddenly. The bigger the dome, the bigger the forces from its own weight.

**(d)** Fix: add **steel reinforcement**: rebar mesh in the shell and a **steel tension ring** around the base. The steel carries the tension the concrete cannot, so the dome resists outward thrust, uneven loads and shaking.
