## 1 · What makes a tall tale?

**Learning target:** I can tell what makes a story a tall tale and explain why people told them. *(TF7, TF3, OL2)*

::: wordbank
a little bigger
bacon on their boots
impossible
lakes of Minnesota
north woods
pine tree
true
words froze in the air
:::

A **tall tale** is a story about a hero whose body or abilities are [[impossible / far beyond real life]]. The teller tells it with a straight face, as if it were [[true]].

| Feature of a tall tale | Example from *Paul Bunyan* (Tale 1) |
| --- | --- |
| A hero with amazing size, strength or skill | Paul combs his beard with a [[pine tree]] (¶2) |
| **Exaggeration** (stretching the truth on purpose) | It was so cold the [[words froze in the air]] (¶3) |
| A real place, job or time | Logging camps in the [[north woods]] |
| A problem solved in an amazing way | Boys skate on the griddle with [[bacon on their boots]] (¶5) |
| Often explains how something in nature came to be | Babe's hoofprints became the [[lakes of Minnesota]] (¶8) |

**Why were tall tales told?** Workers on the frontier, in logging camps and on rivers told them **out loud** to pass long nights, to laugh at hard jobs, and to show pride in their work. Each teller made the hero [[a little bigger]], so the tales grew.

::: myth
WRONG: "A tall tale is just a lie."
WHY: Tall tales say impossible things as if they were true.
RIGHT: A tall tale is a **genre**. Everyone knows it is exaggerated. The fun is seeing how far the teller can stretch it.
:::

**Quick check.** Is "The Three Little Pigs" a tall tale? Explain using one feature from the table.

::: frame
No / Yes, it is not a tall tale because ___ .
:::

(lines 2)

=> No. The pigs are not a legendary hero with impossible human abilities, and the story is not told as if it were true. (Other answers can work if they use a feature.)

## 2 · How a story is built

**Learning target:** I can name the parts of a story and find them in a tale. *(TF3, WR14)*

::: wordbank
feels
setting
solved
starving
wants something
:::

Most stories, including tall tales, follow the same path:

| Part | What it does | In *Paul Bunyan* |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Beginning** | Introduces the main character and the [[setting]] (where and when) | Paul is born; he becomes the greatest logger (¶1–2) |
| **Problem** | Something goes wrong or the character [[wants something]] | The Winter of the Blue Snow: it is so cold, and the loggers are [[starving]] (¶3, ¶5) |
| **Events** | Many steps, with lots of details, as the character tries to fix it | Paul finds Babe; builds the giant griddle; the loggers get back to work (¶4–6) |
| **Resolution** | The problem is [[solved]] | The loggers are fed and cut more wood than ever (¶6) |
| **Ending** | How things end up, and how the character [[feels]] or what changes | Spring comes and the frozen words thaw (¶7–8) |

::: example Watch me find the problem
I ask: *What goes wrong?* In paragraph 3 everything freezes, but the cold alone is not the problem Paul has to fix. In paragraph 5 the author says "the camp had a new problem: the loggers were starving." The word **problem** is a signal. That is what Paul solves.
:::

::: myth
WRONG: "The problem is the first bad thing that happens."
WHY: Stories often have small troubles before the main one.
RIGHT: The main problem is the one the **resolution** fixes. Check: does the ending solve it?
:::

**Quick check.** In *Chinook Charlie* (Tale 5), what is the problem, and what is the resolution?

::: frame
The problem is ___ . The resolution is ___ .
:::

(lines 3)

=> Problem: the Great Freeze; the river is frozen, the cattle can't drink and the town is stuck (¶3). Resolution: Charlie blows a giant warm wind from the mountains and everything thaws (¶5–6).

## 3 · Getting to know a character

**Learning target:** I can describe a character using what they say, think and do, and what others say about them. *(TF10, CO11, CO9)*

::: wordbank
brave
calm
change
determined
surprising
:::

Authors don't just tell us what a character is like. They **show** us. We find out in four ways:

| Way | Question to ask | *Sally Ann* (Tale 2) | So she is… |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What they **say** | What do their words show? | "Determined wins every time." (¶8) | [[determined / confident]] |
| What they **think** | What is on their mind? | She thinks the bear is "only hungry" (¶8) | [[calm / clever]] |
| What they **do** | What do their actions show? | Walks straight out to the bear when everyone hides (¶6) | [[brave]] |
| What **others** say or think | How do other characters see them? | Davy: "the bravest, strongest, most surprising person" (¶5) | [[surprising / strong]] |

A trait is a word for what a character is like **inside** (brave, stubborn, kind), not what they look like (tall, strong).

**Tall-tale heroes are usually flat characters:** they stay the same from start to finish. A **round** character is more layered and can [[change]] during the story.

::: myth
WRONG: "Sally Ann is strong, so her trait is *strong*."
WHY: Body and abilities are easy to see.
RIGHT: Look for what her actions show about her **inside**: she is brave, confident, quick-thinking.
:::

**Quick check.** Write one trait for John Henry (Tale 3) and the evidence that shows it. Name the way (say, think, do, others).

::: frame
John Henry is ___ . I know because he ___ (paragraph ___). This is what he ___ (says / does).
:::

(lines 3)

=> Example: John Henry is determined. He keeps swinging without stopping for water when the machine is ahead (¶6, *do*). Or: proud/courageous, "before I let that steam drill beat me down, I'll die with this hammer in my hand" (¶5, *say*).

## 4 · Exaggeration and comparisons

**Learning target:** I can explain what an exaggeration or comparison means and why the author used it. *(VO13, VO14, TF15)*

::: wordbank
bright and warm
colder than anyone could imagine
very fast and powerfully
:::

| Type | What it is | Example | What it really means |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Hyperbole** | A huge exaggeration that is not meant to be taken literally | "It was so cold that the flames froze solid." | It was [[colder than anyone could imagine]] |
| **Simile** | Compares two things using **like** or **as** | "The hammer whistled through the air **like** the wind before a storm." | The hammer moved [[very fast and powerfully]] |
| **Metaphor** | Compares two things by saying one **is** the other | "Sally Ann's smile was a lantern at midnight." *(our example)* | Her smile was [[bright and warm]] |

::: example Watch me explain it
"Charlie's chest swelled up like a hot-air balloon" (Tale 5, ¶5).
1. **What two things are compared?** Charlie's chest and a hot-air balloon.
2. **What do they have in common?** Both get huge and round when they fill with air.
3. **Why did the author choose it?** It helps us *picture* how giant his breath was, and it is funny.
:::

**The question that matters** is never just "Is it a simile?" It is: *What does it make you picture, and why did the author choose it?*

**Quick check.** "When Big Joe sneezed, the leaves blew off the trees in the next county." What type is it? What does it show about Joe?

::: frame
It is a ___ (hyperbole / simile / metaphor). It shows that Joe ___ .
:::

(lines 3)

=> Hyperbole. It shows Joe is impossibly big and powerful, and it explains, as a joke, why autumn comes early in the Valley.

## 5 · Summarizing a story

**Learning target:** I can write a short summary of a story in my own words. *(CO4)*

::: wordbank
died
my own words
small details
won
:::

A **summary** tells the most important parts of a story in a few sentences. It leaves out [[small details]] and uses [[my own words]].

**Step 1: the gist.** One sentence, 20 words or fewer: who + what happens + how it ends.

**Step 2: Somebody · Wanted · But · So · Then**

| Somebody | Wanted | But | So | Then |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| John Henry | wanted to keep the steel drivers' jobs | a steam drill could do the work of ten men | he raced the machine and [[won]] | he [[died]], and people still sing about him |

**Summary rules:**
- Delete small details and repeats (the twenty-pound hammer, the sparks).
- Replace lists with one word ("cattle can't drink, bus stuck, hockey cancelled" → "the whole town was stuck").
- Keep the order of events.

::: example Watch me
**Gist (19 words):** John Henry raced a steam drill to save the workers' jobs; he won, but died from the effort.
:::

::: myth
WRONG: "A summary retells everything that happened."
WHY: Every part feels important while you read.
RIGHT: A summary keeps only what someone needs to understand the story. If it is longer than the story's best paragraph, cut.
:::

**Quick check.** Write a gist sentence (20 words or fewer) for *Big Joe Mufferaw*.

::: frame
___ (who) ___ (what happens), and in the end ___ .
:::

(lines 2)

=> Example: A real raftsman named Joseph Montferrand became the legend Big Joe Mufferaw as people kept telling bigger stories about him.

## 6 · Reading between the lines

**Learning target:** I can make an inference and back it up with evidence from the text. *(CO6, CO9, CO5)*

::: wordbank
dangerous
worried
:::

An **inference** is something the author does not say straight out, but I can figure out.

**Clue in the text + what I already know = inference**

| Clue in the text | What I know | Inference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "The workers looked at each other. If the machine took their jobs, how would they feed their families?" (Tale 3, ¶5) | People need jobs to earn money for food | The workers are [[worried / scared]] about the steam drill |
| Everyone hides in their cabins when the bear comes (Tale 2, ¶6) | People hide when they are afraid | The settlers think the bear is [[dangerous]], but Sally Ann does not |

**Answering with evidence:**

::: frame
I think ___ . In paragraph ___, the text says " ___ ." This shows ___ because ___ .
:::

::: myth
WRONG: "If it isn't written in the story, I can't know it."
WHY: Some answers are stated; others are implied.
RIGHT: Good readers use clues and what they know to work out what the author **implies**, then point to the clue.
:::

**Quick check.** How does Paul feel about Babe when he finds her? Use the frame.

::: frame
I think Paul ___ Babe. In paragraph 4, the text says "___." This shows ___ .
:::

(lines 3)

=> I think Paul cares about Babe. In paragraph 4, he "tucked the calf inside his coat and carried it back to camp." This shows he wants to keep her warm and safe, even though he is busy.

## 7 · Planning a story: POW + WWW

**Learning target:** I can use POW + WWW to plan a tall tale with all its parts. *(WR3, WR14, WR12)*

::: wordbank
blows a warm wind
Chinooks
end the Great Freeze
Southern Alberta
:::

::: strategy POW: what good writers do
**P** **Pick** my idea
**O** **Organize** my notes (use WWW, What = 2, How = 2)
**W** **Write** and say more
:::

::: strategy WWW, What = 2, How = 2: the story parts
**W** **Who** is the main character? Who else is in the story?
**W** **When** does the story take place?
**W** **Where** does the story take place?
**What** **What** does the main character want to do?
**What** **What** happens then? What happens with the other characters?
**How** **How** does the story end?
**How** **How** does the main character feel? How do the other characters feel?
:::

| Part | *Chinook Charlie* (Tale 5) |
| --- | --- |
| Who | Chinook Charlie, a ranch hand with warm breath; Mrs. Okafor, the mayor |
| When | The Great Freeze, long ago, in January |
| Where | [[Southern Alberta]] and the Rocky Mountains |
| What does he want? | To [[end the Great Freeze]] |
| What happens? | He walks to the mountains, takes a giant breath, and [[blows a warm wind]] |
| How does it end? | Everything thaws; warm winter winds are now called [[Chinooks]] |
| How do they feel? | The town is joyful (dancing in T-shirts); Charlie is worn out |

**Self-talk while I write:** "Do I have all seven parts?" · "What's my next part?" · "I'm stuck, so I'll look at my notes." · "Good, that part is done."

**Quick check.** From memory, write what each letter in POW stands for.

::: frame
P = ___ · O = ___ · W = ___
:::

(lines 2)

=> Pick my idea, Organize my notes, Write and say more.

## 8 · Show, don't tell

**Learning target:** I can show a feeling or a fact through details instead of telling it. *(WR15, WR13, WR11)*

::: wordbank
see, hear or feel
The townspeople are freezing cold.
:::

**Telling** names it: *It was very cold.* · *Charlie was tired.*
**Showing** lets the reader [[see, hear or feel]] it: *It was so cold that people had to chip their shadows off the sidewalk with a spoon.* · *Charlie slept for a week on top of the mountain, snoring softly.*

::: model Tale 5, paragraph 3
"Their teeth were chattering so hard that they sounded like a hundred typewriters."
:::

What is the author **showing** here instead of telling? [[The townspeople are freezing cold.]]

**Ways to show:**
- an **action** (teeth chattering, stamping boots)
- a **sound** or **sense** detail (boots crunched like cereal)
- **dialogue** (what someone says)
- an **exaggeration** that proves the point (cows giving ice cream)

::: myth
WRONG: "Showing means adding more adjectives: *very very very cold*."
WHY: Extra describing words feel like more detail.
RIGHT: Show with something that *happens*, a sense detail or a comparison, so the reader works it out.
:::

**Quick check.** Turn this telling sentence into showing: *Sally Ann was brave.*

::: frame
When ___ , Sally Ann ___ .
:::

(lines 3)

=> Example: When the grizzly roared and everyone ran for their cabins, Sally Ann walked straight out into the snow and smiled at it.

## 9 · Writing dialogue

**Learning target:** I can punctuate dialogue so the reader knows who is speaking. *(CV2, CV3, WR16)*

::: wordbank
inside
new paragraph
:::

::: model Tale 2, paragraph 7–8
"How did you do it?" Davy asked her.

"That bear was only hungry," Sally Ann said. "I was hungry *and* determined."
:::

**Dialogue rules:**
1. Put **quotation marks** around the exact words someone says.
2. The end punctuation goes [[inside]] the closing quotation mark: **,"** **?"** **!"**
3. Use a **comma**, not a period, before the closing mark when a tag follows: *"Hold still," she called.*
4. Start a [[new paragraph]] each time a different person speaks.
5. Use the tag to show **how** it is said: *whispered, roared, muttered, grinned.*

::: myth
WRONG: All the talking goes in one paragraph.
WHY: It feels like one conversation.
RIGHT: New speaker, new line. It helps the reader keep track of who is talking.
:::

**Quick check.** Fix the punctuation: *help us charlie said the mayor the river is frozen*

::: frame
"___, ___," said the mayor. "___!"
:::

(lines 2)

=> "Help us, Charlie," said the mayor. "The river is frozen!"
