::: teacher Using the rubric (research: rubric-design-research.md)
Introduce the rubric in Lesson 13 with the sample stories, before students draft. Students self-assess after the draft (Lesson 23) and again before publishing (Lesson 28); peer feedback in Lesson 24 uses the checklist on page 2. Teacher feedback names one or two moves for the next draft, so the feedback has a response. The rubric is general (it fits any tall tale), so it can be shared without giving answers away. Report by code: WR14/WR12 (criterion 1), TF7/VO15 (criterion 2), WR15/WR16/WR13 (criterion 3), CV2/CV4/CV12–14/WR8 (criterion 4).
:::

## The rubric

| Criterion | Has yet to meet | Meets | Proficient | Excellent |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Story parts** *(WR14, WR12)* | Some of the WWW parts are missing; the problem or ending is hard to find | All 7 WWW parts are there; the problem is clear and the ending solves it | All 7 parts are there and the events build step by step toward the solution | All 7 parts are there; the events build, and the ending ties back to the problem in a surprising or satisfying way |
| **2. Tall-tale hero and exaggeration** *(TF7, VO15)* | The hero has no amazing ability, or the exaggeration is unconnected to the story | The hero has an amazing ability, and there are exaggerations in the story | The hero's amazing ability is what solves the problem; exaggerations show something about the hero or setting | The amazing ability solves the problem; exaggerations build on each other and the tale explains something in nature or a real place |
| **3. Showing, dialogue and comparisons** *(WR15, WR16, WR13)* | Mostly tells; no dialogue or comparisons | Some showing details; at least one line of dialogue and one comparison | Shows feelings and events through action, sense details and dialogue; comparisons help the reader picture things | Shows almost everything; dialogue reveals the characters; comparisons are fresh and fit the story's place and mood |
| **4. Conventions** *(CV2, CV4, CV12–14, WR8)* | Errors make parts hard to read | Mostly correct; dialogue has quotation marks; tense mostly stays in the past | Dialogue is punctuated with a new line for each speaker; tense stays in the past; pattern words spelled correctly | Correct and controlled; punctuation is used for effect (a pause, a shout); spelling of pattern words is accurate |

## Sample stories

These three stories were written for this rubric. They are about the same hero, so you can compare them.

### Sample A · *Meets*

::: model Sample A
Long ago in Calgary there was a girl named Windy Wanda. She could run faster than a train. One day the wind stopped blowing and the windmills stopped. The farmers were sad. Wanda said "I will help". She ran really fast around the farm and made wind. The windmills started again. Everybody was happy. That is why Calgary is windy.
:::

::: note Why it is "Meets"
**Parts:** all 7 are there (who, when, where, want, happens, ends, feel), but the events are only one step. **Exaggeration:** faster than a train; her ability solves the problem. **Showing:** mostly tells ("the farmers were sad", "everybody was happy"); one line of dialogue; one comparison. **Conventions:** dialogue needs a comma inside the quotation marks: *"I will help," she said.*
:::

### Sample B · *Proficient*

::: model Sample B
Long ago, when Calgary was just a few tents, a girl named Windy Wanda could run so fast that her shadow had to take a taxi to keep up.

One summer, the wind stopped. The windmills froze in place, the wheat stood still, and the farmers stared at the sky.

"No wind means no water for the cattle," said Farmer Lee, wiping his forehead.

Wanda laced up her boots. "Then I'll make some," she said.

She ran around the first farm ten times. The leaves started to tremble. She ran around the whole county a hundred times. Hats flew off. She ran around the province until the clouds spun like a merry-go-round, and the windmills began to creak and turn.

The cattle drank, and the farmers cheered. Wanda was so tired she slept for three days. And that is why, even today, Calgary is one of the windiest cities in Canada.
:::

::: note Why it is "Proficient"
**Parts:** all 7, and the events build (one farm → county → province). **Exaggeration:** her shadow takes a taxi; her running solves the problem; explains Calgary's wind. **Showing:** the drought is shown (windmills frozen, wheat still); dialogue shows the farmer's worry; a simile ("like a merry-go-round"). **Conventions:** dialogue punctuated with a new line for each speaker; past tense throughout.
:::

### Sample C · *Excellent*

::: model Sample C
Back when Calgary was three tents and a horse, there lived a girl named Windy Wanda. She ran so fast that her shadow had to take a taxi to keep up, and she once beat a thunderstorm home by an hour.

Then came the Still Summer. One morning the wind simply stopped, the way a clock stops when nobody winds it. The windmills froze mid-turn. The wheat stood straight as soldiers. Even the flags drooped like wet laundry.

Farmer Lee tapped the empty water trough. *Tink. Tink.* "No wind, no water," he said quietly. "The cattle won't last a week."

Wanda didn't answer. She just bent down and double-knotted her boots.

She ran one lap around the farm, and the dust began to swirl. She ran ten laps around the county, and the leaves began to whisper. She ran a hundred laps around the whole province, so fast that the clouds spun like a merry-go-round and the Rocky Mountains leaned back to stay out of her way.

*Creak.* The first windmill turned. Then another, and another, until the whole prairie was clattering. Water splashed into the troughs. Farmer Lee threw his hat in the air, and the wind carried it all the way to Medicine Hat.

Wanda slept for three days in a haystack. But she ran so hard that summer that the wind never quite slowed down again. That is why, to this day, Calgary is one of the windiest cities in Canada, and why Calgarians always hold on to their hats.
:::

::: note Why it is "Excellent"
**Parts:** all 7; the events build in a pattern of three, and the ending ties back to both the problem (water) and a real fact (Calgary's wind). **Exaggeration:** layered and connected (the shadow's taxi, beating a storm home, mountains leaning back). **Showing:** the drought, Farmer Lee's worry and Wanda's determination are all shown, not told (she double-knots her boots instead of saying "I'm determined"); fresh comparisons ("straight as soldiers", "like wet laundry"); sound words. **Conventions:** correct, with italics and short sentences used for effect (*Tink. Tink.*).
:::
