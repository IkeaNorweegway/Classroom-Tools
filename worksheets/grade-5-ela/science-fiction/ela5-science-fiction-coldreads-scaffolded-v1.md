## Cold read 1 · The Last Paper Book

::: passage The Last Paper Book | A new science fiction story

In the city of Port Aurora, in the year 2110, nobody read paper books anymore. Stories floated in the air above your wrist, glowing, and turned their own pages when your eyes reached the bottom. They could even read themselves out loud in any voice you liked.

Eli Ramos liked his reader-band fine. But he loved his grandfather's apartment, because it was the only place he knew that still had a shelf of paper books. They were yellowed and soft at the edges, and they smelled like dust and something sweet.

"Why do you keep these?" Eli asked one rainy Saturday. "They don't even light up."

His grandfather, Lolo Ramon, pulled one down and handed it to him. "Because nobody can change them," he said. "Your band updates its stories every night. Did you know that? If someone decides a story should be different, it's different by morning, and you'd never know."

Eli laughed. "Why would anyone change a story?"

Lolo shrugged. "People have their reasons. Here, read this one. Then read the same story on your band, and tell me what you find."

The book was called *The River Children*. Eli read it on Lolo's couch all afternoon, turning the crackly pages, while the rain tapped on the window. At the end, the children in the story stood up to the mayor who wanted to dam their river, and they won.

That night, he opened *The River Children* on his band. It was the same, almost word for word, until the last chapter. On his band, the children didn't stand up to the mayor. They agreed that the dam was a good idea, and everyone went home happy.

Eli sat very still. His stomach felt hollow.

The next Saturday, he came back to Lolo's apartment with a notebook and a pencil, which he'd had to borrow from a museum gift shop. "Lolo," he said, "can you teach me how to keep a story safe?"

His grandfather smiled the widest smile Eli had ever seen. "First," he said, "we copy it by hand."
:::

(lines 3)

=> In 2110, everyone reads on glowing bands, so Eli thinks paper books are strange and useless ("They don't even light up," ¶3).

=> Round: at the start he laughs at the idea that anyone would change a story (¶5); at the end he wants to learn to keep stories safe by copying them by hand (¶10).

(lines 2)

=> That he is shocked and upset, because he realizes the story on his band was secretly changed.

## Cold read 2 · The Seed

::: passage The Seed | A new science fiction story

The greenhouse on Mars Station Two was the only green place for fifty million kilometres. Rosa Tran, who was ten and the youngest person on the station, spent every free minute there, among the tomatoes and the bean vines climbing toward the grow-lights.

Her favourite plant was the one nobody else cared about: a small, ugly cactus from Earth, with one sharp spine and no flowers at all. The station's botanist, Dr. Pell, wanted to compost it. "It takes up space," he said. "It's never going to do anything."

"Please," Rosa said. "Just one more month."

That night, the station alarms went off. A dust storm had cracked the greenhouse's water pipe, and the recycling system was down. The station had enough water for its people, but not for its plants. "We'll have to let the garden go," Dr. Pell said quietly. "We can replant from seeds when the system's fixed. In six months, maybe."

For three weeks, Rosa watched the greenhouse wilt. The beans went brown. The tomatoes dropped their leaves. Only one plant stayed green: the ugly little cactus, which had stored water inside its thick stem, the way cacti do in Earth's deserts.

And then, on the twenty-second day, it bloomed. A single flower opened at the top of the spine, bright pink, like a tiny sunrise.

*Forty years later, Dr. Rosa Tran stood in the middle of the first open-air garden on Mars, under a sky that was slowly turning from orange to blue. Around her grew hundreds of tough, water-saving plants, every one of them bred from a single ugly cactus that had once refused to die. A reporter asked her where the idea had come from.*

*"From a plant everyone wanted to throw away," she said.*

Back on Mars Station Two, ten-year-old Rosa carried the flowering cactus to Dr. Pell. He looked at it for a long time.

"Well," he said finally, "I suppose it did something after all."
:::
