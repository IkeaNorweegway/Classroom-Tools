## How to use this reader

**Science fiction** asks "**What if?**" What if robots did our homework? What if a whole town lived under a dome? It is set in the future or uses science and technology that doesn't exist yet, and it shows how that changes the way people live, and the choices they make. All four stories are told in the **third person**: a narrator tells the story using the characters' names and *he*, *she* and *they*.

| Story | What if…? | We use it for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. The Homework Helper | …a robot could do your homework? | Theme and third-person point of view |
| 2. Under the Dome | …your town had perfect weather all year? | Setting, context and round characters |
| 3. Kiko's Grandmother | …a robot took care of your grandmother? | Perspectives |
| 4. The Message from 2087 | …you could see what one choice leads to? | Flash-forward; our mentor text for writing |

Every paragraph is numbered so you can point to your evidence.

::: passage The Homework Helper | Story 1

Maya Chen's homework robot was the size of a shoebox and the colour of a ripe lime. It was called Hex, and on the first day of Grade 5, every student at Ridgeview School got one.

"Hex can help you with anything," Mr. Okafor explained. "It can explain a math problem, read with you, or quiz you for a test. Use it well."

For two weeks, Maya used Hex exactly the way Mr. Okafor meant. Hex showed her how to line up decimals, and she practised until she could do it herself. Hex asked her questions about her library book, and she found that talking about the story made it easier to remember.

Then came the night of the hockey tournament. Maya got home at nine o'clock with a sore ankle and a science report due the next morning. She flopped onto her bed and stared at Hex's little green light.

"Hex," she said slowly, "could you just… write the report?"

Hex's light blinked twice. "I can write a report on how plants use sunlight in four seconds. Would you like that?"

Maya hesitated. Then she pictured her bed, and the ten hours until morning, and said, "Yes."

The report was perfect. It was so perfect that Mr. Okafor wrote "Excellent work!" across the top. Maya felt a warm rush of pride, and then, a moment later, a small, cold feeling she couldn't name.

After that, it was easy. Hex wrote her book review. Hex solved her fraction sheet. Hex wrote her journal entry about a time she felt brave, which was strange, because Hex had never been brave about anything. Maya's grades were the best in the class.

Then came the test.

There were no robots allowed in the gym on test day. Maya looked down at the first question: a fraction problem exactly like the ones Hex had done for her. Her mind was as empty as a cleared screen. She looked at the second question, and the third. She didn't know how to start any of them.

When the tests came back, Maya's was covered in red. She walked home slowly, kicking a stone the whole way.

That night, Hex's light blinked on. "You have a math sheet due tomorrow. Shall I complete it?"

Maya sat down at her desk. She pulled the sheet toward her and picked up a pencil. "No," she said. "Show me the first one. Then I'll try the next one by myself."

It took her an hour. She got three wrong. But when she finished, the cold feeling was gone, and in its place was something better, something that felt like it was hers.
:::

::: note Words to know
**hesitated** paused before deciding · **tournament** a series of games or matches
:::

::: passage Under the Dome | Story 2

In the year 2091, the town of New Banff sat under a dome of clear glass so large that clouds formed inside it. Every morning, the weather system announced the day's forecast in a calm voice: "Twenty-two degrees and sunny." It was always twenty-two degrees and sunny.

Theo Marchand had lived under the dome his whole life. He had never been cold. He had never been wet. He had never seen snow, except in his great-grandmother's old photographs, which showed children in puffy coats, red-cheeked and laughing, throwing white balls at each other.

"What does snow feel like?" Theo asked his great-grandmother, Mémère, who was a hundred and three and had been born before the dome.

"Cold," she said. "And wet, and soft, and sharp. It squeaks when you walk on it at thirty below." She closed her eyes and smiled. "The best days of my life were snow days."

Outside the dome, everyone said, the weather was dangerous. The storms that had come after the climate changed were wild and sudden. The dome kept the town safe. That was why it had been built, and Theo had never questioned it. Until now.

On Theo's eleventh birthday, the Weather Council announced something new. For one hour, as an experiment, the dome would open a small panel at the top of the Commons, and let the outside weather in. It was January. The forecast outside said snow.

Most people stayed home. "Why would anyone want to be cold?" Theo's father said. "We built the dome so we would never have to be."

But Theo went. He stood in the Commons with a handful of curious kids and a few old people with walkers, staring up at the panel as it slid open with a hiss.

At first, nothing happened. Then a single white flake drifted down, turning slowly, and landed on Theo's sleeve. It was perfect, six-sided, like a tiny star, and then it was gone, a drop of water on the cloth.

Then came more. Hundreds. Thousands. The air turned sharp and fresh, and the kids shrieked and spun with their mouths open. Theo's fingers ached with cold, and his nose ran, and he had never felt so alive.

Beside him, Mémère sat in her wheelchair with snow in her white hair, laughing like one of the children in her photographs.

When the hour ended and the panel slid shut, the dome's voice said, "Returning to twenty-two degrees." Theo shivered and grinned at the same time.

That night, he wrote a letter to the Weather Council. It started: "The dome keeps us safe. But I think we need snow days too. Here is why."
:::

::: note Words to know
**dome** a rounded roof shaped like half a ball · **Commons** a shared open space in a town · **experiment** a test to find something out
:::

::: passage Kiko's Grandmother | Story 3

Kiko's grandmother, Obaachan, lived alone in a small apartment on the fourth floor, and she was getting forgetful. She left the stove on. She forgot to take her pills. Once, she went out to buy bread and couldn't remember the way home.

So Kiko's parents bought a CareBot.

The CareBot was tall and white, with soft rubber hands and a screen for a face that showed a friendly, simple smile. It reminded Obaachan to take her pills at eight o'clock. It turned off the stove. It cooked rice exactly the way the instructions said, and it called Kiko's mother if anything went wrong.

"It's perfect," Kiko's mother said. "Now we don't have to worry."

Kiko wasn't so sure. On Saturday, she visited Obaachan, as she always did. The apartment was spotless. The pills were in a neat row. The CareBot stood in the corner, its screen glowing.

"Is it nice to have help?" Kiko asked.

Obaachan looked at the robot for a long time. "It is very good at its job," she said carefully. "But it doesn't know any stories."

Kiko thought about that all week. The robot knew when to give Obaachan her pills. But it didn't know that Obaachan had grown up near the sea, or that she liked her rice a little burnt on the bottom, or that she sang an old song from her village while she folded laundry.

The next Saturday, Kiko brought a notebook. "Obaachan," she said, "tell me about when you were a girl."

Obaachan's eyes brightened. She talked for two hours. She talked about the fishing boats, and the festival with paper lanterns, and the time she got lost in a rice field and found her way home by following the frogs singing. Kiko wrote it all down. Even the CareBot turned its head to listen, though it couldn't understand why anyone would follow frogs.

When Kiko's mother arrived to pick her up, she found her daughter and her mother laughing so hard they were wiping their eyes.

"What's so funny?" her mother asked.

"Obaachan was telling me about the frogs," Kiko said.

Her mother looked at the CareBot, and then at Obaachan, who looked younger than she had in months. "Maybe," her mother said slowly, "we should all visit more."

After that, the CareBot still reminded Obaachan about her pills. But now there was a schedule on the fridge, too, in Kiko's handwriting: a family member every day.
:::

::: note Words to know
**Obaachan** a Japanese word for grandmother · **spotless** perfectly clean
:::

::: passage The Message from 2087 | Story 4 · Our mentor text (it includes a flash-forward)

Jamal Osei found the old phone on the bottom shelf of the community recycling centre, wedged between a toaster and a broken lamp. It was cracked, and it was the kind nobody used anymore. When he pressed the button, the screen glowed blue.

**One new message,** it said. **Sent: 2087.**

Jamal frowned. It was 2027. The message was from sixty years in the future.

He opened it. *Jamal,* it read. *This is you. I'm seventy years old. I'm sending this back because tomorrow you have to choose: the science fair or Ryan's party. Choose the science fair. Trust me.*

Jamal laughed out loud. It had to be a prank. Ryan's party was the biggest event of Grade 5. Everybody would be there. And his science fair project, a little machine that turned dirty river water clean using sand and charcoal, wasn't even finished.

But that night, he couldn't sleep. *Trust me.* He kept seeing the words.

The next morning, Jamal went to the science fair. He finished his filter with tape and hope in the school gym, and he stood next to it all afternoon, explaining it to anyone who would listen. Most people walked past. But one woman stopped. She had a name tag that said *Dr. Amina Bello, Water Engineer*. She asked him a lot of questions, and she listened to all the answers.

"Keep working on this," she said, and handed him her card.

*Sixty years later, the old man sat by a clear blue river in a village in Ghana and watched children fill their buckets. The water came from a filter station, one of ten thousand around the world, each one built from a design that had started as a Grade 5 science fair project. A young engineer walked over and asked him, as visitors always did, how it had all begun.*

*"With a choice," the old man said. "And a little help from a stranger who listened." He smiled at the river. "And, well, a message I had to send."*

Back in 2027, Jamal looked at Dr. Bello's card all the way home. He didn't know yet what it would lead to. He only knew that he had missed the party, and, strangely, he didn't mind at all.

When he checked the old phone that night, the message was gone. In its place was a single line: **Sent.**
:::

::: note What makes it a model
The story uses **WWW, What = 2, How = 2**: who (Jamal), when (2027), where (the recycling centre and the science fair), what Jamal wants (to decide what to do), what happens (he goes to the fair and meets Dr. Bello), how it ends (the message changes his life), and how he feels. The *italic* paragraphs are a **flash-forward**: the story jumps sixty years into the future to show what Jamal's choice leads to, then comes back to 2027. The **theme** is a sentence, not a word: *small choices can change the future.*
:::
