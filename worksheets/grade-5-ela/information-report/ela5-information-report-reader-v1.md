## How to use this reader

These texts are **information texts**: they teach facts about a topic. Unlike a story, an information text is organized by **topic and subtopics**, and it uses **text features** (headings, diagrams, captions, bold words, sidebars and a glossary) to help you find what matters. Every paragraph is numbered so you can point to your evidence.

::: passage Your Body: A Team of Systems | Text 1 · Overview

Your body is made of trillions of tiny living units called **cells**. Cells that do the same job group together to make **tissues**, like muscle tissue. Tissues work together to make **organs**, like the heart or the stomach. And organs work together in teams called **body systems**.

Each body system has its own job, but no system works alone. They depend on each other the way players on a team do.

### The systems and their jobs

The **circulatory system** (heart, blood and blood vessels) carries oxygen and food to every cell. The **respiratory system** (nose, windpipe and lungs) brings oxygen into the body and takes carbon dioxide out. The **digestive system** (mouth, stomach and intestines) breaks food down into nutrients the body can use.

The **skeletal system** (bones) holds the body up and protects soft organs. The **muscular system** moves the bones. The **nervous system** (brain, spinal cord and nerves) controls all the other systems and lets you think, feel and remember.

### How the team works together

Think about running for a ball. Your **brain** sends a message through your **nerves** to your **muscles**, which pull on your **bones**. Your muscles need extra oxygen, so you **breathe** faster. Your **heart** beats faster to carry that oxygen in your blood. The energy for all of it came from food your **digestive system** broke down earlier. Six systems, one kick.

> **Did you know?** Scientists estimate an adult body has about 30 trillion cells. If you counted one cell every second, it would take you almost a million years.
:::

::: note Glossary
**cell** the smallest living unit of the body · **tissue** a group of cells that do the same job · **organ** a body part made of tissues that does a special job · **system** organs that work together
:::

::: passage The Heart: A Pump That Never Rests | Text 2 · Circulatory system

Your heart is a **muscle** about the size of your fist, and it is one of the hardest-working parts of your body. It beats about 100,000 times a day, every day of your life, without you ever having to think about it.

### A pump with four rooms

The heart has four hollow spaces called **chambers**. The two upper chambers, the **atria**, receive blood. The two lower chambers, the **ventricles**, push blood out. Small flaps called **valves** let blood flow in only one direction. The "lub-dub" sound a doctor hears through a stethoscope is the sound of the valves closing.

### Two loops

Blood travels in two loops. In the first loop, the right side of the heart pumps blood to the **lungs**, where it picks up oxygen. In the second loop, the left side pumps that oxygen-rich blood out to the whole body. Then the blood comes back to the heart, and the trip starts again. One full trip around the body takes about one minute.

### Blood vessels

Blood travels through tubes called **blood vessels**. **Arteries** carry blood away from the heart. **Veins** carry it back. Tiny **capillaries**, thinner than a hair, connect them and deliver oxygen to each cell. If all the blood vessels in an adult were laid end to end, they would stretch about 100,000 kilometres, more than twice around the Earth.

> **Try it:** Press two fingers gently on the inside of your wrist below your thumb. The beat you feel is your **pulse**, the push of blood each time your heart squeezes. Count the beats in 15 seconds and multiply by 4.

### Keeping it healthy

Like any muscle, the heart gets stronger with exercise. Activities that make you breathe hard, like running, swimming or skating, make the heart pump more blood with each beat.
:::

::: flow How blood travels (simplified)
Right side of heart | pumps blood
Lungs | blood picks up oxygen
Left side of heart | pumps oxygen-rich blood
Body | cells use the oxygen
Back to the heart | through veins
:::

::: passage Breathing In, Breathing Out | Text 3 · Respiratory system

Every cell in your body needs **oxygen**, and every cell makes a waste gas called **carbon dioxide**. Your respiratory system solves both problems at once. Most people breathe about 20,000 times a day.

### The path of air

When you breathe in, air enters through your nose or mouth. Hairs and sticky **mucus** in your nose trap dust, so the air is cleaner when it goes down your **windpipe** (also called the **trachea**). The windpipe splits into two tubes, one for each lung. Inside the lungs, the tubes branch again and again, like an upside-down tree, until they end in millions of tiny air sacs called **alveoli**.

### Why the air sacs matter

The alveoli are wrapped in capillaries. Because the walls of the air sacs and the capillaries are so thin, oxygen can pass from the air into the blood. At the same time, carbon dioxide passes from the blood into the air sacs. As a result, when you breathe out, you get rid of the carbon dioxide.

### What makes you breathe

You don't have to think about breathing because a dome-shaped muscle under your lungs, the **diaphragm**, does the work. When the diaphragm tightens and moves down, the space in your chest gets bigger, so air rushes in. When it relaxes and moves up, the space gets smaller, so air is pushed out.

### When you exercise

When you run, your muscles use oxygen faster. Because of this, your brain signals you to breathe faster and more deeply. That is why you puff and pant after a race.

> **Did you know?** If the inside surface of an adult's lungs were spread flat, it would cover about half a tennis court. All that surface is what lets so much oxygen get into the blood so fast.
:::

::: flow The path of air
Nose or mouth | air is cleaned
Windpipe (trachea) | tube to the chest
Two branches | one to each lung
Air sacs (alveoli) | oxygen into the blood
:::

::: passage Bones and Muscles | Text 4 · Skeletal and muscular systems

Your bones and your muscles are partners. Bones give your body its shape and strength. Muscles give it movement. Neither can do much without the other.

### Bones: the frame

An adult skeleton has **206 bones**. A newborn baby has about 300, but many of them join together as the baby grows. Bones protect soft organs: your **skull** guards your brain, and your **ribs** form a cage around your heart and lungs.

Bones are alive. They have blood vessels and nerves, and they can repair themselves when they break. Inside many bones is soft **marrow**, which makes new blood cells, millions of them every second.

### Muscles: the movers

You have more than 600 muscles. **Skeletal muscles** are attached to bones by tough cords called **tendons**. Muscles can only **pull**, never push. That is why they work in pairs. When you bend your arm, the muscle on the front of your upper arm (the **biceps**) tightens and pulls. To straighten it, the muscle on the back (the **triceps**) pulls the other way while the biceps relaxes.

Not all muscles are skeletal muscles. **Heart muscle** pumps blood, and **smooth muscle** lines your stomach and blood vessels. These muscles work without you deciding to move them.

### Alike and different

Bones and muscles are both living tissues, both need food and oxygen from the blood, and both get stronger when you use them. But they are different in one big way: bones are hard and do not change shape, while muscles are soft and can contract (get shorter) and relax.

> **Did you know?** The smallest bone in your body is in your ear. The **stirrup** is about the size of a grain of rice.

### Keeping them strong

Running, jumping and climbing make bones denser and stronger. Calcium, found in foods like milk, yogurt and leafy greens, helps build bone.
:::

::: note Glossary
**marrow** soft tissue inside bones that makes blood cells · **tendon** a tough cord that joins muscle to bone · **contract** to tighten and get shorter
:::

::: passage The Brain: Your Control Centre | Text 5 · Nervous system

Right now, your brain is reading these words, keeping your heart beating, controlling your breathing, and remembering what you had for breakfast, all at once. The brain is the control centre of the **nervous system**.

### What the brain is like

An adult brain weighs about 1.4 kilograms, about as much as a big bag of flour. It is soft, wrinkly and pinkish-grey. It contains about 86 billion nerve cells, called **neurons**, which pass messages to each other using tiny electrical and chemical signals.

### Three main parts

The **cerebrum** is the biggest part. It handles thinking, learning, memory, the senses and the movements you choose to make. The **cerebellum**, at the back, controls balance and coordination; it helps you ride a bike without falling. The **brain stem** connects the brain to the spinal cord and controls things you don't think about, like breathing and heartbeat.

### The message highway

The **spinal cord** runs down the middle of your backbone. Nerves branch out from it to every part of your body. When you touch something hot, nerves in your skin send a message up the spinal cord. In some cases, the spinal cord sends a message straight back to pull your hand away before your brain even notices. This fast reaction is called a **reflex**.

> **Did you know?** Some nerve messages travel more than 100 metres per second. That is faster than a race car.

### Taking care of your brain

Sleep is when the brain sorts and stores what you learned during the day; children your age need about 9 to 11 hours a night. A helmet protects your brain when you bike, ski or skate.
:::

::: flow A reflex (touching something hot)
Skin nerves | feel the heat
Spinal cord | sends a fast message back
Arm muscles | pull your hand away
Brain | notices "Ouch!" a moment later
:::

::: passage From Sandwich to Energy | Text 6 · Digestive system

Your body can't use a sandwich as a sandwich. It has to be broken down into tiny pieces, called **nutrients**, small enough to pass into your blood. That is the job of the digestive system, and it takes from several hours to about two days from start to finish.

### Step 1: the mouth

Digestion begins before you swallow. Your teeth cut and grind food, and **saliva** (spit) softens it and starts to break down starches. Your tongue pushes the soft lump to the back of your throat.

### Step 2: the esophagus

When you swallow, the food enters the **esophagus**, a muscle tube about 25 centimetres long. Muscles squeeze in waves to push the food down to your stomach. Because of this squeezing, you could even swallow while standing on your head.

### Step 3: the stomach

The stomach is a stretchy muscle bag. It churns food and mixes it with strong acid and juices that break it down further. After a few hours, the food has become a thick liquid.

### Step 4: the small intestine

Next, the liquid moves into the **small intestine**, a narrow tube about 6 metres long, coiled up in your belly. Here, most of the nutrients pass through the walls into the blood, which carries them to every cell.

### Step 5: the large intestine

Finally, what is left moves into the wider **large intestine**, where water is soaked back into the body. The waste that remains leaves the body when you go to the bathroom.

> **Did you know?** Your stomach makes a new lining every few days so its own acid doesn't digest it.
:::

::: flow The path of food
Mouth | chew and soften
Esophagus | squeeze down
Stomach | churn and mix
Small intestine | nutrients into blood
Large intestine | water taken back
:::

::: passage The Body's Delivery System | Text 7 · A model report (our mentor text)

### Introduction

Imagine a delivery truck that visits every home in the country once a minute, every minute, for your whole life. Your body has one. It is called the **circulatory system**, and it is made of the heart, the blood and the blood vessels. Together, they deliver what every cell needs and carry away what it doesn't.

### The heart

The heart is the engine of the circulatory system. It is a muscle about the size of your fist, and it beats about 100,000 times a day. Inside, it has four chambers and valves that keep blood moving in one direction. For example, the right side sends blood to the lungs, and the left side sends it to the rest of the body. Without this pump, blood would not move at all.

### The blood

Blood is the cargo of the delivery system. Red blood cells carry oxygen from the lungs, and the liquid part of blood, called plasma, carries nutrients from food. White blood cells fight germs. In fact, a single drop of blood holds millions of red blood cells. Blood picks up waste, like carbon dioxide, and takes it away to be removed.

### The blood vessels

The blood vessels are the roads. Arteries carry blood away from the heart, and veins bring it back. The smallest vessels, capillaries, are thinner than a hair, so oxygen and nutrients can pass through their walls into the cells. If you lined up all the blood vessels in an adult, they would circle the Earth more than twice.

### Conclusion

The heart, the blood and the blood vessels work as a team to keep every cell alive. The next time you feel your pulse, remember: that beat is the delivery truck making its rounds.
