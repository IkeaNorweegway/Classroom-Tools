## Cold read 1 · The Badlands

::: passage The Badlands: Alberta's Valley of Dinosaurs | An information text

In the middle of the flat prairie, the land suddenly drops away. Below you is a deep valley of bare, striped hills, twisting gullies and strange stone pillars. These are the **badlands** of the Red Deer River valley, home to the town of Drumheller and Dinosaur Provincial Park. Early travellers named them "badlands" because they were so hard to cross.

### How the badlands formed

About 75 million years ago, this part of Alberta was a warm, swampy coast beside an inland sea. Rivers carried mud and sand into the swamps, and dinosaurs lived and died there. Layer by layer, the mud and sand buried their bones and slowly hardened into soft rock. Much later, at the end of the Ice Age, rushing meltwater carved a deep valley through these layers. Since then, rain and wind have kept wearing the soft rock away. This slow wearing away of rock by water and wind is called **erosion**.

### Hot and dry

The badlands are one of the hottest, driest places in Alberta. In summer, the temperature can climb above 35 °C. Because so little rain falls, few plants grow on the steep slopes. Without plant roots to hold it in place, the soil washes away easily when it does rain. Prickly pear cactus, sagebrush, rattlesnakes and lizards are some of the few living things that can handle the heat.

### Bones in the hills

Erosion is a problem for roads and houses, but it is good news for scientists. Every spring, rain and melting snow wash away a little more of the hills, and new fossils appear on the surface. **Palaeontologists**, scientists who study fossils, walk the hills looking for bones poking out of the rock. Dozens of kinds of dinosaurs have been found here, and new ones are still being discovered.

> **Did you know?** Dinosaur Provincial Park is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, a place the whole world agrees is worth protecting, because so many dinosaur fossils have been found there.
:::

## Cold read 2 · Wood Buffalo National Park

::: passage Wood Buffalo National Park: A Safe Home in the North | An information text

In the far north of Alberta, spreading across the border into the Northwest Territories, lies Canada's biggest national park. Wood Buffalo National Park is larger than the country of Switzerland. It is a wild place of boreal forest, rivers, salt plains and wetlands, and it is a safe home for some of the rarest animals in North America.

### A park for bison

The park was created in 1922 to protect the **wood bison**, which had almost disappeared. Wood bison and plains bison are both kinds of bison, and both are large, shaggy grass-eaters. But they are different too. Wood bison are bigger, taller and darker, and they live in northern forests and meadows. Plains bison are smaller and lived on the open prairie in the south. Today, thousands of wild bison roam the park.

### The whooping crane's journey

The park is also the summer home of the world's only natural wild flock of **whooping cranes**. These tall white birds make the same long trip every year. First, they spend the winter on the coast of Texas, in the United States. Next, in spring, they fly north about 4,000 kilometres. Then, they build nests in the park's wetlands and raise their chicks over the summer. Finally, in the fall, the families fly all the way back to Texas.

### Back from the edge

By the 1940s, only about 15 birds were left in this flock. Hunting and the draining of wetlands had taken away the birds and their homes. Because their nesting area in the park is protected, and because laws now stop people from hunting them, the cranes have slowly come back. Today there are several hundred.

### The delta

In the south of the park is the Peace-Athabasca **Delta**, where rivers slow down and spread out into many smaller channels before they reach a lake. It is one of the largest inland deltas in the world, and millions of ducks, geese and other birds stop there each spring and fall.

### People of the land

First Nations and Métis people, including the people of Fort Chipewyan, have hunted, fished and trapped on this land for generations. Today they work with Parks Canada to help care for the park.

> **From an online ad (made up for practice):** "Ride a wild bison through Wood Buffalo! On our tour, you're guaranteed to see 100 whooping cranes in one day!"
:::
