## Cold read 1 · Your Skin

::: passage Your Skin: The Body's Armour | An information text

Your skin is your body's largest organ. If you could spread out all the skin of an adult, it would cover about two square metres, about the size of a single bed. It is also one of the busiest organs, working day and night to protect you.

### Three layers

Skin has three layers. The outer layer, the **epidermis**, is thin and tough. It is always making new cells at the bottom and shedding old, dead cells at the top. In fact, you lose tens of thousands of skin cells every minute, and some of the dust in your home is dead skin. The middle layer, the **dermis**, holds blood vessels, nerves, hair roots and sweat glands. The bottom layer is made mostly of fat, which keeps you warm and cushions your body.

### A shield

Skin keeps germs out. As long as it is unbroken, most germs cannot get through. That is why a cut needs to be cleaned and covered: the shield has a hole in it. Skin also contains a colouring called **melanin**, which helps protect you from the sun's harmful rays.

### A thermostat

Skin helps control your temperature. When you get hot, sweat glands release sweat onto your skin. As the sweat dries, it carries heat away, so you cool down. When you get cold, the blood vessels in your skin get narrower to keep warm blood deeper inside your body. That is why your fingers can look pale on a freezing day.

### A sense organ

Skin is full of nerve endings that sense touch, pressure, heat, cold and pain. Your fingertips have some of the most nerve endings in your whole body, which is why you can feel the tiny raised dots of Braille.

> **Did you know?** The skin on your eyelids is the thinnest on your body. The skin on the soles of your feet is the thickest.
:::

## Cold read 2 · Your Eyes

::: passage Your Eyes: How You See | An information text

You might think your eyes do the seeing. In fact, your eyes and your brain work together. The eyes collect light, and the brain turns that light into the pictures you see.

### The parts of the eye

The coloured part of your eye is the **iris**. In the middle of the iris is a black hole called the **pupil**, which lets light in. Behind the pupil is the **lens**, a clear, bendable part that focuses light, the way a camera lens does. At the back of the eye is the **retina**, a thin layer packed with millions of cells that sense light.

### From light to picture

First, light bounces off an object and enters the pupil. Next, the lens bends the light so it lands on the retina. Strangely, the picture on the retina is upside down. Then, cells in the retina turn the light into nerve signals. Finally, the **optic nerve** carries those signals to the brain, which flips the picture the right way up and tells you what you are looking at. All of this happens in a fraction of a second.

### Bright and dim

The iris is a muscle. In bright light, it makes the pupil smaller, so less light gets in. In dim light, it makes the pupil bigger, so more light gets in. You can watch this happen: look in a mirror in a dim room, then switch on a light.

### Eyes compared with a camera

A camera and an eye are alike in many ways. Both let light in through an opening, both use a lens to focus it, and both record a picture at the back. But they are different too. A camera stores the picture; your eye sends it to the brain, which makes sense of it, remembers it and helps you react.

### Protecting your eyes

Eyelids and eyelashes keep dust out, and tears wash the eye clean. Sunglasses protect against the sun's rays, and safety glasses protect against flying objects.

:::
