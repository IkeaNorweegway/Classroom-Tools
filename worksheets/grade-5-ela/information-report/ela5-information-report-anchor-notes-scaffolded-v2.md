## 1 · Text features

**Learning target:** I can use text features to find and understand important information. *(TF5, TF1)*

::: wordbank
fact
facts
find what is important
important word
inform
meaning
picture or diagram
section
works or changes
:::

An **information text** teaches [[facts]] about a topic. Its purpose is to [[inform]], not to entertain. Text features help the reader [[find what is important]].

| Text feature | What it does | Example in the reader |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Heading** | Tells what a [[section]] is about | "The rain shadow" (Text 3) |
| **Bold word** | Marks an [[important word]] to learn | **muskeg** (Text 4) |
| **Diagram** (flow chart) | Shows how something [[works or changes]], step by step | "How ice shaped Alberta" (Text 6) |
| **Caption** | Explains what a [[picture or diagram]] shows | "How a chinook forms" |
| **Sidebar** ("Did you know?") | Adds an extra, interesting [[fact]] | Big Rock near Okotoks (Text 6) |
| **Glossary** | Gives the [[meaning]] of key words | Text 1 glossary |

::: myth
WRONG: "Text features are decoration. I can skip them."
WHY: They look separate from the paragraphs.
RIGHT: Text features often hold the most important ideas. Read the headings first to preview, and read every caption.
:::

**Quick check.** You want to know what grows in the alpine zone of the mountains. Which text feature would help you find it fastest, and why?

::: frame
I would look at the ___ because it ___ .
:::

(lines 2)

=> The heading "Three zones on one mountain" in Text 5, because headings tell what each section is about, so I can jump straight to it.

## 2 · Topic and subtopics

**Learning target:** I can find the topic and subtopics of an information text. *(TF11, CO5)*

::: wordbank
Caring for them
heading
How they are alike and different
paragraph
The Boreal Forest: Alberta's biggest region
The Parkland and the Boreal Forest regions
The Parkland: groves and meadows
:::

The **topic** is what the whole text is about. **Subtopics** are the smaller parts of the topic. Each subtopic usually gets its own [[heading]] or [[paragraph]].

| | Text 4: *The Parkland and the Boreal Forest* |
| --- | --- |
| **Topic** | [[The Parkland and the Boreal Forest regions]] |
| **Subtopic 1** | [[The Parkland: groves and meadows]] |
| **Subtopic 2** | [[The Boreal Forest: Alberta's biggest region]] |
| **Subtopic 3** | [[How they are alike and different]] |
| **Subtopic 4** | [[Caring for them]] |

::: example Watch me
I read the title first: *The Parkland and the Boreal Forest*. That's the topic. Then I read only the headings, in order. Each heading is a subtopic. Last, I check: does every paragraph fit under one of the headings? Yes, so my map is complete.
:::

**Quick check.** What are the topic and two subtopics of Text 2?

::: frame
The topic is ___ . Two subtopics are ___ and ___ .
:::

(lines 2)

=> Topic: the Grassland region. Subtopics (any two): a dry, windy land; grasses with deep roots; animals of the open prairie; the badlands; people and the prairie.

## 3 · Main idea and summary

**Learning target:** I can find the main idea of a section and summarize it with key details. *(CO4, CO5)*

::: wordbank
most important details
support
The air has lost most of its water when it crosses; this is one reason the Grassland is dry with few trees
The land east of the Rockies is dry because the air dropped its water on the mountains
too small
:::

The **main idea** is the most important point a section makes. **Key details** are the facts that [[support / prove]] it. A summary of an information text gives the main idea and the [[most important details]], in my own words.

| Section | Main idea | Two key details |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Text 3, "The rain shadow" | [[The land east of the Rockies is dry because the air dropped its water on the mountains]] | [[The air has lost most of its water when it crosses; this is one reason the Grassland is dry with few trees]] |

**Main idea test:** if I say my main idea, could every detail in the section fit under it? If not, my main idea is [[too small]].

::: myth
WRONG: "The main idea is the first sentence."
WHY: Many paragraphs start with it, so we expect it there.
RIGHT: Sometimes the main idea is in the middle or at the end, or it isn't written at all. Ask: what is this whole section mostly about?
:::

**Quick check.** Write the main idea of Text 5's section "Three zones on one mountain" in one sentence.

::: frame
This section is mostly about how ___ .
:::

(lines 2)

=> A mountain has three zones (montane, subalpine and alpine) that get colder and have different plants and animals the higher you go.

## 4 · Cause and effect

**Learning target:** I can find causes and effects in an information text. *(TF11, CO5)*

::: wordbank
Chinook air is warm and dry
the land to the east gets much less rain
the water falls as rain and snow on the western slopes
what happens
:::

A **cause** is why something happens. An **effect** is [[what happens]].

**Signal words:** because · so · as a result · therefore · since · that is why · when ___, ___

| Cause | Signal word | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cool air can't hold as much water | so | [[the water falls as rain and snow on the western slopes]] |
| The air has dropped most of its water crossing the Rockies | as a result | [[the land to the east gets much less rain]] |
| [[Chinook air is warm and dry]] | because | snow melts and disappears fast |

::: myth
WRONG: "If two things happen one after the other, the first one caused the second."
WHY: Order in time feels like cause.
RIGHT: Look for a reason in the text (a signal word or an explanation). Things can happen in order without one causing the other.
:::

**Quick check.** From Text 3: why do the mountains get so much snow?

::: frame
Because ___ , the ___ .
:::

(lines 2)

=> Because wet air from the Pacific rises and cools over the mountains, and cool air can't hold as much water, the water falls as snow on the western slopes.

## 5 · Compare and contrast

**Learning target:** I can explain how two things are alike and different. *(TF11, CO9)*

::: wordbank
alike
different
open land; rich soil; farms and cities; most people
thick forest and muskeg; long winters; few people
trembling aspen; cold winters; deer and coyotes
:::

**Compare** = how things are [[alike]]. **Contrast** = how they are [[different]].

**Signal words:** both · also · similarly · but · however · unlike · while · on the other hand

| | Parkland | Boreal Forest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alike | [[trembling aspen; cold winters; deer and coyotes]] | [[trembling aspen; cold winters; deer and coyotes]] |
| Different | [[open land; rich soil; farms and cities; most people]] | [[thick forest and muskeg; long winters; few people]] |

**Write it as a sentence:** *The Parkland and the Boreal Forest **both** have trembling aspen, **but** the Parkland is open farmland **while** the Boreal Forest is thick forest.*

**Quick check.** Compare the Grassland (Text 2) and the Parkland (Text 4) in one sentence using **both** and **but**.

::: frame
The Grassland and the Parkland both ___ , but the Grassland ___ while the Parkland ___ .
:::

(lines 2)

=> The Grassland and the Parkland both have grass and farms, but the Grassland is dry with few trees while the Parkland has groves of aspen.

## 6 · Topic words: clues and parts

**Learning target:** I can work out the meaning of a new topic word from context and word parts. *(VO4, VO11, VO12)*

::: wordbank
a big wetland in the north
a highway that goes across Canada
full of mountains
ground squirrels
related to nature
shapes of the land
the zone below the alpine zone
:::

**Context clues** are hints in the sentence around a new word:

| Clue type | Example | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Definition** right after it | "huge wetlands called **muskeg**" | [[a big wetland in the north]] |
| **Parentheses** | "Richardson's ground squirrels (often called **gophers**)" | [[ground squirrels]] |
| **Example** | "the **landforms** would change: flat prairie, rolling hills, high mountains" | [[shapes of the land]] |

**Word parts:**

| Part | Means | Word | So the word means |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| sub- | under, below | subalpine | [[the zone below the alpine zone]] |
| -al | related to | natural | [[related to nature]] |
| -ous | full of | mountainous | [[full of mountains]] |
| trans- | across | Trans-Canada Highway | [[a highway that goes across Canada]] |

**Quick check.** "Highest of all is the alpine zone, above the **treeline**, the height where it becomes too cold and windy for trees to grow." What is the treeline? What clue told you?

::: frame
The treeline is ___ . The clue is ___ .
:::

(lines 2)

=> The height on a mountain above which it is too cold and windy for trees. The definition right after the word, after a comma.

## 7 · Can I trust this source?

**Learning target:** I can decide whether a source is reliable. *(WR21, CO17, TF12)*

::: wordbank
advertiser
newer
reliable
sell
:::

Ask four questions about every source:

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| **Who** wrote it? Are they an expert? | A scientist or park warden knows more than an [[advertiser]] |
| **Why** was it written? To inform, or to [[sell / persuade]]? | Sellers may only tell you the good parts |
| **When** was it written? | Places change and we learn new things, so [[newer]] is often better |
| **Can I check it** in another good source? | If two good sources agree, the fact is more [[reliable]] |

::: example Watch me
A website says, "Glacier-Power Water, bottled at the Columbia Icefield, makes kids 50% stronger!" **Who:** the company that sells the water. **Why:** to sell it. **Can I check it?** I can't find the 50% anywhere else. This source is **not reliable** for my report.
:::

::: myth
WRONG: "If it's on the internet (or in a video), it's true."
WHY: Anything looks official on a screen.
RIGHT: Anyone can post anything. Check who made it, why, and whether a second good source agrees.
:::

**Quick check.** Which is more reliable for a report on the Boreal Forest: a science centre website or a video by a company selling camping gear? Explain using one of the four questions.

::: frame
The ___ is more reliable because ___ .
:::

(lines 2)

=> The science centre website: it's written by experts to inform, while the company wants to sell camping gear.

## 8 · Notes in my own words

**Learning target:** I can take notes in my own words and give credit to my sources. *(WR23, WR24, WR22)*

::: wordbank
key words
my own words
sources
:::

**Notes are not sentences.** Write [[key words]] and short phrases, then close the book and say it in your own words.

::: example Watch me
**Text:** "Ice sheets are so heavy that they slowly flow, like very thick pancake batter. As the ice moved, it scraped the land like a giant bulldozer."
**My notes:** ice sheets heavy → flow slowly · scraped land
**In my own words:** The ice was so heavy it crept along and scraped the ground as it went.
:::

**Copying** a writer's sentences and calling them yours is called **plagiarism**. Instead, I write facts in [[my own words]] and list my [[sources]] at the end.

**How to list a source:** Title · author or group (if given) · type of source. *Example: Regions of Alberta Reader, Text 6 · our class.*

**Quick check.** Turn this sentence into notes (key words only): "Cool air can't hold as much water, so the water falls as rain and snow on the western slopes."

::: frame
cool air = ___ · water falls as ___ · where: ___
:::

(lines 2)

=> cool air holds less water · falls as rain + snow · western slopes

## 9 · Planning paragraphs: POW + TIDE

**Learning target:** I can use POW + TIDE to plan a paragraph for each subtopic. *(WR3, WR4, WR20)*

::: wordbank
For example, the Athabasca River tumbles out of the mountains and winds through the Foothills near Hinton
The Foothills are a land of rolling hills and ridges.
The Foothills are where the mountains slowly turn into plains.
They lie in a band just east of the Rockies; they cover about one tenth of Alberta; they get more rain and snow than the prairie
:::

::: strategy POW: what good writers do
**P** **Pick** my idea (topic and subtopics)
**O** **Organize** my notes (TIDE for each paragraph)
**W** **Write** and say more
:::

::: strategy TIDE: the parts of an information paragraph
**T** **Topic** sentence: tells the subtopic
**I** **Important** evidence: two or three facts
**D** **Detail**: an example, a number or a comparison that explains
**E** **Ending** sentence: wraps up the paragraph
:::

| TIDE | Model report, "The land" paragraph (Text 7) |
| --- | --- |
| T | [[The Foothills are a land of rolling hills and ridges.]] |
| I | [[They lie in a band just east of the Rockies; they cover about one tenth of Alberta; they get more rain and snow than the prairie]] |
| D | [[For example, the Athabasca River tumbles out of the mountains and winds through the Foothills near Hinton]] |
| E | [[The Foothills are where the mountains slowly turn into plains.]] |

**Self-talk:** "What's my subtopic?" · "Do I have T, I, D and E?" · "That fact is copied, so let me say it my way." · "Good, that paragraph is done."

**Quick check.** From memory, write what each letter of TIDE stands for.

::: frame
T = ___ · I = ___ · D = ___ · E = ___
:::

(lines 2)

=> Topic sentence, Important evidence, Detail, Ending sentence.

## 10 · Beginnings, endings and transitions

**Learning target:** I can write an introduction and conclusion and connect my paragraphs. *(WR4, WR5)*

::: wordbank
It returns to the giant-staircase idea from the hook.
main idea
thought
:::

**Introduction:** a **hook** + the topic. Hooks: a question ("Did you know Alberta has a wind that can melt snow in an afternoon?"), a surprising fact, or a picture in words ("Imagine a giant staircase across Alberta…").

**Conclusion:** sum up the [[main idea]] + leave the reader with a [[thought]] (a tip, a question, or a link back to the hook).

**Transitions** connect ideas:

| To add | To give an example | To show cause | To finish |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| also, in addition, another | for example, such as | because, so, as a result | finally, in the end, overall |

::: model Text 7, conclusion
"The Foothills connect Alberta's mountains and plains, and they are full of forests, rivers and life. The next time you travel west toward the Rockies and the land starts to roll, remember: you are climbing the middle step of Alberta's giant staircase."
:::

How does the conclusion link back to the introduction? [[It returns to the giant-staircase idea from the hook.]]

**Quick check.** Write a hook for a report on the Rocky Mountains.

::: frame
Did you know that the Rocky Mountains ___ ?
:::

(lines 2)

=> Example: Did you know that snow melting on one icefield in the Rockies flows toward three different oceans?

## 11 · Text features in my report

**Learning target:** I can add text features that help my reader. *(TF6, WR9)*

::: wordbank
help the reader
:::

Choose at least **three** for your published report:

| Feature | How to make it |
| --- | --- |
| **Title and headings** | One heading per subtopic, short and clear |
| **Labelled map or diagram** | Draw it, then label the parts with lines; add a **caption** that says what it shows |
| **Bold words + glossary** | Bold 3–5 key words; define them at the end in your own words |
| **Sidebar** | A "Did you know?" box with one surprising fact |
| **Sources list** | Every source you used, at the end |

A text feature must [[help the reader]], not just decorate the page.

**Quick check.** What caption would you write under a diagram of the three zones of a mountain?

::: frame
This diagram shows ___ .
:::

(lines 2)

=> Example: The three zones of a mountain, from warm valleys to cold, treeless peaks.
