::: teacher How to use this pack
**Body Bulletin** (whole class, Weeks 2–3, performed in Lesson 15) is a reader's theatre script written as a TV news broadcast. Information text needs a different voice from a tall tale: clear, confident, with stress on the key facts and numbers. Rehearse 10–15 minutes a day with feedback, then perform (record it on a tablet if you can, and play it back for self-assessment).
**Repeated reading** (fluency group, 3–5 times a week): the passages below, then F&P leveled non-fiction at about 95% accuracy. Model the passage, 3–4 reads with error correction, time one read, graph it, ask one question. Science words are the hard part: pre-teach them with the long-word routine.
**Partner reading** continues 3 days a week; for information text, "paragraph shrinking" becomes: name the subtopic, then the most important fact, in 10 words or less.
:::

## Body Bulletin: a reader's theatre newscast

**Parts:** Anchor 1 · Anchor 2 · Reporter at the Heart · Reporter in the Lungs · Reporter at the Stomach · Doctor Lee (expert) · Weather Reporter · Everyone

::: script Body Bulletin
ANCHOR 1: Good evening, and welcome to Body Bulletin, the only news show that happens inside you.
ANCHOR 2: Tonight: a pump that never takes a day off, the great oxygen swap, and a sandwich on a long journey.
ANCHOR 1: Let's go live to the heart. Reporter, what's happening there?
HEART REPORTER: (shouting over a beat) Thanks! It's loud in here! This heart has beaten about one hundred thousand times today, and it is not slowing down. Four chambers, four valves, and that sound you hear? Lub-dub. That's the valves closing.
ANCHOR 2: Incredible. And where is all that blood going?
HEART REPORTER: Two loops! First to the lungs to pick up oxygen, then out to the whole body. One full trip takes about a minute. Back to you!
ANCHOR 1: Speaking of oxygen, let's check in with our reporter in the lungs.
LUNG REPORTER: (whispering) I'm standing in one of millions of tiny air sacs, called alveoli. The walls are so thin that oxygen is slipping right into the blood. And carbon dioxide is slipping out.
ANCHOR 2: Why are you whispering?
LUNG REPORTER: The diaphragm is about to move. Here it comes. (louder) WHOOSH! Air rushing in!
ANCHOR 1: Dramatic. Now, the story everyone's talking about: the sandwich.
STOMACH REPORTER: I'm here in the stomach, where the sandwich arrived after a squeeze down the esophagus. The stomach is churning it with acid. In a few hours, it'll be a thick liquid on its way to the small intestine, six metres of twists and turns.
ANCHOR 2: Six metres! Is that safe?
STOMACH REPORTER: Totally normal. That's where the nutrients get into the blood. Back to the studio!
ANCHOR 1: To help us understand all this, we have Doctor Lee. Doctor, how do these systems get along?
DOCTOR LEE: Like a team. The brain sends the signals, the lungs bring in oxygen, the heart delivers it, and the digestive system supplies the energy. No system works alone.
ANCHOR 2: Any advice for our viewers?
DOCTOR LEE: Move your body every day, eat well, and get nine to eleven hours of sleep. Your brain does its filing at night.
ANCHOR 1: And now the weather inside the body. Weather Reporter?
WEATHER REPORTER: A steady 37 degrees Celsius, with heavy breathing expected after recess and a strong chance of hiccups after lunch.
ANCHOR 2: That's our show. For Body Bulletin, I'm—
ANCHOR 1: —and I'm—
EVERYONE: —and that's the news from the inside!
:::

**Rehearsal checklist:** ☐ I can say every science word in my part ☐ I stress the key fact in each line ☐ I pause at commas and dashes ☐ I sound confident, like a reporter

## Repeated reading passages

Read to your partner, who marks any missed word. Count the words read in one minute; if you finish early, start again and keep counting. Read it again, and again. Sound like a science reporter: clear, with the important words stressed.

### Passage 1 · The heart

| Text | Words |
| --- | --- |
| Your heart is a muscle about the size of your fist, and it is one of the hardest-working parts of your body. | 22 |
| It beats about 100,000 times a day, every day of your life, without you ever having to think about it. | 42 |
| The heart has four hollow spaces called chambers. | 50 |
| The two upper chambers receive blood. | 56 |
| The two lower chambers push blood out. | 63 |
| Small flaps called valves let blood flow in only one direction. | 74 |

## How did it sound? (expression scale)

| | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Expression and volume** | Flat, quiet | Some expression | Mostly matches the meaning | Confident; key facts stand out |
| **Phrasing** | Word by word | Two- or three-word chunks | Mostly good phrases; pauses at punctuation | Smooth; stresses important words and numbers |
| **Smoothness** | Many stops and sound-outs | Some long pauses | A few rough spots, fixed quickly | Smooth, even on science words |
| **Pace** | Slow and laboured | Uneven | Mostly like talking | Like a clear reporter |
