## How to use this reader

These texts are **information texts**: they teach facts about a topic. Unlike a story, an information text is organized by **topic and subtopics**, and it uses **text features** (headings, diagrams, captions, bold words, sidebars and a glossary) to help you find what matters. Every paragraph is numbered so you can point to your evidence.

::: passage Alberta: Six Regions, One Province | Text 1 · Overview

Alberta is a big province, about 660,000 square kilometres. If you drove from the Montana border in the south to the Northwest Territories border in the north, you would travel about 1,200 kilometres. Along the way, the **landforms** would change again and again: flat prairie, rolling hills, high mountains, thick forests, and lakes surrounded by bare rock.

Scientists divide Alberta into six **natural regions**. A natural region is an area where the land, the **climate**, the soil, the plants and the animals are alike. Each region has its own look and its own living things, but no region stands alone.

### The six regions

The **Grassland** in the southeast is dry, flat and windy, with few trees. The **Parkland** is a band across the middle of the province, where groves of aspen trees mix with grassy meadows. The **Rocky Mountain** region runs along the western edge and has the highest land in Alberta.

The **Foothills** are rolling, forested hills just east of the mountains. The **Boreal Forest** covers the north, more than half of the province, with spruce, pine, aspen and wetlands. The **Canadian Shield** is a small corner in the far northeast, made of very old rock and dotted with lakes.

### How the regions are connected

Think about a drop of water. It falls as snow high in the Rocky Mountains. In spring, it melts and flows into the Bow River. The river carries it east to Calgary, in the Parkland, where people use it for drinking water. Farther east, farmers in the dry Grassland use river water for their crops. One drop of water, three regions.

The regions are connected in other ways too. Elk move between the mountains and the foothills as the seasons change. Warm winds called chinooks blow from the mountains across the prairie. And people have always travelled between regions. First Nations have lived on these lands for thousands of years, and Métis communities have lived here for more than two hundred years.

> **Did you know?** Alberta's highest point is the top of Mount Columbia, 3,747 metres above sea level. Its lowest point is in the far north, where the Slave River leaves Alberta, about 150 metres above sea level.
:::

::: note Glossary
**natural region** an area where the land, climate, plants and animals are alike · **landform** a shape of the land, like a hill, valley or plain · **climate** the usual weather of a place over many years · **habitat** the place where a plant or animal naturally lives
:::

::: passage The Grassland: Wide Open Prairie | Text 2 · Grassland region

Stand in the Grassland and you can see for kilometres: a huge blue sky, rolling grass, and hardly a tree. This is the prairie of southeastern Alberta. It covers about one seventh of the province.

### A dry, windy land

The Grassland is the driest part of Alberta, and its summers are the hottest. In some years it hardly rains at all. Strong winds blow often and dry the soil even more. In winter, warm chinook winds can melt the snow in a few hours. (Text 3 explains why.)

### Grasses with deep roots

Few trees can grow with so little water, but grasses can. Prairie grasses such as **rough fescue**, Alberta's official grass, grow much of their bodies underground. Their roots spread wide and reach deep to find water, often much deeper than the grass is tall. When a drought, a fire or a herd of grazing animals strikes, the roots survive and the grass grows back.

### Animals of the open prairie

Prairie animals are built for open spaces. The **pronghorn**, the fastest land animal in North America, can outrun almost any predator. **Burrowing owls** nest in old ground squirrel holes. **Richardson's ground squirrels** (often called gophers) dig tunnels to escape hawks and the summer heat. Long ago, millions of **plains bison** grazed here.

### The badlands

In some places, rivers have cut deep valleys into the prairie. There you find the **badlands**: bare, striped hills, deep gullies and **hoodoos**, tall pillars of soft rock with a hard stone cap on top. Dinosaur bones are found in the badlands along the Red Deer River.

### People and the prairie

The Niitsitapi (Blackfoot) peoples have lived on these grasslands for thousands of years. For a very long time, the bison gave them food, clothing and shelter. At Áísínai'pi (Writing-on-Stone), beside the Milk River, carvings and paintings on the sandstone hold stories that are still important to the Blackfoot today. Now much of the Grassland is ranches and farms that grow wheat and canola.

> **Did you know?** A pronghorn can run about 90 kilometres an hour, almost as fast as a car on the highway.
:::

::: flow How a hoodoo forms
Layers of rock | soft rock under a hard stone cap
Rain and wind | wear away the soft rock
The hard cap | protects the rock right under it
A hoodoo | a tall pillar is left standing
:::

::: passage Mountains Make the Weather | Text 3 · Rocky Mountain and Grassland regions

Why does Calgary's snow sometimes vanish in a day? Why does southeastern Alberta get so little rain? The answers come from the Rocky Mountains, and from air that has travelled a long way to get here.

### Air from the ocean

Most of Alberta's weather comes from the west. Air over the Pacific Ocean picks up water as it moves across the sea, so it arrives at the coast of British Columbia wet and full of clouds.

### Up and over the mountains

When the wet air reaches the mountains, it has to rise to get over them. As air rises, it cools. Cool air can't hold as much water, so the water falls as rain and snow on the western slopes. That is why the mountains get so much snow, and why glaciers can form there.

### The rain shadow

By the time the air crosses the Rockies, it has dropped most of its water. As a result, the land to the east gets much less rain. Scientists call this dry area a **rain shadow**. The rain shadow is one reason the Grassland is so dry and has so few trees.

### The chinook

Sometimes in winter, strong winds push air down the east side of the mountains. As air sinks, it gets squeezed and warms up. The result is a **chinook**: a warm, dry wind that can raise the temperature by 20 degrees or more in a few hours.

### What a chinook does

Because chinook air is warm and dry, snow melts and disappears fast, sometimes in a single afternoon. Deer, elk and cattle can find grass to eat again. Some people say chinooks give them headaches. Before a chinook, people often see a **chinook arch**: a long band of cloud across the western sky, with clear blue sky underneath it.

> **Did you know?** In January 1962, a chinook in Pincher Creek is reported to have warmed the air from −19 °C to 22 °C. Some sources say it took one hour; others say a few hours.
:::

::: flow How a chinook forms
Pacific Ocean | air picks up water
Western slopes | air rises, cools and drops snow
Over the top | the air is now dry
Eastern slopes | air sinks, is squeezed and warms
Chinook | a warm, dry wind on the prairie
:::

::: passage The Parkland and the Boreal Forest | Text 4 · Parkland and Boreal Forest regions

Drive north from Edmonton and you will notice the land changing. Open fields and small groves of trees give way to thick forest that goes on for hundreds of kilometres. You have left the **Parkland** and entered the **Boreal Forest**. These two regions sit side by side, but they are very different places.

### The Parkland: groves and meadows

The Parkland is a wide band across the middle of Alberta, from Calgary north to Edmonton and east to Saskatchewan. A smaller piece lies around the Peace River in the northwest. Here, **groves** of trembling aspen trees grow in clumps between grassy meadows, like islands in a sea of grass.

The Parkland has some of the richest black soil in Canada, and enough rain for crops. Because of this, most of it has been turned into farms, towns and cities. More Albertans live in the Parkland than in any other region: Calgary, Red Deer and Edmonton are all here. Only a small part of the natural Parkland is left.

### The Boreal Forest: Alberta's biggest region

The Boreal Forest covers the north of Alberta. It is the largest region, bigger than all the other regions put together. Winters are long and very cold, and summers are short. Forests of **conifers**, trees with needles and cones such as white spruce, black spruce and jack pine, mix with aspen. Between the forests lie lakes, rivers and huge wetlands called **muskeg**.

Far fewer people live in the Boreal Forest. Towns are far apart, and muskeg makes roads hard to build. Moose, wolves, lynx, black bears and woodland caribou live in the forest, and millions of birds come to the wetlands every spring to nest. Fire is part of life here: after a forest fire, new aspens sprout from the roots, and jack pine seeds fall from cones that the heat has opened.

### Alike and different

The two regions have some things in common. Both have trembling aspen, both have cold winters, and both are home to animals such as deer and coyotes. But they are different in big ways. The Parkland is open land with rich soil, farms and cities, while the Boreal Forest is thick forest and wetland with few people.

> **Did you know?** Trembling aspen leaves shake in the smallest breeze. A whole grove of aspens can grow from one shared root system, so the grove is really one giant plant.

### Caring for them

People protect parts of both regions in parks. Elk Island National Park, east of Edmonton, protects a piece of Parkland and herds of bison. Large provincial and national parks in the north protect wide areas of Boreal Forest.
:::

::: note Glossary
**grove** a small group of trees growing together · **conifer** a tree with needles and cones · **muskeg** a wet, spongy wetland in the north · **wetland** land that is covered with shallow water for much of the year
:::

::: passage The Rocky Mountains: Alberta's High Country | Text 5 · Rocky Mountain region

Along Alberta's western edge, the land rises into a wall of jagged peaks. This is the Rocky Mountain region, the highest and coldest part of the province, and one of the most visited places in Canada.

### What it is like

The Rockies are made of layers of rock that were pushed up, folded and stacked over millions of years. The highest point in Alberta is the top of **Mount Columbia**, 3,747 metres above sea level. Banff, Canada's first national park, was created here in 1885. Jasper National Park is just to the north.

### Three zones on one mountain

As you climb a mountain, the air gets colder and the plants change. Scientists divide the mountains into three zones. The **montane** zone is the lowest, in the valleys. It is the warmest zone, with forests of Douglas fir, lodgepole pine and aspen, and grassy meadows where elk and deer graze in winter.

Higher up is the **subalpine** zone, with thick forests of spruce and fir and deep winter snow. Highest of all is the **alpine** zone, above the **treeline**, the height where it becomes too cold and windy for trees to grow. Only low plants, mosses and small flowers grow there. Pikas, mountain goats and bighorn sheep live among the rocks.

### Rivers of ice

Some snow in the mountains never melts. Over many years, it packs down into thick ice called a **glacier**. The **Columbia Icefield**, between Banff and Jasper national parks, is the largest area of ice in the Rocky Mountains. Its meltwater flows into rivers that travel toward three different oceans: the Pacific, the Arctic and the Atlantic (through Hudson Bay). Like most glaciers in the world, the glaciers here are shrinking.

### Visiting safely

The mountains are wild. Park wardens advise staying at least 30 metres away from elk and 100 metres away from bears, and never feeding wildlife. The weather can change quickly, so hikers carry warm clothes even in summer.

> **Did you know?** Bighorn sheep are Alberta's provincial mammal. In the fall, rams run at each other and crash their curled horns together. The crack can be heard from far across a valley.
:::

::: flow From valley to peak
Montane | warm valleys, forests and meadows
Subalpine | spruce and fir, deep snow
Treeline | too cold and windy for trees
Alpine | low plants, rocks, pikas and goats
:::

::: passage How Ice Shaped Alberta | Text 6 · All six regions

Alberta's hills, lakes and valleys look as if they have always been there. But much of the land you see today was shaped by ice, during a cold time that ended only about 10,000 years ago. It happened in steps, over thousands of years.

### Step 1: very old rock

Long before the ice, Alberta was built on rock. In the far northeast, in the **Canadian Shield**, that rock comes right to the surface. It is at least two billion years old.

### Step 2: the ice arrives

Then Earth's climate grew colder. Each winter, more snow fell than melted the next summer. The snow piled up and pressed down into ice. Over thousands of years, huge **ice sheets** formed, more than a kilometre thick in places. At their biggest, about 20,000 years ago, they covered almost all of Alberta.

### Step 3: the ice moves

Ice sheets are so heavy that they slowly flow, like very thick pancake batter. As the ice moved, it scraped the land like a giant bulldozer. It scraped the soil off the Canadian Shield, down to bare rock. It picked up rocks and boulders and carried them hundreds of kilometres.

### Step 4: the ice melts

About 15,000 years ago, the climate began to warm, and the ice started to melt. Huge rivers of **meltwater** rushed across the land. They carved deep valleys, like the Red Deer River valley, where the badlands are today. Meltwater also filled low places and made lakes.

### Step 5: a new land

When the ice was gone, it left a new landscape behind. The Canadian Shield was bare rock dotted with thousands of lakes. In other places, the ice left hills of sand, gravel and clay. Slowly, soil formed, plants grew, and animals and people moved in.

> **Did you know?** Big Rock, near Okotoks, is a boulder bigger than a house. An ice sheet carried it from the mountains near Jasper, hundreds of kilometres away. The name Okotoks comes from a Blackfoot word for rock.
:::

::: flow How ice shaped Alberta
Old rock | the base of the land
Snow piles up | ice sheets form
The ice moves | scrapes the land and carries rocks
The ice melts | meltwater carves valleys and fills lakes
A new land | soil, plants, animals and people
:::

::: passage The Foothills: The Middle Step | Text 7 · A model report (our mentor text)

### Introduction

Imagine a giant staircase across Alberta. The bottom step is the flat prairie in the east. The top step is the Rocky Mountains in the west. In between is the middle step: the **Foothills**, a region of rolling hills, forests and rivers. The Foothills join the mountains to the plains, and they are home to many plants, animals and people.

### The land

The Foothills are a land of rolling hills and ridges. They lie in a long band just east of the Rocky Mountains and cover about one tenth of Alberta. Because they are higher than the prairie, they get more rain and snow. For example, the Athabasca River tumbles out of the mountains and winds through the Foothills near the town of Hinton. The Foothills are where the mountains slowly turn into plains.

### Plants and animals

Forests cover most of the Foothills. Lodgepole pine, Alberta's provincial tree, grows here with aspen and white spruce. For example, many lodgepole pine cones stay shut for years until the heat of a forest fire opens them, so new trees grow after a fire. Grizzly bears, elk and moose find food and shelter in these forests. The Foothills forests are a home for many living things.

### People and work

People have lived in the Foothills for thousands of years. First Nations, including Nakoda and Cree peoples, hunted, gathered and travelled here, and Métis families have lived here too. Today, towns like Hinton and Edson are in the Foothills, and many people work in forestry or in oil and gas. In fact, logs cut in the Foothills become lumber and paper. The Foothills give people both a home and a living.

### Conclusion

The Foothills connect Alberta's mountains and plains, and they are full of forests, rivers and life. The next time you travel west toward the Rockies and the land starts to roll, remember: you are climbing the middle step of Alberta's giant staircase.
