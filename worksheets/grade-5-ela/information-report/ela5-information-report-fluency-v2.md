::: teacher How to use this pack
**Alberta Tonight** (whole class, Weeks 2–3, performed in Lesson 15) is a reader's theatre script written as a TV news broadcast. Information text needs a different voice from a tall tale: clear, confident, with stress on the key facts and numbers. Rehearse 10–15 minutes a day with feedback, then perform (record it on a tablet if you can, and play it back for self-assessment).
**Repeated reading** (fluency group, 3–5 times a week): the passages below, then F&P leveled non-fiction at about 95% accuracy. Model the passage, 3–4 reads with error correction, time one read, graph it, ask one question. Place names and topic words are the hard part: pre-teach them with the long-word routine (*sub-al-pine*, *Ath-a-bas-ca*, *chi-nook*).
**Partner reading** continues 3 days a week; for information text, "paragraph shrinking" becomes: name the subtopic, then the most important fact, in 10 words or less.
:::

## Alberta Tonight: a reader's theatre newscast

**Parts:** Anchor 1 · Anchor 2 · Reporter in the Rockies · Reporter in the Badlands · Reporter in the Boreal · Doctor Lee (geographer) · Weather Reporter · Everyone

::: script Alberta Tonight
ANCHOR 1: Good evening, and welcome to Alberta Tonight, the news show that covers every corner of the province.
ANCHOR 2: Tonight: ice that feeds three oceans, bones in the hills, and a forest bigger than all the other regions put together.
ANCHOR 1: Let's go live to the Rocky Mountains. Reporter, where are you?
ROCKIES REPORTER: (shivering) I'm standing on the Columbia Icefield, and it is cold up here! This ice is the largest in the Rockies. The meltwater under my boots flows toward three different oceans: the Pacific, the Arctic and the Atlantic.
ANCHOR 2: Three oceans! Any wildlife?
ROCKIES REPORTER: A mountain goat is watching me from the rocks above the treeline. I'm keeping my distance. Back to you!
ANCHOR 1: From ice to dust. Let's check in with our reporter in the badlands.
BADLANDS REPORTER: (whispering) I'm in the Red Deer River valley, where the hills are bare and striped. A palaeontologist beside me has just spotted something poking out of the rock.
ANCHOR 2: Why are you whispering?
BADLANDS REPORTER: Because it might be a dinosaur bone. Erosion uncovers new fossils every spring. (louder) It IS a bone! Back to the studio!
ANCHOR 1: Exciting. Now, up north to the Boreal Forest.
BOREAL REPORTER: I'm on a boardwalk over the muskeg, a huge, spongy wetland. All around me are spruce, pine and aspen, and the forest goes on for hundreds of kilometres. A moose just walked by. It did not stop for an interview.
ANCHOR 2: How big is this forest, exactly?
BOREAL REPORTER: More than half of Alberta. That's bigger than all the other regions put together! Back to you.
ANCHOR 1: To help us understand all this, we have Doctor Lee, a geographer. Doctor, how do the regions get along?
DOCTOR LEE: Like a team. Snow in the mountains becomes river water for cities in the Parkland and farms in the Grassland. No region stands alone.
ANCHOR 2: Any advice for our viewers?
DOCTOR LEE: Get outside and look at the land where you live. Then ask: how did it get this way?
ANCHOR 1: And now the weather. Weather Reporter?
WEATHER REPORTER: Look west for a chinook arch, folks. A warm, dry chinook is on the way. Expect the temperature to jump twenty degrees and your snowman to melt by suppertime.
ANCHOR 2: That's our show. For Alberta Tonight, I'm—
ANCHOR 1: —and I'm—
EVERYONE: —and that's the news from across Alberta!
:::

**Rehearsal checklist:** ☐ I can say every place name and topic word in my part ☐ I stress the key fact in each line ☐ I pause at commas and dashes ☐ I sound confident, like a reporter

## Repeated reading passages

Read to your partner, who marks any missed word. Count the words read in one minute; if you finish early, start again and keep counting. Read it again, and again. Sound like a reporter: clear, with the important words stressed.

| Text | Words |
| --- | --- |
| Stand in the Grassland and you can see for kilometres: a huge blue sky, rolling grass, and hardly a tree. | 20 |

## How did it sound? (expression scale)

| | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Expression and volume** | Flat, quiet | Some expression | Mostly matches the meaning | Confident; key facts stand out |
| **Phrasing** | Word by word | Two- or three-word chunks | Mostly good phrases; pauses at punctuation | Smooth; stresses important words and numbers |
| **Smoothness** | Many stops and sound-outs | Some long pauses | A few rough spots, fixed quickly | Smooth, even on place names and topic words |
| **Pace** | Slow and laboured | Uneven | Mostly like talking | Like a clear reporter |
