## How to use this reader

A **lyric poem** expresses a speaker's personal **feelings**. It is usually short, and it uses sound, rhythm and pictures in words to make the reader feel something too. Poems are meant to be **heard**: read each one aloud, twice. Stanzas are numbered so you can point to evidence.

| Poem | Poet | Notice… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who Has Seen the Wind? | Christina Rossetti (England, 1872) | How you can know something you can't see |
| The Song My Paddle Sings | E. Pauline Johnson, Tekahionwake (Mohawk, Ontario, 1895) | Sound and rhythm; a river journey |
| Where Go the Boats? | Robert Louis Stevenson (Scotland, 1885) | A child's wondering |
| "Hope" is the thing with feathers | Emily Dickinson (United States, about 1861) | A metaphor that lasts a whole poem |
| Prairie Morning · Chinook · River Valley, January | New poems for this class | Alberta places |
| Coulee in October | A new poem | Our mentor poem for writing |

The first four poems are more than 100 years old and in the **public domain**: anyone may share them. The rest were written for this reader.

::: poem Who Has Seen the Wind? | Christina Rossetti, from *Sing-Song* (1872)
Who has seen the wind?
Neither I nor you:
But when the leaves hang trembling
The wind is passing thro'.

Who has seen the wind?
Neither you nor I:
But when the trees bow down their heads
The wind is passing by.
:::

::: note Words to know
**thro'** an old way of writing *through* · **trembling** shaking a little
:::

::: poem Where Go the Boats? | Robert Louis Stevenson, from *A Child's Garden of Verses* (1885)
Dark brown is the river,
  Golden is the sand.
It flows along for ever,
  With trees on either hand.

Green leaves a-floating,
  Castles of the foam,
Boats of mine a-boating—
  Where will all come home?

On goes the river
  And out past the mill,
Away down the valley,
  Away down the hill.

Away down the river,
  A hundred miles or more,
Other little children
  Shall bring my boats ashore.
:::

::: poem The Song My Paddle Sings | E. Pauline Johnson (Tekahionwake), from *Flint and Feather* (1914 edition)
West wind, blow from your prairie nest,
Blow from the mountains, blow from the west.
The sail is idle, the sailor too;
O! wind of the west, we wait for you.
Blow, blow!
I have wooed you so,
But never a favour you bestow.
You rock your cradle the hills between,
But scorn to notice my white lateen.

I stow the sail, unship the mast:
I wooed you long but my wooing's past;
My paddle will lull you into rest.
O! drowsy wind of the drowsy west,
Sleep, sleep,
By your mountain steep,
Or down where the prairie grasses sweep!
Now fold in slumber your laggard wings,
For soft is the song my paddle sings.

August is laughing across the sky,
Laughing while paddle, canoe and I,
Drift, drift,
Where the hills uplift
On either side of the current swift.

The river rolls in its rocky bed;
My paddle is plying its way ahead;
Dip, dip,
While the waters flip
In foam as over their breast we slip.

And oh, the river runs swifter now;
The eddies circle about my bow.
Swirl, swirl!
How the ripples curl
In many a dangerous pool awhirl!

And forward far the rapids roar,
Fretting their margin for evermore.
Dash, dash,
With a mighty crash,
They seethe, and boil, and bound, and splash.

Be strong, O paddle! be brave, canoe!
The reckless waves you must plunge into.
Reel, reel.
On your trembling keel,
But never a fear my craft will feel.

We've raced the rapid, we're far ahead!
The river slips through its silent bed.
Sway, sway,
As the bubbles spray
And fall in tinkling tunes away.

And up on the hills against the sky,
A fir tree rocking its lullaby,
Swings, swings,
Its emerald wings,
Swelling the song that my paddle sings.
:::

::: note About the poet and words to know
E. Pauline Johnson (1861–1913), whose Mohawk name was **Tekahionwake**, was born on the Six Nations of the Grand River reserve in Ontario. She was one of the most famous poets and performers in Canada in her time, and she travelled across the country, including the prairies, reciting her poems on stage. **wooed** tried to win over · **lateen** a triangle-shaped sail · **laggard** slow · **eddies** water spinning in circles · **seethe** churn and bubble · **keel** the bottom ridge of a boat
:::

::: poem "Hope" is the thing with feathers | Emily Dickinson, from *The Complete Poems* (1924 edition)
"Hope" is the thing with feathers
That perches in the soul,
And sings the tune without the words,
And never stops at all,

And sweetest in the gale is heard;
And sore must be the storm
That could abash the little bird
That kept so many warm.

I've heard it in the chillest land,
And on the strangest sea;
Yet, never, in extremity,
It asked a crumb of me.
:::

::: note Words to know
**perches** sits, like a bird on a branch · **gale** a very strong wind · **abash** embarrass, make shy · **extremity** the very worst moment
:::

::: poem Prairie Morning | a new poem
The sky comes first, out here.
It takes up all the room,
a blue so wide the barns look small,
the grain elevator, a thumb.

The meadowlark goes off like a kettle,
one bright whistle, then two.
The wheat is talking in whispers
the way it always knew.

And I stand at the end of the gravel road
where the fence posts march away,
and the whole flat world says *good morning*
and I say it back today.
:::

::: poem Chinook | a new poem
All January, the cold sat on us
like a dog that would not move.
Then one afternoon, the west went soft,
a band of blue above the mountains, an arch,
and the wind came down warm as a breath
on the back of my neck.

Icicles let go.
Water talked in the gutters.
My dad stood in the driveway in a T-shirt
like he'd won something.

By morning it was minus twenty again.
But for one afternoon
the whole town remembered summer,
and nobody forgot to say so.
:::

::: poem River Valley, January | a new poem
The North Saskatchewan has gone quiet,
locked under glass,
a long white road between the trees.

My breath hangs in front of me
like a word I haven't said yet.
The snow squeaks. Somewhere a chickadee
counts its own name.

I think of the river under the ice,
still moving, still going somewhere,
the way I keep going
in the dark months, too.
:::

::: poem Coulee in October | a new poem · our mentor poem
The coulee holds the last of the light
like a cupped hand holds water.
The grass has turned the colour of toast,
and the wind smells like dust and cold.

I sit on the rim where the hills fall away.
Below, the creek is a silver thread
stitching the bottom of the world together,
and a hawk turns slow circles overhead.

My grandpa brought me here when I was five.
He said, *The land remembers everyone.*
I didn't know what he meant then.
I think I do now, in the setting sun.
:::

::: note What makes it a model
**Place:** a real kind of Alberta landscape (a **coulee** is a deep, dry valley). **Senses:** sight (toast-coloured grass, silver creek), smell (dust and cold), the feel of the wind. **Feeling:** quiet, remembering someone. **Comparisons:** a simile (*like a cupped hand*) and a metaphor (*the creek is a silver thread*). **Shape:** three stanzas of four lines; each stanza moves closer, from the land, to the view, to a memory. The last stanza tells us why this place matters to the speaker.
:::
