Part A — Core Questions (Q1–Q19)

Q1★ Core
Opens after Lesson covering Section 1 — Types of political systems · spectrum diagram
AnswerAnswers (left to right): Dictatorship · Oligarchy · Monarchy · Democracy · Anarchy. Hardest to place is usually monarchy or the dictatorship/oligarchy boundary — accept any reasonable justification that references number of people holding power.
Q2★ Core
Opens after Section 6 — Canada's and Alberta's parties
AnswerFederal: New Democratic Party (NDP) and Bloc Québécois. Alberta: United Conservative Party (UCP) and New Democratic Party (NDP).
Q3★ Core
Opens after Section 2 — Direct vs. representative democracy
Answer(a) The 600-person town — direct democracy. (b) The 40-million-person country — representative democracy. (c) Direct democracy requires gathering everyone to vote on every decision, which is realistic for a small population but not for millions of people spread across a large area; representative democracy solves this by having elected people vote on citizens' behalf.
Q4★★ Extended
Opens after Section 3 — Parliamentary vs. presidential comparison
AnswerPresidential advantage: stability/predictability — a fixed term means no sudden leadership changes. Presidential disadvantage: an unpopular president stays in power the full term regardless of public opinion. Parliamentary advantage: a PM who loses public/legislative confidence can be replaced quickly. Parliamentary disadvantage: less predictable — elections or leadership changes can happen with little warning.

Q6★ Core
Opens after Section 5 — Party systems around the world
AnswerChina = one-party. United States = two-party. Canada = multiparty. A party system tells us how many parties realistically have a chance of winning power in a country.
Q7★★ Extended
Opens after Section 1 — Is it really a democracy?
AnswerNo — a defining feature of democracy is that citizens can genuinely choose between competing options. A country that legally allows only one party to run is functioning more like a one-party/dictatorship-leaning system than a true democracy, regardless of what it calls itself. Holding elections is not enough on its own; the elections must offer real choice.
Q8★ Core
Opens after Section 6 — Party membership, error analysis
AnswerError: Party membership and volunteering are open to people of many ages — most major Canadian parties do not require voting age (18) to join as a member or help with riding association activities. Correction: Voting in an election requires being 18+, but joining a party or volunteering with a riding association does not have the same age requirement.
Misconception-Adjacent

Watch for confusion between "voting age" and "participation age."

Q9★★ Extended
Opens after Section 4 — Governor General misconception
Answer(a) No. (b) The Governor General's role is mostly ceremonial — they give Royal Assent (formal approval) to bills that Parliament has already passed and represent the monarch, but they act on the advice of the Prime Minister and Cabinet by long-standing convention. Real day-to-day political power belongs to the Prime Minister and Cabinet, who are accountable to elected MPs.

Q11★ Core
Opens after Section 5 — Government and opposition roles
Answer(a) Government. (b) Official opposition. (c) Sample completion: "…question and challenge the government's decisions and offer a different point of view…"
Q12★★ Extended
Opens after Section 3 — "All democracies are the same" misconception
AnswerStrong answers name at least two real differences, such as: Canada uses a parliamentary system where the PM is chosen through party leadership and seat count, while the U.S. uses a presidential system where the President is elected directly, nationwide, for a fixed term; Canada's executive and legislative branches are closely connected, while the U.S. keeps them separate with checks and balances (e.g., the veto). Both countries are democracies, but "democracy" covers multiple different models, not one single system.
Misconception Target

[misconception target: democracy = one single system everywhere]

Q13★ Core
Opens after Section 1 — Sort the political systems
AnswerBusiness families with no public vote = Oligarchy. One leader, unfair elections = Dictatorship. Elected representatives make laws = Democracy.
Q14★★ Extended
Opens after Section 4 — Ballot analysis: the Prime Minister misconception
AnswerThe ballot only lists local candidates for that riding — no party leader's name appears anywhere on any ballot in the country, because every riding has different candidates. This proves Canadians vote only for their local MP. A party becomes government by winning the most seats nationwide, and that party's leader becomes Prime Minister — a result of party leadership and seat count, not a direct nationwide vote for a single person.
Misconception Target

[misconception target: Canadians directly elect the Prime Minister like Americans elect a president]

Q15★★ Extended
Opens after Section 6 — Joining a party, applying the process
AnswerAccept any two realistic actions such as: joining the party as a member (most parties don't require voting age), attending or voting at riding association meetings on candidate selection, volunteering with the local riding association, or attending party events to express support for a candidate. Should reference that riding associations elect the local candidate, and party members have a real vote in that process.
Q16★★ Extended
Opens after Section 3 — Presidential vocabulary, veto scenario
Answer(a) Congress (the elected legislature) passed a bill; the President used the veto to reject it; Congress then voted again and got enough votes to override the President's veto, making it law despite the President's rejection. (b) No — in Canada's parliamentary system, the executive (PM and Cabinet) is drawn from and depends on the support of the legislature (Parliament), so there is no separate presidential veto power built into the system the way there is in the U.S.'s separated executive/legislative structure.
Q17★★ Extended
Opens after Section 5 — Multiparty coalition scenario
Answer(a) Multiparty system. (b) No party has a majority (more than 50%) of seats, so under majority rule no single party can pass laws alone — parties may need to negotiate a coalition or govern as a minority government, which can slow decisions or require compromise. (c) Strong response acknowledges trade-offs both ways: multiparty systems can be slower to reach decisions when no majority exists, but they also give voters more choice; two-party systems decide faster but limit voter options. Neither is simply "broken" or "better."
Misconception Target

[misconception target: more political parties automatically means a healthier democracy]

Q18★ Core
Opens after Section 2 — Direct democracy limits, transfer
AnswerA referendum lets citizens weigh in directly on one especially important question (e.g., a constitutional change) where getting direct public input is valued, without requiring that model for every law. Using representative democracy for everyday law-making is more practical because it would be unrealistic to hold a nationwide direct vote on every single bill.

Part B — Stretch Questions (S1–S4)

Multi-concept synthesis. Use as discussion guides or for extended assessment marking.

S1★★
Design a political party
Answer(a) Answer depends on the student's designed party — key marking point: the student must justify their party system choice using the trade-offs discussed in Section 5 (e.g., "multiparty, because my party represents a narrow issue and wouldn't need to win outright"). (b) Must correctly describe one real structural feature (e.g., "in a parliamentary system, my party would need to win a plurality of ridings, not a nationwide vote, to have a chance at government").
S2★★
Compare two systems fully
AnswerShould correctly compare leader selection (direct election vs. party leadership + seats), term stability (fixed term vs. no fixed term/can lose confidence), and branch connection (separated with checks and balances vs. closely connected). Accept either conclusion on which handles loss of trust better, provided the reasoning is grounded in the system's actual structure (e.g., parliamentary allows faster removal; presidential offers more short-term stability).
S3★★
Full chain: citizen to Prime Minister
AnswerSample full-mark answer: A citizen joins a party as a member → participates in their local riding association → helps select the party's candidate for their riding → the candidate wins that riding in the general election → the party wins enough seats overall to form government → the party's leader (already head of the party's caucus) becomes Prime Minister. Award marks for correct use of 5+ terms in a logical order, not for verbatim wording.
S4★★
Evaluate a system design
AnswerStrong answers weigh control levels (both are democracies — moderate/low central control compared to dictatorship/oligarchy), individual rights (both preserve individual rights via elections, though the presidential model separates power more to prevent one branch dominating), and stability (fixed-term presidential = predictable but can trap an unpopular leader in power; parliamentary can remove an unpopular leader fast but is less predictable). Recommendation can go either way — mark on the quality and unit-grounded justification, not on which option is "correct."
