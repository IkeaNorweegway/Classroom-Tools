## 2 core | Section 1 — The road to Confederation

- ___ London Conference
- ___ Great Coalition
- ___ BNA Act comes into force

=> 1 Great Coalition (1864) · 2 Charlottetown (Sept. 1864): agree to discuss union · 3 Quebec (Oct. 1864): 72 Resolutions · 4 London (1866–67): finish the plan with Britain · 5 BNA Act (July 1, 1867).

## 3 core | Section 4 — Inside Parliament

After a federal election, the seats in the House of Commons are: Party A 160, Party B 120, Party C 40, Party D 23.

a) Which party's leader will most likely become Prime Minister? Why?
b) Did any voter put that leader's name on their ballot? Explain.

(lines 3)

=> a) Party A's leader: Party A has the most seats.
=> b) Only voters in the leader's own riding. Everyone else voted for a local candidate in their own riding.

## 4 ext | Sections 3 and 4 — Branches and Parliament

In Canada, the Prime Minister and Cabinet (executive) are also MPs who sit in Parliament (legislative). Explain one advantage and one risk of the executive and legislative branches being this closely connected.

(lines 4)

=> Advantage: the government can usually get its laws passed, and it answers to MPs every day in the House. Risk: a government with a majority can pass laws with little check from other branches, since its own MPs vote for them.

## 7 core | Section 6 — The courts

A man is found guilty in an Alberta trial court. He thinks the judge applied the law wrongly. Describe the steps his case could take, from the trial court to the final decision.

(lines 3)

=> Trial court → Alberta Court of Appeal → Supreme Court of Canada (if it agrees to hear the case), which gives the final answer.

## 8 ext | Section 3 — Why three branches?

A student says: "It would be faster to let the Prime Minister make the laws, enforce them and judge them." Agree or disagree, and explain why the BNA Act split power into three branches.

(lines 4)

=> Disagree: faster, but one person with all the power could pass unfair laws, enforce them unfairly and judge their own case. Three branches mean each job is done by a different part of government, which protects citizens.

## 9 ext | Section 1 — Deadlock

Explain how the deadlock in the Province of Canada led to Confederation. Use the words *deadlock*, *Great Coalition* and *Quebec Conference*.

(lines 4)

=> Canada West and Canada East had the same number of seats and disagreed, so governments kept falling: a deadlock. In 1864 Macdonald, Cartier and Brown formed the Great Coalition to find a new system. That led to the conferences, and at the Quebec Conference they wrote the 72 Resolutions, the plan for Canada's government.

## 10 ext | Section 4 — Reading a ballot

A voter says, "I can't find the Prime Minister on here. This ballot must be wrong." Explain what the voter misunderstands, and how their vote still affects who becomes Prime Minister.

(lines 4)

=> The ballot only lists the candidates in this riding; Canadians vote for an MP, not the PM. The voter's choice helps decide which party wins this seat, and the party with the most seats across Canada forms government; its leader becomes PM.

## 11 ext | Section 5 — Sharing power

Explain one benefit and one challenge of dividing power between the federal and provincial governments. Give a real example for each.

(lines 4)

=> Benefit: provinces make decisions that fit their people, e.g. Quebec runs French-language schools; Alberta manages its own oil and gas. Challenge: levels disagree or overlap, e.g. a pipeline crossing provinces, or health care paid for partly by Ottawa but run by provinces, so decisions take longer.

## 12 ext | Section 2 — Rep by pop and the Senate

Under rep by pop, Ontario and Quebec had most of the seats in the House of Commons in 1867. Explain why the Maritimes agreed to Confederation anyway. Use evidence about the Senate.

(lines 3)

=> The Senate gave each region 24 seats (Ontario, Quebec, the Maritimes), so the smaller Maritime colonies had an equal voice in the upper house, which balanced rep by pop in the House of Commons.

## 13 ext | Section 6 — One top court

Evaluate this claim: "Each province should have the final say on what Canada's criminal law means inside that province." Use what you know about courts of appeal and the Supreme Court.

(lines 4)

=> Disagree: if each province had the final say, the same law could mean different things in different provinces, and someone could be guilty in one and innocent in another. A general court of appeal, the Supreme Court of Canada, keeps the law consistent across the country.

## S1 stretch | Sections 2 and 4

Responsible government means the executive must keep the support of the House of Commons. Explain how this protects citizens, using what you know about how the Prime Minister is chosen.

(lines 5)

=> Citizens elect MPs; the party with the most seats forms government and its leader becomes PM. If the government loses the support of those elected MPs, it must resign or call an election, so the people's representatives can remove a government that stops listening.

## S2 stretch | Sections 1, 5 and 6

"Canadian sovereignty was developed gradually." Use two pieces of evidence from this unit to support this statement.

(lines 5)

=> The BNA Act was a British law passed in London; Canada's top court was only created in 1875; until 1949 final appeals still went to the Privy Council in London. Canada gained control in steps, not all at once.
