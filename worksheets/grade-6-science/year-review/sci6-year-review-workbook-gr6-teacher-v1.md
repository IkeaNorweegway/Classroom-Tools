### Q1 ★ [Unit: Forces]

*Scenario: Students at Riverside School are building a wooden bridge over a small creek. The main beam is 4 metres long. When a student walks across the middle, the top of the beam gets squeezed. The bottom of the beam gets pulled apart.*

**(a)** Compression (top of beam is being squeezed together)

**(b)** Tension (bottom of beam is being pulled apart)

**(c)** The external force — the weight of the walker pressing down on the beam — causes the beam to bend, which produces these internal forces as the material resists deformation.

**Circulation prompt:** If students reverse compression and tension, ask: *"If I squeezed clay from both sides, what's happening to the middle — is it being pulled or pushed?"*

### Q2 ★ [Unit: Living Systems]

*Scenario: A boreal forest near Fort McMurray has: spruce trees, wolves, insects, moss, rocks, soil, sunlight, and water.*

**(a)** Biotic (any two): spruce trees, wolves, insects, moss | Abiotic (any two): rocks, soil, sunlight, water

**(b)** Decomposers break down dead organic material, returning nutrients to the soil. They complete the nutrient cycle — without them, ecosystems would accumulate dead matter and nutrients would be locked away from living organisms.

**Circulation prompt:** Students often say decomposers "clean up" — push for what happens to the nutrients specifically.

### Q3 ★ [Unit: Matter]

*Scenario: Steel railway tracks run through southern Alberta. On a hot summer day, the steel rail feels warm to the touch. Its total length increases slightly. Students use the particle model to explain what is happening inside the rail.*

**Answer:** When the rail heats up, the particles absorb energy and begin to move faster / vibrate more. As they move faster, they push slightly further apart from each other, increasing the spaces between particles. This causes the overall length and volume of the rail to increase (expansion).

**Key language to listen for:** particles moving faster, spacing increasing, volume/length increasing.

**Circulation prompt:** Students who say "the rail gets bigger because it absorbs water" or similar — redirect to the particle model.

### Q4 ★ [Unit: Climate]

*Scenario: In the last 30 years, High River, Alberta has had three major floods. A local resident says the weather has been getting worse. A climate scientist says the climate in this region has changed. Students distinguish the two kinds of claims.*

**(a)** Weather = short-term atmospheric conditions in a specific place and time (temperature, precipitation, wind on a given day). Climate = long-term patterns of weather in a region over decades or longer.

**(b)** The scientist's claim requires long-term data — a single flood or a few bad years is weather variability. To claim the climate has changed, you need decades of data showing a consistent pattern or trend.

### Q5 ★ [Unit: Space]

*Scenario: Ex-Alta 1 is a small satellite designed and built by University of Alberta students. It orbits Earth and does not produce its own light.*

**(a)** Artificial satellite (designed and built by humans, not formed naturally)

**(b)** Ex-Alta 1 reflects sunlight. We can detect it because sunlight bounces off its surface and reaches observers on Earth (or through telescopes/tracking equipment).

**(c)** Produces own light: the Sun (or any star) | Reflects light: any planet, moon, or asteroid (e.g., Mars, the Moon)

**Circulation prompt:** Students sometimes say planets "shine" — clarify that planets are visible because they reflect, not because they emit.

**Any three of:** availability (is the resource accessible in this location?), environmental impact (pollution, land use, emissions), economic cost (installation, maintenance, ongoing cost), societal impact (jobs, reliability, public acceptance), responsible management (long-term sustainability).

### Q7 ★ [Unit: Scientific Methods]

*Scenario: A researcher wants to test whether higher temperatures cause spruce trees to grow faster. Students name two features a controlled experiment must include to make the results correct.*

**Any two of:**

- **Control group:** A group of spruce trees kept at normal temperature, with all other conditions identical to the experimental group. This isolates temperature as the variable being tested.
- **Variable isolation:** Only one variable (temperature) changes between the experimental and control group. All other conditions (water, soil, light, species) stay the same.
- **Repeated trials:** Multiple trees tested at each temperature to ensure the result isn't due to chance.
- **Measurable outcome:** Growth must be measured in a consistent, quantifiable way (e.g., height in cm over 6 weeks).

### Q9 ★ [Unit: Forces]

*Scenario: The timber bridge from Q1. The walking planks flex slightly and spring back. One railing post was hit by a bicycle and is now bent out of shape for good.*

**(a)** Elasticity (elastic deformation — temporary change; material returns to original shape)

**(b)** Plasticity (plastic deformation — permanent change; material does not return to original shape)

**(c)** Elasticity = temporary change; the material returns to its original shape when the force is removed. Plasticity = permanent change; the material is deformed beyond its elastic limit and stays changed.

### Q10 ★ [Unit: Matter] — Visual

*Scenario: Three containers of water particles (A, B, C) show different particle arrangements — tightly packed regular rows; close together disordered; far apart freely moving. Students label each state of matter and explain phase changes.*

**(a)** Box A: solid | Box B: liquid | Box C: gas

**(b)** Melting (solid → liquid). *Accept: fusion.*

**(c)** During the change from liquid to gas (vaporization), particles are gaining enough energy to overcome the attractive forces holding them close together. They break free from each other and spread out, which requires a large energy input.

**Circulation prompt:** Students who label C as "steam" rather than "gas" — acceptable colloquially, but push for "gas" as the state-of-matter term.

**(b)** After processing: wind turbine converting wind energy into electricity. *Must specify conversion to another form — electricity is the expected answer.*

### Q14 ★ [Unit: Climate]

*Scenario: Climate change has both natural and human causes. Students name two natural and two human factors.*

**(a)** Any two natural: volcanic eruptions (release CO₂, ash blocks sunlight), changes in Earth's orbit (Milankovitch cycles), variations in solar output, large meteor/asteroid impacts.

**(b)** Any two human: burning fossil fuels (releasing CO₂), deforestation (removing CO₂ absorption), industrial agriculture (methane), industrial pollution.

### Q15 ★ [Unit: Matter]

*Scenario: A railway worker uses a liquid thermometer to measure air temperature near the steel tracks. Students explain why the liquid rises using the particle model, then name the scientific temperature scale.*

**(a)** When the temperature rises, the liquid particles gain energy and move faster. As they move faster, they push further apart (expand), causing the liquid to take up more space and rise up the thermometer tube.

**(b)** Celsius (°C). 0°C = the temperature at which water freezes (or ice melts).

### Q17 ★ [Unit: Space / Scientific Methods]

*Scenario: Pluto was called a planet for 76 years. Scientists reclassified it as a dwarf planet in 2006. A student claims this means science can't be trusted.*

**(a)** Science updates its classifications and explanations when new evidence or better criteria are established. The reclassification of Pluto shows that science changes when understanding improves, not that earlier scientists made careless mistakes.

**(b)** This is a sign that science is working correctly. Scientists updated their classification system (defining what qualifies as a planet) when they had more information about the Kuiper Belt and similar objects. Self-correction is a strength of science — it means the field does not hold onto wrong ideas indefinitely.

**[Misconception target: science is "wrong" when it changes]** *Push students to articulate why changing is a feature, not a flaw.*

### Q18 ★ [Unit: Scientific Methods / Climate]

*Scenario: Elder knowledge keepers near High River have recorded that the Bow River's ice breaks up three weeks earlier in spring than it did 40 years ago. A climate scientist has 30 years of temperature data showing 1.8°C average warming. Students explain how traditional knowledge and scientific data work together.*

**Strong answer:** The two types of evidence work together because they measure the same phenomenon from different perspectives and timescales. Temperature data provides precise, quantitative, instrument-verified measurements over 30 years. Traditional knowledge provides qualitative, observation-based documentation that may extend further back in time and captures local, on-the-ground changes that instruments don't record. When both types point to the same conclusion (warming, earlier ice break-up), the explanation is more robust — it has been tested by different methods and still holds.

**What to look for:** Students must say more than "they agree with each other." Push for *why* agreement across different knowledge systems strengthens the explanation.

### Q19 ★ [Unit: Forces] — Visual

*Scenario: A diagram shows a student (500 N) standing on the timber bridge deck. Arrow A points downward — the student's weight pressing on the deck. Students name Arrow A, draw the reaction force, and distinguish a Newton's Third Law pair from balanced forces.*

**(a)** Applied force (the student's weight / gravitational force acting downward through the student onto the deck). *Accept: weight, gravitational force.*

**(b)** The reaction arrow should point **upward** from the bridge deck toward the student, labelled: 500 N, upward, Normal force (or reaction force / contact force).

**(c)** A Newton's Third Law pair always acts on **two different objects** — one force acts on the student, the other acts on the bridge. Forces that "balance out" (like two equal forces on the same object) act on the **same object**. Newton's Third Law pairs are always equal, opposite, and on different objects.

**[Misconception target: action-reaction forces cancel out]** *This is the key Forces misconception. Students who say "they cancel" are confusing Newton's Third Law with equilibrium. The forces act on different objects and cannot cancel.*

### Q20 ★★ [Unit: Matter]

*Scenario: Water in rock cracks near the railway freezes in winter. Unlike most substances, water is less dense as a solid than as a liquid — ice floats.*

**(a)** In most substances, particles pack more tightly when they freeze (solid = denser than liquid). Water particles, however, arrange into a **crystal lattice structure** when they freeze — a hexagonal arrangement that actually has **more space between particles** than liquid water. This makes ice less dense than liquid water. *(At Grade 6 level: students should convey that the particle arrangement in ice is more spread out/open than in liquid water, even without knowing the term "hexagonal lattice.")*

**(b)** If ice were denser, it would sink to the bottom. Lakes would freeze from the bottom up. Over a cold Alberta winter, the entire lake could freeze solid — leaving no liquid water layer. Most aquatic life (fish, invertebrates, plants) would not survive. The ice layer floating on top actually insulates the water below; without it, there would be no protected zone for aquatic life to survive winter.

### Q21 ★★ [Unit: Climate]

**[Misconception target: greenhouse effect = ozone hole]** *One of the most persistent climate misconceptions. Both parts of the answer are important.*

**(a)** The error is treating the greenhouse effect and the ozone hole as the same phenomenon caused by the same thing. They are unrelated processes.

**(b)** The greenhouse effect: certain gases in the atmosphere (CO₂, methane, water vapour) absorb heat radiated from Earth's surface and redirect some of it back toward Earth, warming the planet. It is a natural process — amplified by human-added greenhouse gases.

**(c)** The ozone hole: a thinning of the ozone layer (upper atmosphere) caused primarily by chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) — chemicals used in older refrigerants and aerosols. CFCs break down ozone molecules, reducing the layer that absorbs ultraviolet (UV) radiation. The ozone hole is not caused by the same gases that drive the greenhouse effect.

### Q22 ★★ [Unit: Energy Resources]

**[Misconception target: renewable = clean]**

**Strong answer:** The claim is partially correct but misleading. "Renewable" means a resource can be replenished naturally and will not run out on a human timescale (wind, solar, hydroelectric). "Clean" refers to the environmental impact of using the resource — how much pollution, land disruption, or carbon is produced. These are separate concepts.

**Example:** Large-scale hydroelectric dams are renewable (water keeps flowing) but can flood entire valleys, destroy ecosystems, and displace communities — significant environmental impact. Solar panel manufacturing uses toxic materials and creates waste. Wind turbines require land and can affect bird migration. Renewable does not guarantee clean.

*Accept any well-reasoned example that separates the two concepts.*

### Q23 ★★ [Unit: Living Systems]

**[Misconception target: plants get food from soil — the most persistent misconception in biology]**

**(a)** What is correct: plants do absorb water through their roots, and water is a genuine input to photosynthesis. Fertilizer does support plant growth (by providing minerals for cell structure and function).

**(b)** What is wrong: the statement implies that soil and water are the plant's "food" — that the plant gets energy from the soil. This is incorrect. Soil and water are raw materials, not food. The plant makes its own food (glucose) using light energy — it is not consuming a pre-made food source the way animals do.

**(c)** Corrected explanation: Plants make their own food through photosynthesis. Using the energy from sunlight, they combine carbon dioxide (from the air) and water (from the soil) to produce glucose. The glucose is the food — the plant creates it using light energy. Water is an ingredient, not the food itself. Soil minerals support growth but are not energy sources.

### Q24 ★★ [Unit: Forces]

**[Misconception target: heavier/larger object exerts more force]**

**Strong answer:** The claim is incorrect based on Newton's Third Law. The railing cannot push back differently on two people pushing with the same force — it can only push back equal to the force applied to it. If both students exert 60 N, the railing pushes back with 60 N on each of them, regardless of their mass. Mass determines how much force a person can exert, but the Third Law pair depends on the actual force applied, not the person's size. Student A being heavier doesn't change the force they actually pushed with in this scenario — both pushed with 60 N.

### Q26 ★★ [Unit: Matter]

**[Misconception target: particles change identity during phase changes]**

**(a)** The statement is wrong because particles do not "freeze" or "harden" or change what they are during a phase change. The water particles (molecules) remain water molecules in all three states — solid, liquid, and gas. What changes is how they are arranged and how fast they are moving, not their identity.

**(b)** Corrected: "When the water in the rock cracks freezes, the water particles do not change into a different kind of particle. They are still water molecules. What changes is that they slow down and arrange themselves into a fixed pattern (solid state), held together by stronger attractive forces."

### Q27 ★★ [Unit: Space]

**[Misconception target: solar system is crowded and planets are similar in size]**

**Error 1 — Scale/spacing:** Planets are not evenly or closely spaced. The solar system is almost entirely empty space. The distances between planets increase dramatically as you move outward. A true scale model of the solar system would require most planets to be microscopic dots separated by enormous distances — the scale is wildly counterintuitive.

Why people imagine it this way: textbook diagrams compress distances to fit on a page. Students never see the actual scale; the visual representation is a necessary but misleading simplification.

**Error 2 — Relative size:** The Sun is vastly larger than all the planets — the Sun contains over 99% of the solar system's mass. Planets vary enormously in size relative to each other (Jupiter is 11 times Earth's diameter; Mercury is less than half). Diagrams often show all bodies at similar sizes for readability.

Why people imagine it this way: same as above — diagrams require all objects to be visible, so size is not shown to scale.

### Q28 ★★ [Unit: Forces]

*Scenario: The structural engineer for the timber bridge must think about three different internal forces — a horizontal rope pulled tight by the deck's weight, a vertical post holding up the deck, and a diagonal brace resisting sideways twisting.*

**(a)** Tension — the rope is being pulled/stretched from both ends by the weight it supports. The material is under a pulling force. *(If a student says "the weight pulling down" — redirect: what is happening *inside* the rope?)*

**(b)** Compression — the post is being squeezed from above by the weight pressing down. The material is under a pushing/squeezing force.

**(c)** Torsion — the brace resists twisting, meaning the forces are acting in a rotational/twisting direction. *Accept shear if students argue the diagonal brace resists sliding forces — acknowledge and clarify that torsion is the most precise term for resisting twist.*

### Q29 ★★ [Unit: Living Systems]

*Scenario: A researcher wants to prove that light is required for photosynthesis in spruce seedlings — one of the three curriculum photosynthesis experiments. Students describe the question, setup, and expected result.*

**(a)** Does photosynthesis require light? / Can plants make food without light?

**(b)** Setup: Take two groups of identical spruce seedlings in identical conditions (same soil, water, temperature). Control group: kept in normal light. Experimental group: kept in complete darkness (or covered so no light reaches the leaves). All other variables kept the same. Run for a consistent time period (e.g., 2 weeks).

**(c)** The seedlings in the dark group would show evidence of not performing photosynthesis — they would not produce starch (test with iodine solution: no colour change in dark group, blue-black colour change in light group). They may also show yellowing (chlorophyll breakdown without light).

**Circulation prompt:** Push for the iodine test or oxygen/glucose measurement as the observable outcome — not just "they would die" (they don't die immediately; they draw on stored glucose first).

### Q30 ★★ [Unit: Energy Resources]

*Scenario: The Alberta town from Q6 currently heats all buildings with natural gas. The council wants to switch to electricity made by wind turbines. Students name two advantages and one limitation.*

**(a)** Any two valid advantages:
- Wind is renewable — will not run out, unlike natural gas (a fossil fuel)
- Lower long-term environmental impact / reduced greenhouse gas emissions from heating
- Local energy production — reduces dependence on a market-priced fuel
- Economic: once installed, fuel cost is essentially zero (wind is free)

**(b)** Any valid limitation:
- Wind is intermittent — turbines don't generate when wind is calm; need energy storage or backup
- High upfront capital cost for turbines and grid upgrades
- Alberta wind patterns may not be consistent enough in all locations
- Natural gas infrastructure already exists — transition requires significant new infrastructure

### Q31 ★★ [Unit: Matter]

*Scenario: Concrete sidewalks and railway tracks in Alberta have small gaps built in at regular intervals — expansion joints. Students explain why the gaps are needed and what would happen without them.*

**(a)** Steel and concrete expand when temperatures rise and contract when temperatures fall. In Alberta, seasonal temperature variation is extreme (–30°C winters to +35°C summers). Expansion joints allow the material to expand in summer without pressing against adjacent sections. Without the gap, the expanding sections would push against each other, generating enormous compressive forces.

**(b)** Without expansion joints: in summer, the rail sections expand and press against each other. The compressive forces build to the point where the material buckles, curves sideways, or cracks. Using particle model language: the particles are moving faster and pushing each other further apart — but if they can't expand outward, they push against adjacent sections with increasing force, eventually causing structural failure (buckling = a compression-driven internal force failure).

### Q32 ★★ [Unit: Scientific Methods / Climate]

*Scenario: A climate researcher shows that High River's average temperature has risen 1.8°C over 30 years. A scientist says science is self-correcting. Students explain what that means and give one example from this year's science.*

**Self-correcting science:** Science is self-correcting because scientists use new evidence to update or revise explanations. When better data, better instruments, or better methods reveal that an earlier explanation was incomplete or wrong, science changes its position. This is not a failure — it is how knowledge improves over time.

**Example from the year:** Pluto's reclassification — scientists updated their definition of "planet" when they discovered other Kuiper Belt objects. The evidence required a revision. The scientific community agreed on a better classification system. The change was made not arbitrarily but because the evidence demanded it.

*Accept any other valid example from the year's science (particle model revisions, photosynthesis understanding evolving, climate models improving with better data).*

### Stretch Zone — Answer Notes

---

**S1 ★★ [Matter + Forces]**

**(a)** Particle model: heat causes the steel particles to gain energy, move faster, and push further apart, increasing the overall length of the rail.

**(b)** Compression — the sections are pressing against each other (pushed together), so the internal force at the contact point is compressive.

**(c)** Without expansion gaps, the compressive force builds until the rail buckles sideways (thermal buckling). The material exceeds its compressive limit. This is a compression failure — the track deforms permanently outward to relieve the force.

**Cross-unit connection:** The particle model explains *why* expansion happens; the internal forces concept explains *what structural failure* results.

---

**S2 ★★ [Living Systems + Climate]**

**(a)** Trees absorb CO₂ during photosynthesis and incorporate the carbon into their biomass (wood, leaves). A healthy boreal forest removes large amounts of CO₂ from the atmosphere continuously, acting as a carbon sink.

**(b)** Feedback loop: wildfire releases the stored carbon as CO₂ → more CO₂ in atmosphere → stronger greenhouse effect → warmer temperatures → drier conditions → more fires. The forest that was removing carbon is now adding it, and there are fewer trees to absorb CO₂ in the future.

**(c)** The boreal forest is one of Earth's largest carbon sinks. Its loss affects global CO₂ concentrations, not just Alberta's climate. This connects Alberta's forest health to global climate stability.

---

**S3 ★★ [Energy Resources + Scientific Methods]**

**(a)** Strong answer: Collect wind speed and direction data at multiple points in the valley over at least one full year (to capture seasonal variation). Compare against the town's energy demand profile. A controlled approach would involve standardized measurement tools, consistent recording intervals, and comparison against energy demand data collected independently. Testing "viability" would require cost analysis separate from availability data — keep claims separate.

**(b)** Traditional knowledge could reveal seasonal and long-term wind pattern shifts that a one-year instrument study would miss entirely. If wind patterns have shifted over a generation, a one-year study might conclude the valley is viable when it is actually less reliable than historical patterns. Indigenous observation provides a longer-term trend that improves research accuracy.

---

**S4 ★★ [Forces + Living Systems]**

**(a)** The windward side (facing the wind) experiences tension — being stretched as the wind pushes the crown away. The leeward side experiences compression — being squeezed as the trunk bends. The outer surface on the windward side also experiences some shear as layers of wood slide relative to each other.

**(b)** Elasticity — the living wood returns to its original shape when the deforming force (wind) is removed. This is elastic deformation.

**(c)** Plasticity — the dead branch has exceeded its elastic limit; the deformation is permanent. Dead wood is typically less flexible (more brittle) because it has lost the moisture and cellular structure that gives living wood its elasticity.

**Cross-unit connection:** Both the bridge (Forces) and the tree (Living Systems context) illustrate the same internal force principles — elasticity and plasticity operate in biological materials, not just engineered ones.

---

**S5 ★★ [Matter + Living Systems + CS]**

**(a)** Abstraction — a simplified version of a complex system that keeps only the most relevant features.

**(b)** Simplified models are useful because they make the essential relationships understandable without overwhelming with detail. The particle model correctly predicts the key behaviours (expansion, phase changes, pressure) without requiring knowledge of quantum mechanics. The photosynthesis model correctly identifies inputs, outputs, and energy flow without requiring biochemistry. For both: the simplification allows Grade 6 students (or any learner) to build accurate, usable understanding of the core concept.

**(c)** A simplified model becomes a problem when its omissions lead to incorrect predictions in contexts that require the missing detail. Example: the Grade 6 particle model treats all particles as identical spheres — this works for explaining expansion but would produce incorrect predictions about chemical reactions, where particle type matters enormously.

---

**S6 ★★ [All Tier 1 Units]**

*Sample answer — students may identify different valid connections:*

**Matter:** Concept: thermal expansion and contraction | Observable change: permafrost thaw in northern Alberta (warming causes ice in soil to melt and expand, destabilizing ground) | Connection: as temperatures rise, the water in permafrost transitions from solid to liquid — particle spacing changes — causing ground subsidence and infrastructure damage.

**Living Systems:** Concept: photosynthesis / carbon absorption | Observable change: boreal forest fires and reduced forest cover | Connection: higher temperatures and drought reduce forest health; fires release stored carbon. The boreal forest's role as a CO₂ absorber is weakened, accelerating the greenhouse effect.

**Forces:** Concept: thermal expansion → compressive internal forces | Observable change: road buckling and infrastructure failure in summer heat events | Connection: extreme summer temperatures cause expansion in paved roads and railways; without adequate expansion joints, compressive forces build until the material buckles. Heat waves in Alberta are increasing in frequency and intensity.

*Accept any scientifically sound connection that names a unit concept, an observable Alberta change, and explains the link. Penalize only if the connection is factually incorrect or if the unit concept is not named.*
