# Grade 6 Science — Forces
## Inquiry Project Briefs
*Three options — choose one · Second half of unit*

---

## How the inquiry phase works

The project runs after Section 3 (Internal Forces) is complete. Students begin while Sections 4 and 5 are still being taught, so they are applying knowledge as it accumulates. The project does not require all the unit content upfront — each option is designed to use Sections 1–3 immediately and naturally incorporate Sections 4 and 5 as the unit progresses.

**Timeline:** ~3–4 class periods (lab/investigation time) + presentation or report. Schedule the final submission after Newton's Third Law is taught.

**Assessment is on its own rubric** — separate from the unit test. The test assesses individual knowledge; the project assesses application, investigation skill, and scientific communication.

## Option 1 — Structure Challenge
*Design and build a structure that must survive specified forces — then explain the forces.*

---

### Guiding question
**What makes a structure strong enough to withstand forces — and how do you know?**

### Context
Engineers who design bridges, towers, and buildings must understand the forces their structures will face. Before building anything, they analyze: What forces will act on this? What forces will develop inside the materials? What will happen if those forces exceed the material's limits? This project asks you to think like a structural engineer.

### What you will do

**Phase 1 — Design**
Design a bridge or tower using only these materials: index cards, tape, and paper clips. Your structure must meet at least two of the following force requirements (you choose which two — state them before you build):
- Support a load of at least 200 g placed at the centre (compression and tension)
- Withstand a horizontal push of at least 5 N applied to the top without collapsing (shear and/or torsion)
- Support a 100 g load hung from one end (tension and/or torque)

**Phase 2 — Build and test**
Build your structure. Test each requirement you chose. Measure or estimate the forces. Record whether the structure passed or failed each test.

**Phase 3 — Analyze and explain**
After testing:
- Draw a labeled force diagram showing at least 3 forces acting on your structure during one test
- Identify at least two internal forces developing inside the structure's materials during testing
- If your structure failed: identify which force type caused failure and explain why
- If your structure survived: identify the design decision that most contributed to success, with a force explanation

### What to hand in
1. **Design sketch** — labeled with materials and why you chose each one
2. **Test record** — forces applied, results, measurements or estimates
3. **Force diagram** — labeled, with arrows showing direction and relative magnitude
4. **Analysis paragraph** — ~150 words: what worked, what failed, what internal forces were acting, what you would change

### How it is assessed

| Criterion | Full (3) | Partial (2) | Beginning (1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Force diagram** | All labeled arrows, correct directions, magnitudes reflected | Arrows present but one or more unlabeled or incorrect | Arrows missing or directions wrong |
| **Internal force identification** | Correct type named for at least 2 locations, with description of what particles are doing | One correct identification, or correct type without particle description | Incorrect or absent |
| **Scientific explanation** | Explains what worked/failed using force vocabulary; connects to internal forces | Uses some force vocabulary; explanation partially connected | Describes result without force explanation |
| **Design reasoning** | Explains specific design choices in terms of forces before or after testing | Mentions design choices without force connection | Describes what was built without design reasoning |

## Option 2 — Action-Reaction Investigation
*Design experiments that demonstrate Newton's Third Law across multiple situations.*

---

### Guiding question
**How can you show that Newton's Third Law holds even when the objects are very different in size or mass?**

### Context
Newton's Third Law seems simple — every action has an equal and opposite reaction. But it also seems impossible. How can a tiny bicycle push on a truck with the same force the truck pushes on it? This investigation asks you to collect evidence that the law holds, then explain why equal forces produce unequal effects.

### What you will do

**Phase 1 — Design three tests**
Design three different situations that demonstrate Newton's Third Law. At least one must involve objects with noticeably different sizes or masses. For each test, write:
- What you predict will happen (state the action and reaction forces)
- How you will observe or measure the reaction force
- What result would confirm the law

Possible setups to consider: balloons, elastic bands, spring scales (two students pull one spring scale from opposite ends), a ball rolling off a surface, toy cars pushing off each other, pushing a chair while standing on wheels.

**Phase 2 — Run the tests**
Run each test. Record: what you observed, any measurements, whether your prediction was confirmed.

**Phase 3 — Analyze**
For each test:
- Identify the action-reaction pair (name each force, its direction, what object it acts on)
- Confirm: were the forces equal in magnitude? Opposite in direction? Acting on different objects?
- If objects moved differently: explain why using mass, not force

**Phase 4 — Answer the challenge question**
*"If the truck and bicycle push on each other with equal forces, why does the bicycle get destroyed and the truck barely move?"* Write a scientific explanation using your investigation results as evidence.

### What to hand in
1. **Three test plans** — prediction, setup description, what would confirm the law
2. **Test records** — observations and measurements for each test
3. **Action-reaction analysis** — for each test: name both forces, directions, objects
4. **Challenge question response** — ~100–150 words, using evidence from your tests

### How it is assessed

| Criterion | Full (3) | Partial (2) | Beginning (1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Test design** | Three tests planned with clear predictions; at least one involves different masses | Two tests planned, or predictions vague | One test planned, or no predictions |
| **Action-reaction identification** | Both forces named, direction stated, object named &mdash; for all three tests | Complete for one or two tests | Incomplete or incorrect for all tests |
| **Law confirmation** | Clearly states whether each test confirmed the law and why | Some tests confirmed with partial reasoning | Result stated without connection to the law |
| **Challenge explanation** | Correctly distinguishes force (equal) from effect (different masses → different accelerations) | Partially correct; may conflate force and effect | Incorrect or absent |

## Option 3 — Materials Engineering Investigation
*Test materials for elasticity and plasticity, then make engineering recommendations.*

---

### Guiding question
**How do you choose the right material for an engineering application that involves forces?**

### Context
Engineers choose materials based on how they behave under force. A material that is perfect for a spring would be dangerous in a helmet. A material that is perfect for a helmet would fail as a bridge cable. This investigation asks you to test materials systematically, find their elastic limits, and use the evidence to recommend materials for real applications.

### What you will do

**Phase 1 — Choose and test at least 3 materials**
Choose at least 3 materials that can be stretched, squeezed, or twisted (examples: rubber band, tape, wire, foam, clay, paper, fabric strip, spring from a pen). For each material, apply increasing force or deformation and record whether the material returns to its original shape. Find the approximate elastic limit for each material.

Your data must include:
- Material name
- Amount of force or deformation applied (in consistent units — cm, grams of load, etc.)
- Whether it returned to its original shape (yes/no)
- Your conclusion about the deformation type (elastic or plastic)

**Phase 2 — Determine elastic limits**
From your data, identify the approximate elastic limit of each material (the point at which deformation switches from elastic to plastic). Present this data in a table.

**Phase 3 — Make engineering recommendations**
Using your data, recommend the best material from your tests for each of the following applications:
1. A spring that must return to its original length after being compressed
2. A padding material inside a protective helmet
3. A cable that must support a hanging weight without permanently stretching

For each recommendation:
- Name the material
- State its elastic or plastic behaviour at the relevant force
- Explain why that property fits the requirement

**Phase 4 — Identify limits of your evidence**
Identify one weakness in your testing method. What would you need to do differently to be more confident in your recommendations?

### What to hand in
1. **Data table** — material, amount stretched/compressed, returns? (yes/no), deformation type
2. **Elastic limit conclusions** — where the elastic limit falls for each material, with evidence from data
3. **Three engineering recommendations** — material, behaviour, justification
4. **Limitations reflection** — one weakness and how you would address it

### How it is assessed

| Criterion | Full (3) | Partial (2) | Beginning (1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Data collection** | At least 3 materials tested at multiple force levels; data recorded clearly | Two materials tested, or inconsistent measurements | One material or data difficult to interpret |
| **Elastic limit conclusions** | Elastic limit range identified for each material with evidence cited from data | Elastic limit identified for some materials; evidence not cited | Elastic limit not identified, or incorrect |
| **Engineering recommendations** | Correct material recommended for all 3 applications with force-property justification | Correct for 1–2 applications with partial justification | Incorrect or no justification |
| **Limitations reflection** | Names a specific weakness (not just &ldquo;I should test more materials&rdquo;) and proposes a concrete solution | General weakness identified; solution vague | No weakness identified |

## Project rubric — all options

Each option uses the same 4-criterion rubric (3 marks each = 12 marks total). There is no bonus for choosing a harder option — all three are designed to be equally challenging, just in different directions.

**What a 3 looks like:** Evidence is complete, vocabulary is accurate, explanations connect observations to forces explicitly.

**What a 2 looks like:** Evidence is partial or explanations are present but incomplete — the connection to forces is implied rather than stated.

**What a 1 looks like:** The student completed an activity but has not demonstrated understanding of the forces involved.

**Returning projects:** Return with written comments on each criterion. Students write a one-paragraph response addressing two questions: (1) What did you misunderstand or leave incomplete? (2) What would you change if you could redo it? Allow 10 minutes in class for this response — do not assign it as homework.
