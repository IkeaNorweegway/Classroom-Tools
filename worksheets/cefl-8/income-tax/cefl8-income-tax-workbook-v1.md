## Moment 1 — Tax brackets

::: scenario
Canada taxes income in **brackets**: slices of income taxed at different rates. In 2026, the federal government taxes the first **$58,523** of income at **14%**, and the next slice (up to $117,045) at **20.5%**. Then everyone gets a credit for the tax-free basic personal amount.
:::

::: example
**How much federal tax does someone earning $70,000 pay?**

1. **First bracket:** the first $58,523 is taxed at 14%: 0.14 × 58,523 = **$8,193.22**.
2. **Second bracket:** the rest is 70,000 − 58,523 = $11,477, taxed at 20.5%: 0.205 × 11,477 = **$2,352.79**.
3. **Bracket tax:** $8,193.22 + $2,352.79 = $10,546.01.
4. **Subtract the credit:** everyone gets 0.14 × $16,452 = $2,303.28 off. **Federal tax = $8,242.73.**
:::

## Moment 2 — Three ways to tax

::: scenario
Governments can share the cost of public services in different ways. A **progressive** tax takes a bigger share from people who earn more. A **flat** tax takes the same share from everyone. A **regressive** tax ends up taking a bigger share from people who earn less.
:::

::: example
**Why is GST (a 5% sales tax) regressive, even though everyone pays the same 5%?**

1. Sam earns **$20,000** and spends **$15,000** on taxable things. GST: 0.05 × 15,000 = **$750**.
2. Taylor earns **$200,000** and spends **$60,000** on taxable things. GST: 0.05 × 60,000 = **$3,000**.
3. **Share of income:** Sam pays 750 ÷ 20,000 = **3.75%**. Taylor pays 3,000 ÷ 200,000 = **1.5%**.
4. People who earn less spend more of their income, so a sales tax takes a **bigger share** of their income. That makes it regressive.
:::

## Moment 3 — Total compensation

::: scenario
Priya gets two job offers. **Offer A** pays a bigger salary. **Offer B** pays less salary, but comes with extras. Which is worth more? To decide, she compares **total compensation**: salary plus everything else the employer gives.
:::

::: example
**Which offer has more total compensation?**

1. **Offer A:** salary only = **$52,000**.
2. **Offer B:** $48,000 + $3,000 (health and dental) + $2,000 (bonus) + $1,440 (the employer adds 3% of salary to an RRSP) = **$54,440**.
3. **Difference:** $54,440 − $52,000 = **$2,440** more in Offer B.
4. **Non-money factors:** Offer B also has an extra week of vacation. Priya should still check hours, commute and promotion chances before deciding.
:::

## Moment 4 — Taxes and the social contract

::: scenario
In Canada, people pay taxes, and governments use the money to provide services everyone shares. This idea is called the **social contract**. In 2026–27, Alberta planned to spend about **$84 billion**: about **$34.4 billion** on health and **$10.8 billion** on education.
:::

::: example
**How does one person's tax help everyone?**

1. **One person:** someone earning $70,000 pays about $12,197 in income tax (federal + Alberta).
2. **Pooled:** millions of people's taxes together pay for things no one could pay for alone, like a hospital.
3. **Shared benefit:** anyone can use the emergency room, a public school, or a highway, whatever they paid.
4. **So:** taxes link individual contributions to collective benefits. That's the social contract.
:::
