Why our process works

A one-glance picture of how every piece is made, checked and used. Read each section top to bottom.

STARTThe big picture

Every result from the classroom feeds the next round of research.

1. ResearchWe read studies before we design anything.
↓
2. Ten ideas we always useWritten down, so every piece follows them.
↓
3. Plan the unit in phasesStart with what students already know.
↓
4. Make the classroom setSlides, notes, practice, quizzes and tests.
↓
5. Check each piece before useFive plain questions. A piece must pass them.
↓
6. Teach, check and adjustExit tickets show what students still need.
↓
7. Learn from the resultsChanges go back into the plan and the ideas.
↺ new evidence returns to step 1

BUILDHow every new piece starts

Every request follows the same path. A piece that fails a check goes back for a fix.

1. Someone asks for a pieceFor example, a quiz, a worksheet or a unit plan.
↓
2. The matching guide is read firstEvery time. Nobody works from memory alone.
↓
3. Only missing details are askedNo more than four quick questions.
↓
4. A proven template is chosen and filledNobody starts from a blank page.
↓
5. The goal is stated in one sentenceIt names what the piece must do well for students.
↓
6. Does the piece pass the check?
NO
↓
Fix itReturn to step 4, then check again.
YES
↓
7. Saved with a version numberOld versions are kept. Nothing is written over.
Rule that never bendsA team may ask for faster work. The template and the check still stay in place.

IDEASWhere our ideas come from

Our ideas come from studies, not from habit. A finding joins our set only after it is read and filed.

1. Ask a useful questionFor example: does a short quiz help students remember?
↓
2. Find strong evidenceStudies by trusted researchers. Not opinions or trends.
↓
3. Write a plain summaryWhat was found, how strong it is, and where it stops working.
↓
4. File it with the othersEvery summary lives in one shared research folder.
↓
5. Does it change how we teach?
NO
↓
Keep it on fileIt is not added to the ten ideas.
YES
↓
6. Added to the ten ideasEvery new material uses it.

UNITHow a unit is planned

Teaching always comes before projects. Students need knowledge before they can use it.

1. Score the unit's importance out of 10Big units get more weeks, a quiz and a full test.
↓
2. Start: find out what students thinkStudents write what they already know. No grades.
↓
3. Teach: build the knowledgeSlides, guided notes and workbook practice.
↓
4. Use: apply it in a projectStudents use the ideas in a real project brief.
↓
5. Check: see what was learnedExit tickets all along. A quiz mid-way. A test at the end.

CLASSHow one class is run

Every class follows the same four parts. The teaching part stops at 20 minutes.

1. Quick warm-up, 5 to 8 minutesThree to five short questions. No notes. Nothing is graded.
↓
2. Teach, 15 to 20 minutesTwo or three worked examples. Stop at 20 minutes.
↓
3. Practice, 20 to 25 minutesWorkbook problems. Old and new ideas are mixed together.
↓
4. Exit ticket, 5 to 8 minutesTwo or three quick questions, handed in. Not discussed as a class.
↓
5. Teacher reads the ticketsThe next class starts where students actually are.

CHECKThe five-question check

Before a piece reaches a student, it answers five plain questions.

1. Must the student remember, not just recognize?Recall builds memory. Spotting the right answer does not.
↓
2. Must the student explain their thinking?At least one question asks for a reason.
↓
3. Does it bring back older material?Old ideas must return, so they stick.
↓
4. Can the teacher act on the results?Results must show what to fix next.
↓
5. Is new material shown before practice?Show the example first, then the practice.
↓
Pass or fix3 of 5 to pass. Big units need all 5. A piece that fails is fixed, then asked again.

MEETINGSWhat a student meets over a unit

One good lesson is rarely enough. Each big idea is met at least three times.

1. First meeting: taught clearlyLessons 1 to 3. Worked examples show each idea.
↓
2. Second meeting: brought back, mixed inWarm-up questions and workbook practice bring it back.
↓
3. Third meeting: used in a new settingA project or test asks for the idea in a new context.
↓
4. The idea is still there laterStudents recall it in a later unit or quiz.
↓
5. Lasting memorySpaced meetings are what make an idea stick.
